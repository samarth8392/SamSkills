# Dissociative Identity Disorder

> **Spark note:** DID = identity fragmentation (≥2 distinct personality states *or* a possession experience) with marked discontinuity in sense of self and agency, **plus** recurrent amnesia for everyday events, important personal information, and/or trauma. Everyday time loss is common but not separately mandatory. The big misconception is that DID looks dramatic: most non-possession cases switch subtly, hide or minimize symptoms, and present instead with depression, anxiety, self-injury, substance use or "refractory" neurological complaints. Named alters with different wardrobes/handwriting occur in only a minority and are **not** required (p. 493).

## Criteria at a glance
- **A. Identity disruption**: ≥2 distinct personality states (may be described culturally as possession), with *marked* discontinuity in sense of self and agency, plus related changes in affect, behavior, consciousness, memory, perception, cognition and/or sensory-motor functioning. Can be **observed by others or self-reported** (pp. 492–493).
- **B. Recurrent amnesia**: gaps in recall of everyday events, important personal information and/or trauma, inconsistent with ordinary forgetting (p. 493).
- **C.** Clinically significant distress or impairment (p. 493).
- **D.** Not a normal part of a broadly accepted cultural or religious practice. *Children:* not better explained by imaginary playmates or other fantasy play (p. 493).
- **E.** Not attributable to a substance (e.g., alcohol blackouts, chaotic intoxicated behavior) or another medical condition (e.g., complex partial seizures) (p. 493).

## Specifiers & severity
- No subtypes, course or severity specifiers. Text distinguishes descriptively **possession-form** (overt; identities experienced as external spirits, demons, deities, animals, the dead) vs **non-possession-form** (usually covert/subtle) (p. 493).

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- **Overtness varies** with motivation, stress, culture, internal conflict and resilience; prolonged identity confusion can occur under severe/prolonged psychosocial pressure (p. 493).
- When alternate states can't be observed directly, infer them from **sudden discontinuities in self/agency (A) + recurrent amnesia (B)** (p. 493).
- **Criterion A phenomena** (pp. 493–494):
  - Feeling like a depersonalized observer of one's own speech/actions, unable to stop them.
  - Voices (child's voice, commentary, persecutory, command). Some deny voices but describe multiple, uncontrollable, independent thought streams.
  - Hallucinations in **any modality** (auditory, visual, tactile, olfactory, gustatory).
  - Emotions, impulses, thoughts, speech or actions that appear without ownership/control; or thoughts/emotions that suddenly vanish and speech/action abruptly inhibited. Typically ego-dystonic and puzzling.
  - Sudden shifts in attitudes and preferences (food, activities, gender identity); body feels different (child-sized, other gender, several ages at once); "not mine / not under my control."
  - Switching usually subtle; experienced as **multiple, simultaneously overlapping and interfering states**; more overt in possession form.
- **Criterion B — three amnesia domains** (p. 494):
  1. Gaps in remote autobiographical memory (e.g., marriage, childbirth, whole school years).
  2. Lapses for recent events or well-learned skills (job, computer, cooking, driving).
  3. Finding possessions, writings or drawings one can't remember acquiring or making.
  - Also: **dissociative fugues** with amnesia for travel are common ("coming to" in another city, a closet, under the bed); "time loss," "blackouts"; failing to recognize family; forgetting one's own name. Amnesia is **not limited to trauma** — it covers everyday events. Minimization/rationalization is common (p. 494).
- **Possession form**: behavior as if a spirit, supernatural being or outside person has taken control; recurrent, unwanted, involuntary, distressing. Most possession states worldwide are culturally sanctioned and **do not** meet Criterion D (p. 494).
- Stress transiently worsens dissociative symptoms, making them more visible (p. 492).

## Associated features
- Typical presenting complaints: depression, anxiety, substance use, self-injury (p. 495).
- **Functional neurological symptoms** (nonepileptic seizures, etc.) prominent in some, especially non-Western settings; in Western settings may present with apparently refractory headaches, seizures, MS-like symptoms (p. 495).
- **Dissociative flashbacks**: sensory reliving as if present, often with identity change, partial/complete loss of contact with current reality, then amnesia for the flashback (p. 495).
- History of multiple types of interpersonal maltreatment in childhood and adulthood; also long, painful early medical procedures (p. 495).
- NSSI frequent; high hypnotizability and dissociation scores; some have transient psychotic episodes (p. 495).
- **Personality features**: avoidant traits rate highest (some prefer isolation); when decompensated, borderline-like features (self-destructive, mood instability); attachment problems but usually *no* frantic abandonment avoidance; often stable but dysfunctional/abusive long-term relationships; obsessional > histrionic; subgroup narcissistic/antisocial (p. 495).

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- **Prevalence**: 12-month 1.5% (small U.S. community study); lifetime 1.1% in community women in mid-eastern Turkey (p. 495).
- **Onset**: any age, early childhood to late life (p. 495).
- **Children**: imaginary companions acting independently or personified "mood" states rather than identity shifting; problems with memory, concentration, attachment; traumatic play (pp. 495–496).
- **Adolescents**: come to attention for externalizing behavior, suicidality/self-destruction, rapid behavior shifts misattributed to ADHD or pediatric bipolar; some aggressive/irritable (p. 496).
- **Older adults**: may mimic late-life mood disorder, OCD, paranoia, psychotic mood disorder, or cognitive disorder (from amnesia) (p. 496).
- **Triggers of overt alteration**: later trauma (e.g., sexual assault) or even minor stressors (minor MVA); one's child reaching the age at which one was abused; death or fatal illness of abuser (p. 496).
- High risk for **revictimization**: rape, IPV, sexual exploitation, ongoing incest into adulthood, trafficking (p. 496).
- **Environmental**: early trauma (usually before age 5–6) in the context of family/attachment pathology; ~**90%** report multiple types of early neglect and abuse; also extrafamilial abuse/severe bullying, repeated painful medical procedures, war, terrorism, childhood trafficking; some cases from prolonged dysfunctional family dynamics without overt abuse (p. 496).
- **Genetic/physiological**: twin studies — genetics ~**45–50%** of variance in dissociative symptoms; brain regions implicated: orbitofrontal cortex, hippocampus, parahippocampal gyrus, amygdala (p. 496).
- **Poorer outcome**: ongoing abuse/exploitation, revictimization, IPV, refractory substance use, eating disorders, severe medical illness, enmeshment with abusive family, criminal involvement; perpetration of child maltreatment/IPV (pp. 496–497).

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- Where possession is common (rural LMIC settings, some religious groups in U.S./Europe), identities may be spirits, deities, demons, animals, mythical figures. Acculturation shapes alters (e.g., English-speaking, Western-dressed identities in India) (p. 497).
- **Pathological vs cultural possession**: pathological is involuntary, distressing, uncontrollable; conflicts with family/social/work milieu; occurs at times/places that violate cultural or religious norms (p. 497).
- Mixed dissociative-psychotic episodes may be more frequent amid communal violence/oppression (p. 497).
- **Sex/gender**: women predominate in adult clinical samples but not in child/adolescent clinics or general population; few symptom differences, women may have more somatization (p. 497).

## Suicide risk
- **>70% of outpatients have attempted suicide**; multiple attempts common; other self-injury and high-risk behavior very prevalent (p. 497).
- Interacting risks: cumulative severe trauma; comorbid PTSD, depressive and substance use disorders; personality disorder features. **Dissociation is an independent risk factor** for multiple attempts; higher dissociation scores → more attempts and NSSI (p. 497).

## Functional consequences
- Youth: some function poorly at school/relationships; others do well, school as respite (p. 497).
- Adults: range from minimal (high-functioning professionals; relational/marital/parenting more affected than work) to chronic SMI-level impairment; many improve over time (pp. 497–498).

## Differential diagnosis
- **Dissociative amnesia**: amnesia without ≥2 distinct personality states (p. 498).
- **DPDR**: no identity states, typically no amnesia (p. 498).
- **MDD / persistent depressive disorder**: DID often shows lifelong posttraumatic negative mood and anniversary reactions resembling MDD with seasonal pattern; mood disorders lack self/agency fluctuations and amnesia. **Check whether all or only some identity states carry the mood** (p. 498).
- **Bipolar disorders** (often misdiagnosed as bipolar II with mixed features): DID shifts in minutes–hours, linked to specific identity states; elevated/depressed mood may be "loculated" in particular identities; sleep disruption from nightmares/nocturnal flashbacks, not reduced need for sleep (p. 498).
- **PTSD / ASD / dissociative subtype of PTSD**: most DID patients meet PTSD criteria comorbidly. PTSD amnesia is for specific trauma events/aspects vs DID's chronic, complex amnesia; PTSD DP/DR is trauma-reminder-bound vs DID's ongoing DP/DR in daily life and state interference (pp. 498–499).
- **Schizophrenia/psychotic disorders**: DID intrusions can mimic first-rank symptoms (voices arguing/commenting, thought insertion/withdrawal), but in DID they are ego-alien, frightening, not delusionally explained; hallucinations multimodal and tied to flashbacks vs predominantly auditory in schizophrenia; amnesia present in DID; hypnotizability highest in DID, low in schizophrenia (p. 499).
- **Substance/medication-induced**: substance blackouts distinguished from dissociative amnesia when substance judged etiologic (p. 499).
- **Personality disorders (esp. borderline)**: DID shows longitudinal *variability* in personality style due to inconsistent identities vs pervasive, persistent affect/relationship dysfunction in PD (p. 499).
- **TBI / NCD due to TBI**: LOC, disorientation, neuro signs, NCD-pattern deficits (attention, executive, processing speed, social cognition) starting right after injury; TBI lacks marked discontinuity in self/agency (p. 500).
- **Functional neurological symptom disorder**: no ≥2 personality states/possession; amnesia circumscribed (e.g., for a nonepileptic seizure) (p. 500).
- **Factitious disorder / malingering**: feigners overreport media-type features (dramatic amnesia, melodramatic switching), underreport depression; seem untroubled or enjoy the label; ask clinicians to "find" memories; stereotyped, limited alters ("all-good"/"all-bad" for exculpation); amnesia only for gain-related events; switching only when observed. The source describes shame, underreporting, and avoidance as common in genuine cases (p. 500). These presentation tendencies are not stand-alone validity tests; no demeanor or dramatic symptom alone establishes feigning.

## Comorbidity
- PTSD, depressive disorders, substance-related, feeding/eating disorders, OCD, antisocial PD, other specified PD with avoidant/obsessive-compulsive/borderline traits (p. 500).
- Functional neurological symptom disorder — commonly nonepileptic seizures (resembling grand mal or temporal-lobe complex partial; sometimes absence/partial), gait disturbance, paralysis (p. 500).

## Documentation cues (for client notes)

Use examples only when supported by the actual assessment. Attribute client/collateral reports, distinguish observations from interpretations, and leave unassessed findings unknown.
- **Criterion A evidence**: record specific reported or witnessed discontinuities — e.g., client describes "watching myself talk without being able to stop"; abrupt observed changes in voice, posture, vocabulary, affect or apparent age within session; reports of thoughts/feelings/impulses experienced as not their own; sudden preference shifts; referring to self in third person or by another name; possession experiences and whether they occur outside sanctioned ritual.
- **Criterion B evidence**: document concrete amnesia examples in each domain — gaps in life history, lost skills, unexplained possessions/writings, "coming to" in unfamiliar places, being told of actions they don't recall, session content not remembered later. Note minimization/rationalization ("I just have a bad memory").
- **Collateral**: family/partner reports of different "sides," names, or behaviors the client doesn't recall; for children, school/caregiver reports, imaginary companions that act independently.
- **Rule-outs to document**: substance intoxication/blackouts (timeline vs use); seizure disorder (EEG/neurology input when indicated); TBI history; cultural/religious context of possession phenomena (Criterion D); fantasy play in children; psychotic disorder (reality testing, delusional explanation of voices); feigning indicators if forensic/secondary-gain context.
- **MSE language**: "Abrupt shift in demeanor and speech mid-session with subsequent apparent unawareness of preceding exchange"; "Reports voices experienced as internal, ego-dystonic, without delusional attribution; reality testing intact"; "Affect labile across states, congruent within each"; "Endorses time loss and finding unfamiliar items at home."
- **Risk**: always document suicide/NSSI history and current risk given the high rate reported in outpatient samples (not a population-wide individual prediction); screen for current victimization/IPV/trafficking and for safety of dependents.
- Note which identity states endorse mood symptoms when assessing comorbid depression or bipolar.
- Optionally reference standardized dissociation measures used (text notes elevated scores).
