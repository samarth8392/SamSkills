# Dissociative Amnesia

> **Spark note:** A potentially reversible *retrieval* failure for important autobiographical material that should be stored and freely recallable, usually traumatic or stressful in content, and out of keeping with ordinary forgetting. Most cases are localized/selective and the person is unaware of or minimizes the gap, so it seldom shows up as a complaint. The dramatic generalized form (lost identity, sometimes with fugue) is rare. Remember that recurrent amnesia plus identity disruption means **DID**, which overrides this diagnosis.

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** Can't recall important autobiographical information, usually traumatic/stressful, in a way inconsistent with ordinary forgetting. *Note:* most often localized or selective for specific event(s), or generalized for identity and life history (p. 501).
- **B.** Clinically significant distress or impairment (p. 501).
- **C.** Not due to a substance (alcohol/drug/medication) or a neurological/medical condition — e.g., partial complex seizures, **transient global amnesia**, closed head injury/TBI sequelae, other neurological condition (p. 501).
- **D.** Not better explained by **DID, PTSD, acute stress disorder, somatic symptom disorder, or major/mild neurocognitive disorder** (p. 501).

## Specifiers & severity
- **With dissociative fugue**: apparently purposeful travel *or* bewildered wandering associated with amnesia for identity or other important autobiographical information (pp. 501–502).
- No severity or course specifiers.
- Descriptive types (not coded): **localized** (circumscribed period), **selective** (some but not all events in a period), **systematized** (a category of information — e.g., a violent sibling, a specific room), **generalized** (most/all life history, often identity; acute onset; rare), **continuous/anterograde** (forgets each new event) (p. 502).

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Amnesia is for info that 1) should have been stored and 2) would ordinarily be freely recalled; conceptualized as a reversible retrieval deficit, unlike amnesias from neural damage/toxicity (p. 501).
- Generally **retrograde**; ongoing amnesia for current life is rare. Lost material includes both trauma and ordinary nontraumatic stretches of life (p. 502).
- People rarely complain about localized/selective/systematized forms; they minimize, rationalize and get uncomfortable when asked to address it (p. 502).
- May recall some traumatic events (or parts) but not others of the same type (p. 502); may deny a documented trauma (e.g., in medical/social service records) while recalling similar events before and after it (p. 503).
- **Generalized**: loss of personal identity, world knowledge (recent events, technology), less often well-learned skills; acute onset; perplexity, disorientation and purposeless wandering bring them to police/psychiatric ER; commonly linked with fugue; more common in combat veterans, sexual assault victims and people under extreme emotional stress/conflict (p. 502).

## Associated features
- Chronic difficulty forming/maintaining relationships; histories of trauma (esp. child abuse) and victimization (p. 502).
- Dissociative flashbacks (behavioral re-experiencing); NSSI, suicide attempts, high-risk behavior (pp. 502–503).
- Depressive and functional neurological symptoms, depersonalization, autohypnotic symptoms, high hypnotizability; sexual dysfunction common; **mild TBI may precede** it (p. 503).

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- **12-month prevalence 1.8%** (small U.S. community study) (p. 503).
- Observed across the lifespan; children <12 hardest to evaluate; multiple informants (teacher, therapist, caseworker) may be needed. Traumatized adolescents with amnesia may be overlooked (fewer PTSD intrusions, less externalizing) (p. 503).
- Fugue in children/teens is limited by their range (e.g., biking to an unfamiliar neighborhood, taking a bus to a nearby town) (p. 503).
- Generalized amnesia: usually sudden onset; may recur; one episode predisposes to more; may or may not seem symptomatic between episodes; some resolve rapidly once removed from the stressor (e.g., combat) or brought to care (p. 503).
- A substantial subgroup develops **chronic, disabling autobiographical memory loss** that relearning life history doesn't fix (p. 503). **Fugue-related memory loss may be especially refractory** (p. 503).
- Later-life trauma/loss may break down long-standing amnesia for childhood/adult trauma → acute PTSD, mood disorders, substance misuse, danger to self/others (p. 503).
- **Environmental**: severe acute or chronic trauma is the main risk factor; cumulative early adversity (esp. physical/sexual abuse) for childhood amnesia; more severe, repeated, intrafamilial abuse with betrayal by attachment figure → more extensive gaps; severe cumulative adult trauma (combat, trafficking, POW/camp); generalized form linked to extreme acute trauma with no escape, social dislocation, asylum-seeking/refugee status, or inescapable psychological conflict (nearly all such cases have prior severe trauma) (pp. 503–504).
- **Genetic/physiological**: genetics ~**50%** of variance in dissociative symptoms; candidate-gene work suggests G×E with earlier/chronic childhood trauma (p. 504).

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- Where possession is normative, amnesia/fugue may be framed as pathological possession (p. 504).
- In highly constraining social/cultural circumstances, precipitants are often not frank trauma but severe stress/conflict (marital or family conflict, attachment problems, restriction/oppression) (p. 504).
- Sex/gender: not specifically addressed.

## Suicide risk
- Suicidal and other self-destructive behavior common (p. 504).
- **Risk rises as amnesia lifts**; case reports suggest special danger when amnesia remits suddenly and floods the person with intolerable memories (p. 504).

## Functional consequences
- Childhood-trauma–related amnesia: impairment limited to severe; attachment difficulties; some succeed occupationally via compulsive overwork (p. 504).
- Acute generalized amnesia: impairment across all domains; chronic subgroup with poor function in most life areas (pp. 504–505).

## Differential diagnosis
- **DID**: pervasive self/agency discontinuities plus many dissociative symptoms; DID amnesia also includes ongoing "time loss" for everyday events, unexplained possessions, big fluctuations in skills/knowledge, brief gaps during interactions (p. 505).
- **PTSD**: amnesia for part/all of a specific trauma (e.g., the day of a rape). If amnesia extends beyond the immediate trauma, a **comorbid** dissociative amnesia dx may be warranted; dissociative subtype PTSD can include amnesia (p. 505).
- **Neurocognitive disorders**: evidence of neural damage; broad cognitive decline (attention, executive, learning/memory, language, perceptual-motor, social cognition); identity awareness spared until late; retrograde almost always with anterograde amnesia. Anterograde dissociative amnesia can mimic **delirium**, but medical/lab/tox/neuro/imaging workups are normal and repeated evaluations show no true neurocognitive deficit (p. 505).
- **Substance-related**: blackouts/"grayouts"; history shows amnestic episodes **only during intoxication**; tricky when the person also misuses substances under stress or substance use began in childhood amid abuse; observe after detox; some attribute all memory problems to substances to minimize dissociation; substance-induced NCD shows protracted use and persistent intellectual deficits (pp. 505–506).
- **TBI**: head impact/acceleration, LOC, disorientation, neuro signs (imaging abnormalities, new/worse seizures, visual field cuts, anosmia); NCD present immediately post-injury/after regaining consciousness and persisting; mild TBI can precede dissociative amnesia, but deficits are out of proportion and follow dissociative patterns (p. 506).
- **Seizure disorders**: complex behavior ictally/postictally with amnesia; wandering is nonpurposive and limited to seizure; fugue behavior is purposeful, goal-directed and may last days–weeks+. Seizure-related memory loss is random, not trauma-linked; serial/telemetric EEG abnormalities. **Both can coexist** (p. 506).
- **ECT**: memory loss mostly for the day of treatment; wider deficits unrelated to stressful epochs and remit after the series; ECT doesn't worsen dissociation in depressed dissociative patients (p. 506).
- **Catatonic stupor**: mutism without true recall failure; rigidity, posturing, negativism; memory loss only for the catatonic episode (p. 506).
- **Acute dissociative reactions to stressful events (OSDD)**: mixed acute dissociative sx, typically <1 month; brief (hours–days) "micro-amnesias" with other prominent dissociative symptoms (p. 507).
- **Factitious disorder / malingering**: no test reliably separates genuine from feigned amnesia. Feigning more common with acute florid amnesia; financial, sexual or legal problems; wish to escape stress; wish to seem interesting; planned "recovered memory" litigation — but genuine amnesia can occur in the same contexts and coexist with feigning; many malingerers confess spontaneously or when confronted (p. 507).
- **Normal aging / mild NCD**: problems learning and retaining new info (word-list or story recall), plus slowed processing/executive changes in aging — unlike autobiographical retrieval gaps (p. 507).

## Comorbidity
- Comorbidities emerge especially **as amnesia remits**: dysphoria, grief, rage, shame, guilt, turmoil; NSSI/high-risk behavior (p. 507).
- Persistent depressive disorder, MDD, other specified depressive disorder; **PTSD** often develops when traumatic antecedents surface (often dissociative subtype) (p. 507).
- Somatic symptom and related disorders, esp. **functional neurological symptom disorder** (bidirectional) (p. 507).
- Substance-related/addictive, feeding/eating disorders, sexual dysfunctions; most common PD = other specified PD with mixed avoidant, obsessive-compulsive, dependent, borderline features (p. 507).

## Documentation cues (for client notes)

Use examples only when supported by the actual assessment. Attribute client/collateral reports, distinguish observations from interpretations, and leave unassessed findings unknown.
- **Criterion A**: specify the gap — what period/category is missing, type (localized/selective/systematized/generalized/continuous), how the gap was discovered (told by others, documents, photos), and whether content is trauma-related. Note awareness level and minimization ("doesn't seem bothered by not remembering third grade").
- **Fugue specifier**: document travel/wandering (purposeful vs bewildered), duration, how/where found, amnesia for identity or key history during it → F44.1.
- **Criterion B**: concrete impairment (unable to work, relationship strain, ER/police contact).
- **Criterion C rule-outs to document**: substance use timeline relative to episodes (and tox screen if acute); head injury history, LOC, neuro exam/imaging; seizure history/EEG; transient global amnesia features; medications; ECT history.
- **Criterion D rule-outs**: screen for identity states/time loss (DID); PTSD symptoms and whether amnesia is confined to the trauma; NCD screening showing intact new learning and other cognition.
- **MSE language**: "Alert and oriented to place/time but unable to provide name, address or personal history" (generalized); "Intact attention and new learning on bedside testing; deficit confined to autobiographical recall for [period]"; "Perplexed, not distressed by the gap"; "No evidence of delirium; workup reportedly unremarkable."
- **Risk**: document suicide/self-harm screening at baseline **and repeatedly as memories return**; note any sudden memory recovery.
- If feigning is a concern, document context (legal/financial gain), consistency across observations, and avoid conclusions based on any single test.
