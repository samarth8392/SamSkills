# Enuresis

> **Spark note:** Repeated wetting of bed or clothes at age **≥5** (developmental age), either **≥2×/week for 3 consecutive months** or causing significant distress or impairment, and not explained by a substance or medical condition. Usually involuntary. **Nocturnal-only** is the most common subtype. Common errors: skipping the medical rule-out (UTI, diabetes, structural or neurogenic causes, sleep apnea), and diagnosing enuresis alone when an antipsychotic or diuretic explains it.

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** Repeated voiding of urine into bed or clothes, **involuntary or intentional**.
- **B.** Clinically significant: **≥2×/week for ≥3 consecutive months**, **OR** clinically significant distress or impairment (social, academic/occupational, other).
- **C.** Chronological age **≥5 years** (or equivalent developmental level, i.e., mental age ≥5).
- **D.** Not attributable to a **substance** (e.g., diuretic, antipsychotic) or **another medical condition** (e.g., diabetes, spina bifida, seizure disorder). The text also lists ectopic ureter (females), posterior urethral valves (males) and tethered cord (p. 578).

## Specifiers & severity
**Specify whether:**
- **Nocturnal only:** urine passes only during nighttime sleep. Also called **monosymptomatic enuresis**. This is the most common subtype, typically in the **first third of the night**.
- **Diurnal only:** urine passes during waking hours, without nocturnal enuresis. Also called simply urinary incontinence. Two groups (p. 578):
  - **Urge incontinence:** sudden urgency, detrusor instability.
  - **Voiding postponement:** consciously deferring the urge until incontinence happens.
- **Nocturnal and diurnal:** a combination. Also called **nonmonosymptomatic enuresis**.

There are no severity or remission specifiers. Course descriptors (text, not coded): **primary** (never continent; by definition begins at age 5) vs **secondary** (after a period of established continence).

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Voiding is most often involuntary, occasionally intentional.
- The age requirement is based on when continence is **expected**. Use mental age for children with developmental delay.
- If wetting is less frequent than 2×/week, the diagnosis still applies **if** it causes clinically significant distress or impairment.

## Associated features
- **Nocturnal:** voiding sometimes occurs in REM sleep, and the child may recall a dream about urinating.
- **Diurnal:** the child postpones voiding until incontinent, sometimes from **social anxiety** about toilets or absorption in school or play. Events cluster in the **early afternoon on school days** or after returning home.
- Children with **executive-function or neurological problems** (with or without disruptive behavior) are at high risk of incontinence **without sensory awareness**.
- Daytime and nocturnal-and-diurnal incontinence often **persists after an associated infection is treated** (p. 578).

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- **Daytime incontinence:** 3.2–9.0% at age 7; 1.1–4.2% at ages 11–13; 1.2–3.0% at ages 15–17.
- **Nocturnal enuresis** (US, Netherlands, Hong Kong): about **5–10% at age 5, 3–5% at age 10, ~1% at ≥15**.
- Higher in **boys** and members of **socially oppressed groups** (e.g., African American children in the US; Turkish or Moroccan children in the Netherlands), and in youth with **learning disabilities or ADHD** (p. 578).
- Primary vs secondary types have **no difference in comorbid mental disorder rates**. Secondary onset is most common at **ages 5–8** but can occur any time.
- **Spontaneous remission of 5–10% per year** after age 5. Most are continent by adolescence. About **1%** persist into adulthood. Diurnal enuresis is uncommon after **age 9**. Occasional daytime incontinence in middle childhood is much more common with co-occurring cognitive or behavioral problems.
- When it persists into late childhood or adolescence, **urinary frequency** often continues, and incontinence may recur in adult women (p. 579).
- **Predisposing:** developmental delays, neuropsychiatric problems.
- **Environmental:** delayed toileting, psychosocial stress.
- **Genetic/physiological:** a mismatch between nocturnal urine production, nocturnal bladder capacity and **arousal from sleep**, possibly from CNS signal processing or default-mode-network differences. Children have high arousal thresholds but **poor sleep quality**. Genetically heterogeneous. Risk is **~3.6× with an enuretic mother** and **~10.1× with paternal urinary incontinence**. Risk magnitudes are similar for nocturnal and diurnal forms.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- National prevalence and developmental trajectories are **remarkably similar** across countries. Local school surveys vary widely (**4–50%**) across Africa, South Asia, Europe and the Caribbean, partly due to method. **Very high rates in orphanages and residential institutions** are not explained by the mode or timing of toilet training.
- Culture shapes the perceived cause (e.g., traditional Chinese medicine: **kidney yang deficiency**). The burden on parents is greater where care is costly or under policies such as the one-child policy, with possible parental emotional disorders (p. 579–580).
- **Nocturnal: males > females (~2:1)**, especially at younger ages, in milder cases and in nocturnal-only cases. **Diurnal: females > males**, and the ratio rises with age. UTIs often accompany daytime wetting, especially in girls. Children of **previously enuretic fathers** have a higher relative risk than children of enuretic mothers (p. 580).

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed.

## Functional consequences
- Impairment depends on limits on social activities (e.g., missing **sleep-away camp**), effects on **self-esteem**, peer **ostracism**, and caregiver **anger, punishment and rejection**.

## Differential diagnosis
- **Neurogenic bladder or another medical condition:** do not diagnose with neurogenic bladder, structural anomalies (posterior urethral valve, ectopic ureter), conditions causing polyuria or urgency (untreated **diabetes mellitus or insipidus**), or during an **acute UTI**. **Exception:** it is compatible if incontinence was regularly present **before** the condition or **persists after** appropriate treatment (p. 580).
- **Medication side effects:** antipsychotics, diuretics, or drugs causing constipation, polyuria or executive-function changes. Note it as a **medication side effect**, not enuresis in isolation. It may be diagnosed if incontinence was regularly present **before** the medication.

## Comorbidity
- Most enuretic children have **no** comorbid mental disorder. Behavioral and developmental symptoms are more common with **nocturnal and diurnal** enuresis.
- Developmental delays (speech, language, learning, motor) in a subset.
- **Encopresis and constipation** occur with both day and night incontinence.
- **Nocturnal:** associated with **restless legs syndrome**, NREM sleep arousal disorders (**sleepwalking, sleep terrors**), **heavy snoring and sleep apnea**. About **50%** of enuretic children with proven sleep-disordered breathing become dry after **adenotonsillectomy** (p. 581).
- **UTIs** are more common with daytime incontinence and nonmonosymptomatic nocturnal enuresis, especially the diurnal subtype.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **Criterion A:** where and when wetting occurs (bed, clothes, night vs day). Note intentional vs involuntary, and the reporter (parent diary, child, school).
- **Criterion B:** frequency per week and duration (**≥2×/week × ≥3 consecutive months**), **or** specific distress or impairment (avoids sleepovers or camp, teasing, shame, family conflict).
- **Criterion C:** chronological age. If delayed, developmental or mental age with its source (e.g., cognitive testing).
- **Criterion D:** pediatric or medical evaluation (urinalysis or culture, glucose, history of polyuria or polydipsia, neuro exam or spine findings, seizure history) and **medication review** (antipsychotics, diuretics, others), with timing relative to onset.
- **Subtype:** nocturnal only / diurnal only / both. For diurnal, note **urgency vs postponement** behavior.
- **Course:** primary (never dry) vs secondary (dry for a period, then relapse). Record any precipitating stressors.
- **Associated screening:** snoring or apnea, RLS, parasomnias, constipation or soiling, ADHD, learning or developmental delays, social anxiety about bathrooms.
- **Family history:** parental enuresis (especially paternal).
- **Psychosocial:** caregiver response (punitive vs supportive), self-esteem, peer effects. These document impairment and guide intervention.
- **Phrases:** "Parent reports bed-wetting ~4 nights/week for the past 6 months; child has never had a sustained dry period (primary, nocturnal only)." "UA negative; no polyuria; not on medications."
