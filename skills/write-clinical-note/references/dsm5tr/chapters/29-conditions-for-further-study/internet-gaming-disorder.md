# Internet Gaming Disorder

## Status and coding boundary
This is a **Section III research proposal**, not an officially recognized Section II disorder or a criteria set intended for clinical diagnostic use. No independent billable diagnosis is established by meeting these proposed thresholds. Assess and document actual symptoms, impairment, safety, and any established Section II condition under its own criteria. The chapter's introductory boundary applies even when its narrative uses diagnostic language. (p. 1168)


> **Spark note:** The research threshold is ≥5 of 9 features within 12 months with meaningful impairment/distress. Hours played alone are neither the threshold nor evidence of addiction.

## Proposed criteria at a glance
Persistent/recurrent gaming, often online with others, produces clinically significant impairment/distress and ≥5:
1. Gaming dominates thoughts and daily priorities.
2. Irritability, anxiety, or sadness when gaming stops; not pharmacological physical withdrawal.
3. Increasing gaming time needed.
4. Unsuccessful control attempts.
5. Other hobbies/interests lost to gaming.
6. Excessive play continues despite recognized psychosocial harm.
7. Deception about time spent gaming.
8. Gaming to escape/relieve negative mood.
9. Significant relationship, job, education, or career opportunity lost or jeopardized. (pp. 1180–1181)

## Specifiers & severity
Proposed mild/moderate/severe ratings reflect disruption of normal activity, lost opportunities/relationships, and intensity of gaming; the chapter gives **no numeric severity count cutoffs**. Five criteria remain the proposed inclusion threshold. (p. 1181)

## Scope and diagnostic features
The criteria target nongambling gaming. Work-required Internet use, social media, pornography, and general recreational browsing are outside scope. Online gambling follows the Section II gambling-disorder assessment. Although wording focuses on Internet gaming, narrative notes that offline computerized games can be included but have less research support. Long sessions and neglected food/sleep or responsibilities illustrate impairment, not mandatory hourly cutoffs. Team/social reinforcement can sustain play. (pp. 1181–1182)

## Prevalence · Risk · Sex/gender issues
Definitions vary: mean 12-month estimate 4.7% across studies (0.7%–15.6%); large U.S. Internet surveys ≤1%. Adolescent pooled estimate 4.6%, with 6.8% among boys/men and 1.3% girls/women in the cited meta-analysis. Access to games matters; adolescent/young adult men were overrepresented in studied cases. Do not treat gender or preferred game genre as diagnostic evidence. (p. 1183)

## Suicide risk
Studies link problematic Internet/gaming use with suicidal thoughts/attempts and self-injury; several use broader Internet-addiction phenotypes, limiting specificity. Sleep disruption may contribute. Individual assessment is needed rather than assuming this label determines risk. (pp. 1183–1184)

## Functional consequences · Differential · Comorbidity
School failure, job loss, relationship disruption, and neglected health/family roles may occur. Distinguish nongaming Internet overuse and gambling; high engagement without distress/impairment is insufficient. Depression, ADHD, and OCD can coexist. The chapter notes ICD-11 recognition, but that does **not** change the DSM-5-TR Section III status. (pp. 1182–1184)

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Identify actual games/online or offline pattern, each proposed symptom, persistence, unsuccessful control, and specific impairment.
- Obtain collateral where appropriate; record discrepancies rather than assuming deception.
- Distinguish time spent, recreation/social connection, required activities, and functional harm; review sleep, mood, ADHD/OCD symptoms, and safety.
