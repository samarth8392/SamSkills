# Attenuated Psychosis Syndrome

## Status and coding boundary
This is a **Section III research proposal**, not an officially recognized Section II disorder or a criteria set intended for clinical diagnostic use. No independent billable diagnosis is established by meeting these proposed thresholds. Assess and document actual symptoms, impairment, safety, and any established Section II condition under its own criteria. The chapter's introductory boundary applies even when its narrative uses diagnostic language. (p. 1168)


> **Spark note:** Recently emerging/worsening psychosis-like experiences remain below full psychotic intensity with some testable insight. Most affected help-seekers do not progress to full psychosis; risk estimates are group findings, not individual predictions.

## Proposed criteria at a glance
- **A:** ≥1 attenuated delusion, hallucination, or disorganized-speech symptom, sufficiently frequent or severe for clinical attention.
- **B:** present ≥weekly over the preceding month.
- **C:** onset or worsening within the preceding year.
- **D:** distress/disability sufficient to require clinical attention.
- **E:** not explained by another mental disorder, including mood disorders with psychotic features; not due to substances or medical illness.
- **F:** a psychotic disorder's criteria have **never** been met. (pp. 1168–1169)

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
Doubt about beliefs or perceptions can be elicited; altered perceptions are generally recognized as such. Speech can be vague, tangential, or overelaborate yet reorganizes with questions. This is a state change with clinically meaningful consequences, rather than only longstanding magical thinking or eccentricity. Anxiety, withdrawal, cognitive/negative symptoms, sleep disruption, and concentration problems can accompany it. Imaging findings distinguish research cohorts but are not individual diagnostic tests. (pp. 1169–1170)

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk
- Swiss non-help-seeking sample ages 16–40: 0.3% met syndrome criteria, versus another 2.3% with criterion-A symptoms lacking recent onset/worsening. Experiences alone occur in up to 7% in population studies. (p. 1170)
- Typical onset: mid/late adolescence or early adulthood. Evidence is strongest ages 12–35, limited at the youngest end. (pp. 1170–1171)
- In studied help-seeking cohorts, transition was up to 22% at 3 years, versus 1.54% among those not meeting the proposal; 10-year cumulative transition estimate 34.9%. Risk persists beyond initial 2 years. About thirds progress, remit, or remain disabled in the broad summary; do not treat this simplification as a precise prediction. (pp. 1170–1171)
- Poorer functioning, greater positive/negative/cognitive disorganization, male sex, stress/trauma, unemployment, and living alone predicted transition in cited work. Family history did not independently increase 4-year transition risk in the comparison described. Biomarkers remain unvalidated clinically. (p. 1171)

## Culture-related issues
Assess actual persecution, trauma, racism, migration experiences, and religious/community norms before calling suspiciousness or spiritual experience pathological. Adaptive vigilance and culturally accepted perceptions can be misread; context and impairment matter. (pp. 1171–1172)

## Functional consequences · Comorbidity
Social/role limitations may persist even when symptoms lessen. Depression (41%) and anxiety (15%) are common at presentation in cited data; over half had a comorbid disorder at follow-up. Persistent comorbidity is linked to poorer outcomes. (pp. 1172–1173)

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed as a separate association in this chapter's syndrome text.

## Differential diagnosis
- **Brief/other psychotic disorder:** full psychotic intensity crosses the proposed boundary; any prior psychotic disorder excludes this proposal.
- **Schizotypal personality disorder:** enduring trait pattern and broader features rather than required recent change.
- **Depression, bipolar, social anxiety, borderline personality presentations:** reality distortions restricted to those disorders are better accounted for there.
- **Normative adolescent adjustment or non-ill extreme traits:** mild/transient/context-appropriate experiences without needed distress/impairment do not qualify.
- **Substance-related experiences:** determine relation to intoxication/withdrawal, including cannabis, stimulants, hallucinogens, alcohol, and sedatives.
- **ADHD:** prior attention problems may coexist or precede the presentation; history alone neither confirms nor excludes it. (pp. 1172–1173)

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Record actual speech/thought/perception examples, frequency, onset/worsening date, insight, conviction, response to clarification, and role change.
- Obtain past psychosis and substance/medical/mood timelines and culturally informed threat appraisal.
- Clearly separate an observed subthreshold presentation or research assessment from an official diagnosis; document follow-up and current safety based on actual findings.
