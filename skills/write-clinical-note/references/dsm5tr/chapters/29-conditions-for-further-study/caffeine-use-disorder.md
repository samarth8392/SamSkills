# Caffeine Use Disorder

## Status and coding boundary
This is a **Section III research proposal**, not an officially recognized Section II disorder or a criteria set intended for clinical diagnostic use. No independent billable diagnosis is established by meeting these proposed thresholds. Assess and document actual symptoms, impairment, safety, and any established Section II condition under its own criteria. The chapter's introductory boundary applies even when its narrative uses diagnostic language. (p. 1168)


> **Spark note:** This deliberately restrictive proposal requires the first three features together. Daily caffeine consumption, tolerance, or withdrawal alone does not establish a disorder.

## Proposed criteria at a glance
Clinically significant impairment/distress from caffeine use, with **all of 1–3 within 12 months**:
1. Persistent wish or unsuccessful efforts to reduce/control use.
2. Continued use despite knowing a persistent/recurrent physical or psychological problem is likely caused or aggravated by caffeine.
3. Characteristic caffeine withdrawal, or caffeine/related substance taken to prevent/relieve withdrawal.

Additional assessed features, **not substitutes** for any of the three required items:
4. More or longer use than intended.
5. Recurrent major role failures from use.
6. Continued use despite recurrent interpersonal/social problems.
7. Tolerance: increased amount needed or diminished effect at the same amount.
8. Much time obtaining, using, or recovering.
9. Craving. (pp. 1177–1178)

## Features and assessment
Problems can include insomnia, anxiety, irritability, stomach/heart/urinary complaints, or trouble thinking. Use to avoid withdrawal can maintain the pattern. The algorithm differs intentionally from the ≥2-symptom threshold in many other substance use disorders to avoid pathologizing common habitual use. Caffeine intoxication and withdrawal are separate Section II diagnoses assessed under their own criteria. (pp. 1177–1178)

## Prevalence · Course · Risk
Exact population prevalence remains uncertain. A demographic-quota U.S. caffeine-consumer sample found 8% endorsing the required three; a Boston adolescent medical sample found 3.9%; these are not universal population estimates. No lifespan longitudinal/cross-sectional research on the disorder is reported. Cases involve varied products and intake levels; no mg/day threshold is supplied. Heavy use/tolerance/withdrawal heritability estimates span 35%–77%, with both shared and substance-specific genetic effects. (pp. 1178–1179)

## Culture-related issues
Beverage type, preparation, serving size, and cultural patterns change caffeine exposure. A “cup” is not a standardized dose; include tea, coffee, soda, energy drinks, mate, and medications. (p. 1179)

## Suicide risk
No research specifically addresses this proposed disorder. Studies of consumption and suicidality conflict; do not infer individual risk or protection from intake alone. (p. 1179)

## Functional consequences · Comorbidity
Reported associations include distress/guilt, sleep problems, anxiety, depression, stress, and potentially greater pregnancy use. Tobacco, cannabis problems, personal/family alcohol problems, and mood/anxiety/other substance disorders can coexist; associations do not establish caffeine causality. (p. 1180)

## Differential diagnosis
Nonproblematic habitual use may produce tolerance/withdrawal; harmful continued use and failed control are necessary here. Evaluate other stimulants. Chronic caffeine effects can resemble GAD and acute effects panic; establish exposure and symptom timelines before attributing a primary anxiety syndrome. (p. 1180)

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Record products, amounts/servings/preparation, estimated exposure, pattern, withdrawal, and attempts to reduce.
- Identify the particular harm, evidence of caffeine relationship, the person's knowledge, continued use, and role effects.
- Review co-used stimulants, medicines, medical causes, and baseline sleep/anxiety; distinguish research criteria from Section II diagnoses.
