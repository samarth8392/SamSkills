# Premature (Early) Ejaculation

> **Spark note:** A persistent pattern of ejaculating within about 1 minute of vaginal penetration, before wanting to, on ~75–100% of occasions for ≥6 months, with distress. Unlike most of this chapter, **severity is graded by latency**, not distress. The client's own latency estimate is enough for diagnosis. Men with normal latency who want to last longer, or who ejaculate early only with a new partner, don't qualify.

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** Persistent/recurrent ejaculation during partnered activity within ≈**1 minute** after vaginal penetration and before the person wishes.
  - Note: may be applied to nonvaginal activity, but no duration criteria are established for it.
- **B.** Present ≥**6 months** and on ~75–100% of occasions (situational or all contexts).
- **C.** Clinically significant distress in the individual.
- **D.** Not better explained by a nonsexual mental disorder or severe relationship distress/stressors; not due to substance/medication or medical condition.

## Specifiers & severity
- Lifelong / Acquired; Generalized / Situational.
- **Severity by latency:**
  - **Mild:** ~30 s to 1 min after penetration.
  - **Moderate:** ~15–30 s.
  - **Severe:** before sex, at the start of sex, or within ~15 s.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Operationalized by the client's **estimated** latency after penetration; estimated and stopwatch latencies correlate highly when short, so self-report suffices.
- The narrative suggests similar latency concerns in nonvaginal activity, but the formal criterion note says **validated duration criteria have not been established** for those activities. Do not apply the vaginal one-minute rule as an established nonvaginal threshold (p. 702).
- **TR note:** a 60-second IELT was the earlier cutoff for lifelong PE, but expert consensus now considers this too brief and recommends **120 seconds** (p. 702–703). The criterion text itself still says ~1 minute.

## Associated features
- Sense of lack of control over ejaculation; anticipatory apprehension about future encounters.
- Assessment factors: partner; relationship; individual vulnerability/psychiatric comorbidity/stressors; cultural/religious (incl. lack of privacy); medical.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- 8–30% internationally (definition-dependent); with the ~1-minute definition only **1–3%** qualify.
- May increase with age: ~9–11% at 18–30 (Switzerland, Turkey) vs up to 55% concern among US men 50–59.
- Lifelong: starts with first experiences and is relatively stable. Some gain control over time; persistence >6 months defines the disorder.
- Acquired: develops after normal latency, less studied, usually onset during or after the fourth decade (the 30s), rather than necessarily after age 40 (p. 703).
- **Temperamental:** more common with anxiety disorders, especially **social anxiety disorder**.
- **Genetic/physiological:** moderate genetic contribution to lifelong PE (dopamine or serotonin transporter polymorphisms); thyroid disease, prostatitis, drug withdrawal → acquired PE; PET shows ventral tegmental area/mesocephalic transition zone activation.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- What counts as normal latency differs across cultures; measured latencies may differ by country.
- More reports in arranged marriages (family pressure, limited premarital experience).
- Partners may differ in what latency is acceptable; women increasingly report concern, possibly reflecting changing attitudes about women's sexuality.
- Anatomy-based diagnosis; for gender-diverse people use current anatomy.

## Diagnostic markers
- Research uses partner stopwatch timing; clinically, accept the man's own estimate.

## Suicide risk
- Men treated for PE with comorbid depression show elevated suicidal thoughts/behavior; depression is likely contributory.

## Functional consequences
- Lower self-esteem and confidence, lack of control, relationship strain, partner distress and reduced partner satisfaction.
- Single men more bothered (affects seeking/maintaining new relationships).
- Ejaculation before penetration may impair conception.

## Differential diagnosis
- **Substance/medication-induced sexual dysfunction:** attributable exclusively to use, intoxication or withdrawal.
- **Ejaculatory concerns not meeting criteria:** normal latency but wanting longer; episodic early ejaculation (e.g., first encounter with a new partner) → no diagnosis even if distressing.

## Comorbidity
- Erectile problems (often unclear which came first).
- Lifelong PE: certain anxiety disorders. Acquired PE: prostatitis, thyroid disease, drug withdrawal (e.g., opioid withdrawal).

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **A:** client's estimated latency after penetration (seconds/minutes); occurs before he wishes; note if ejaculation occurs before penetration.
- **B:** ≥6 months; % of occasions; lifelong (since first experiences) vs acquired; generalized vs situational.
- **C:** distress, sense of lost control, anticipatory anxiety, avoidance of dating/relationships.
- **Severity:** record latency band (mild 30–60 s, moderate 15–30 s, severe ≤15 s or before/at start).
- **Rule-outs:** opioid or other drug withdrawal; prostatitis/thyroid symptoms (medical referral); new-partner-only pattern; unrealistic expectations; co-occurring ED; social anxiety screen.
