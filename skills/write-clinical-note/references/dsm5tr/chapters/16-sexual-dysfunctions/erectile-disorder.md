# Erectile Disorder

> **Spark note:** Marked difficulty getting or keeping an erection, or reduced rigidity, on ~75–100% of sexual occasions for ~6 months, with distress. "Erectile dysfunction" is a descriptive/ICD term; "erectile disorder" is the DSM category requiring 6 months plus distress. The hardest call is excluding cases fully explained by medical (especially vascular) factors. Situational, acute-onset, stress-linked problems in men under 40 point to psychological causes; generalized, gradual onset points to organic causes.

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** ≥**1 of 3**, on almost all/all (~75–100%) occasions of sexual activity (in identified situations or, if generalized, all contexts):
  1. Marked difficulty **obtaining** an erection during sexual activity.
  2. Marked difficulty **maintaining** an erection until sexual activity is completed.
  3. Marked decrease in erectile **rigidity**.
- **B.** ≈**6 months** minimum.
- **C.** Clinically significant distress in the individual.
- **D.** Not better explained by a nonsexual mental disorder or severe relationship distress/stressors; not attributable to substance/medication or another medical condition.

## Specifiers & severity
- Lifelong / Acquired; Generalized / Situational.
- Severity = degree of distress (mild, moderate, severe/extreme).

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- A careful sexual history confirms ≥~6 months and ≥75% of occasions (p. 678).
- May be situational (certain stimulation/partners) or generalized.
- Terminology: erectile dysfunction ≠ erectile disorder (p. 678).

## Associated features
- Low self-esteem and self-confidence, reduced sense of masculinity, depressed mood.
- Guilt, self-blame, sense of failure, anger, worry about disappointing partner; fear/avoidance of future sex.
- Partner: reduced sexual satisfaction and desire common.
- Assessment factors: partner; relationship; individual vulnerability (e.g., hypoactive desire)/psychiatric comorbidity/stressors; cultural/religious; medical (e.g., TURP surgery, hypogonadism, MS, diabetic neuropathy); substances/medications.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- Lifelong vs acquired prevalence unknown. Strong age increase, especially after 50.
- Ages 40–80: ~13–21% internationally. <40: <10%; 60s: ~20–40%; >70: ~50–75%. Australian longitudinal study: 80% at ≥70.
- ~20% fear erectile problems at first sex; ~8% have erectile problems that hinder penetration at first sex.
- No significant difference by ethnoracial background (US online survey); similar prevalence in older men who have sex with men or with men and women.
- First-attempt failure linked to unknown partner, drugs/alcohol, not wanting sex, peer pressure; usually remits spontaneously.
- **Acquired** → often biological (diabetes, cardiovascular disease), usually persistent. **Lifelong** → linked to psychological factors that are self-limiting or responsive to psychological treatment.
- Some with moderate erectile failure remit spontaneously.
- Risk factors for acquired ED: age, tobacco smoking, lack of exercise, diabetes, decreased desire.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- Cross-national variation may reflect cultural expectations (appearing weak/less masculine, norms on aging) rather than true frequency.
- Cultural expectations about marriage, performance, fertility and gender roles can drive anxiety. Genital-size concern (US, Middle East) and male infertility fears (Middle East) noted.
- Given only to males by definition (anatomy-based; use current anatomy for gender-diverse people). Female arousal difficulty → FSIAD.

## Diagnostic markers
- Nocturnal penile tumescence testing: adequate REM erections suggest psychological etiology.
- Doppler ultrasound, intracavernosal vasoactive injection, dynamic infusion cavernosography (vascular); pudendal nerve conduction/somatosensory evoked potentials (neuropathy).
- Bioavailable/free testosterone (esp. with diabetes, low desire, or PDE5-inhibitor nonresponse); thyroid function; fasting glucose; **serum lipids** — ED in males ≥40 predicts future coronary artery disease.

## Suicide risk
- Men treated for ED with comorbid depression show elevated suicidal thoughts/behavior; depression likely contributes.
- Elevated suicide rates in prostate cancer may partly relate to treatment-associated ED and resulting depression.

## Functional consequences
- Can interfere with fertility; individual and interpersonal distress.
- Fear/avoidance of sex can impede forming intimate relationships.

## Differential diagnosis
- **Nonsexual mental disorders (MDD):** closely linked; if ED better explained by severe depression, don't diagnose erectile disorder.
- **Normal erectile function** with excessive expectations → no diagnosis.
- **Substance/medication:** onset with start, resolves with stop/dose reduction → substance/medication-induced.
- **Another medical condition:** most difficult step. If >40–50 years old or medically ill, consider vascular disease. An organic disease does not prove causation (e.g., a man with diabetes can develop psychogenic ED). Organic: generalized, gradual onset (exception: nerve trauma like spinal cord injury). Psychological: situational, inconsistent, acute onset after a stressor, age <40.

## Comorbidity
- Premature (early) ejaculation, male hypoactive sexual desire disorder; anxiety and depressive disorders (depression risk especially high in first year after onset); PTSD.
- Lower urinary tract symptoms with prostatic hypertrophy; dyslipidemia, cardiovascular disease, hypogonadism, MS, diabetes.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **A:** which domain (obtaining / maintaining / rigidity); % of attempts affected; morning or nocturnal erections and erections with masturbation (if preserved, suggests psychogenic factors).
- **B:** onset and duration ≥~6 months; lifelong vs acquired; generalized vs situational (e.g., "only with new partners").
- **C:** client distress, avoidance, self-esteem impact.
- **Rule-outs to document:** medical workup/referral (cardiovascular risk, diabetes, testosterone, lipids, thyroid), medications (antihypertensives, SSRIs, antipsychotics), substances (alcohol, nicotine), depression screen, relationship/stressor context.
- Suicide screen when ED co-occurs with depression or after prostate cancer treatment.
- Hypothetical example (not client evidence): "Reports difficulty maintaining erection in most partnered encounters (~80%) for 8 months, onset after job loss; morning erections intact. Endorses shame and avoidance. PHQ-9 = 6. Referred to PCP for cardiometabolic labs."
