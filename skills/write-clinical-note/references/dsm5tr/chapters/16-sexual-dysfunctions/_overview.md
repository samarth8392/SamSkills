# Sexual Dysfunctions — Chapter Overview

> **Spark note:** A heterogeneous group defined by a clinically significant disturbance in the ability to respond sexually or to experience sexual pleasure. Most non-induced specific disorders require approximately 6 months, **distress in the individual**, and exclusions addressing better explanations. The ~75–100% encounter threshold applies to DE, ED and FOD, to PE, and specifically to FSIAD A4/A6; it is not a general criterion for MHSDD or GPPD (pp. 673–705). The most common error is diagnosing a sexual dysfunction when the picture is better explained by inadequate stimulation, normal aging, a desire discrepancy, depression, a medication, or a medical cause.

## What unites the chapter
- Core construct: impaired sexual response (desire, arousal, orgasm/ejaculation, pain/penetration) or impaired pleasure, with **distress** (p. 672).
- **Multiple diagnoses allowed:** if several dysfunctions co-exist, diagnose all of them (p. 672).
- **Inadequate sexual stimulation** (e.g., lack of knowledge about effective stimulation) → care may be needed but **no** sexual dysfunction diagnosis (p. 672).
- Biopsychosocial: biological underpinning experienced in intrapersonal, interpersonal and cultural context; etiology often unknown (p. 672).
- Aging and relationship duration can bring a **normative** decline in sexual response (p. 672).

## How the disorders differ
| Domain | Disorder | Key threshold |
|---|---|---|
| Ejaculation (delayed) | Delayed Ejaculation | Marked delay/infrequency/absence, partnered activity, ~75–100%, ~6 mo |
| Erection | Erectile Disorder | ≥1 of 3 (obtain / maintain / rigidity), ~75–100%, ~6 mo |
| Orgasm (female) | Female Orgasmic Disorder | Delay/infrequency/absence or reduced intensity, ~75–100%, ~6 mo |
| Desire + arousal (female) | Female Sexual Interest/Arousal Disorder | ≥3 of 6 indicators, ~6 mo |
| Pain/penetration | Genito-Pelvic Pain/Penetration Disorder | ≥1 of 4 dimensions, persistent/recurrent, ~6 mo; no generalized/situational specifier |
| Desire (male) | Male Hypoactive Sexual Desire Disorder | Deficient thoughts/fantasies **and** desire (both), ~6 mo, clinician judges deficiency |
| Ejaculation (early) | Premature (Early) Ejaculation | ≤~1 min after vaginal penetration, ≥6 mo, ~75–100%; severity by latency |
| Etiologic | Substance/Medication-Induced Sexual Dysfunction | Temporal link + capable agent; severity by % of occasions |
| Residual | Other Specified (F52.8) / Unspecified (F52.9) | Distressing, subthreshold or unclassifiable |

Note: In females, desire and arousal are merged (FSIAD); in males they remain separate (MHSDD vs erectile disorder) (p. 700–701).

## TR changes / notable updates in the text
- **Gender-diverse individuals** (transgender, nonbinary, agender): despite sex-labelled names, MHSDD and FSIAD criteria describe experiences not dependent on sex/gender → either can be applied by clinical judgment. For anatomy-linked diagnoses (erectile disorder, premature ejaculation, delayed ejaculation, GPPD) base the diagnosis on **current anatomy**, not sex assigned at birth (p. 673).
- **Asexual self-identification** explaining lifelong low desire → do not diagnose FSIAD or MHSDD (pp. 688, 701).
- The supplied TR text discusses suicidal thoughts/behavior with erectile disorder, FOD, FSIAD and PE; these passages do not themselves establish which sections are new versus earlier editions (pp. 680–681, 685, 690, 704).
- Premature ejaculation: text notes expert consensus now favors a **120-second** IELT threshold for lifelong PE, though the criterion still states ~1 minute (p. 702–703).
- Substance/medication-induced: this edition supplies the ICD-10-CM table (4th character 1/2/9 by comorbid use disorder); 5-α-reductase inhibitors, gabapentin, and methadone versus buprenorphine are discussed (pp. 706, 708). The excerpt does not establish that each code changed in TR.
- Sexual Dysfunctions codes: delayed ejaculation F52.32; FOD F52.31; erectile disorder F52.21; FSIAD F52.22; GPPD F52.6; MHSDD F52.0; PE F52.4.

## Shared specifiers & coding conventions
- **Lifelong** (present since sexually active) vs **Acquired** (after relatively normal function) — the seven non-induced specific disorders; substance/medication-induced dysfunction uses exposure-onset specifiers instead (pp. 674–707).
- **Generalized** vs **Situational** (limited to certain stimulation, situations, partners) — all except GPPD.
- **Severity** (mild/moderate/severe) = **degree of distress** for DE, ED, FOD, FSIAD, GPPD, MHSDD. Exceptions: PE severity by **ejaculatory latency**; substance-induced severity by **% of occasions** (25–50 / 50–75 / ≥75%).
- FOD has an extra specifier: **Never experienced an orgasm under any situation**.
- **Five assessment factors** to consider for each dysfunction (not specifiers, but document them): (1) partner factors, (2) relationship factors, (3) individual vulnerability / psychiatric comorbidity / stressors, (4) cultural/religious factors, (5) medical factors. DE and ED add (6) substances/medications (p. 672, and each disorder).
- Relationship distress/partner violence/stressor better explains → no sexual dysfunction; list a Z code instead (e.g., **Z63.0** Relationship distress with spouse or intimate partner) (p. 673).
- Substance-induced: one combined code (no separate SUD diagnosis); medications not in a class (e.g., fluoxetine) use F19.x81.

## Chapter-level differential logic
1. **Nonsexual mental disorder** (depressive, bipolar, anxiety, PTSD, psychotic) mostly explains it → diagnose only that disorder (p. 673).
2. **Substance/medication** onset/offset linked → substance/medication-induced sexual dysfunction.
3. **Medical condition** fully explains it (e.g., peripheral neuropathy, pelvic nerve damage) → no psychiatric diagnosis. Coexistence with a medical condition is still allowed when not fully explanatory; a known organic disease does not prove causation.
4. **Severe relationship distress, partner violence or significant stressors that better explain the problem** → no sexual dysfunction; use an appropriate contextual Z code. Their mere coexistence does not independently establish that explanation (p. 673).
5. **Inadequate stimulation**, **unrealistic expectations**, **normal aging**, **desire discrepancy**, **transient adaptive changes**, **episodic/new-partner** problems → not a disorder.
6. Sex-labeled individual sections describe conventional counterparts, but the chapter introduction explicitly permits either desire diagnosis for gender-diverse individuals by clinical judgment and bases anatomy-linked diagnoses on **current anatomy**. Male urological pelvic-pain presentations are discussed as residual-category examples; do not substitute assigned sex or gender label for the relevant anatomy (pp. 673, 696).
7. Anhedonic ejaculation (normal ejaculation, reduced pleasure) → other specified/unspecified, not DE (p. 677).

## Pitfalls
- Forgetting distress must be in the **individual** (partner distress alone often drives help-seeking, e.g., DE).
- FOD and FSIAD explicitly allow judgment around **approximately 6 months** (pp. 683, 688). Do not turn approximate language into permission to disregard the required persistence or encounter-frequency pattern.
- Orgasm only with clitoral stimulation (not during intercourse) is **not** FOD.
- MHSDD requires **both** deficient fantasies and deficient desire.
- GPPD: all four dimensions should be assessed even though one suffices.
- Erectile **dysfunction** (descriptive/ICD term) ≠ erectile **disorder** (DSM category with 6 mo + distress).

## Documentation cues (chapter-wide)
- Record: specific symptom, % of occasions, duration (≥~6 mo), onset (lifelong/acquired), context (generalized/situational), distress level (severity).
- Record the five factors: partner, relationship, individual vulnerability/psychiatric comorbidity/stressors, cultural/religious, medical; plus medications/substances with start dates.
- Document rule-outs: depression/other mental disorder, medication/substance timeline, medical evaluation/referral, relationship distress/IPV screen, adequacy of stimulation, age-normative change, asexual identity where relevant.
- Use current anatomy and the client's identity/terms for gender-diverse clients.
