# Female Sexual Interest/Arousal Disorder

> **Spark note:** Lack of or significantly reduced sexual interest/arousal shown by ≥3 of 6 indicators for ~6 months, with distress. It merges the old hypoactive desire and arousal diagnoses for women. A **desire discrepancy** (lower desire than a partner) is not enough. Do not diagnose if lifelong low desire is better explained by **asexual** identity, if stimulation is inadequate, or if changes are short-term and adaptive.

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** Lack of or significantly reduced interest/arousal, ≥**3 of 6**:
  1. Absent/reduced interest in sexual activity.
  2. Absent/reduced sexual/erotic thoughts or fantasies.
  3. No/reduced initiation, and typically unreceptive to partner's initiation.
  4. Absent/reduced excitement/pleasure during sex on ~75–100% of encounters (situational or all contexts).
  5. Absent/reduced interest/arousal in response to internal or external erotic cues (written, verbal, visual).
  6. Absent/reduced genital or nongenital sensations during sex on ~75–100% of encounters.
- **B.** ≈**6 months**.
- **C.** Clinically significant distress in the individual.
- **D.** Not better explained by a nonsexual mental disorder or severe relationship distress (e.g., partner violence)/stressors; not due to substance/medication or medical condition.

## Specifiers & severity
- Lifelong / Acquired (for A3, A4, A6, lifelong = since first sexual experiences); Generalized / Situational.
- Severity = distress (mild / moderate / severe-extreme).

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Interpersonal context must be considered (p. 687).
- Profiles vary: some women show mainly low interest, few thoughts and noninitiation; others show inability to become excited, poor responsive desire and lack of physical arousal.
- Desire and arousal problems co-occur: women with loss of desire may be ~9× more likely to also have lost arousal.
- Short-term changes are common and adaptive; persistence (~6 months) is required, and clinical judgment can be used when duration can't be pinned down.
- A1 was formerly the single criterion of hypoactive sexual desire disorder.
- A2: fantasies vary widely and may include memories; account for normative age decline.
- A3: behavioral; couple's initiation preferences matter.
- A4: lack of pleasure is a common complaint in women with low desire.
- A5: lack of "**responsive desire**"; at least two possible types — low sensitivity to sexual cues vs overactive sexual inhibition. Assess adequacy of stimuli.
- A6: may include reduced lubrication/vasocongestion; physiological measures don't discriminate, so **self-report is sufficient**.
- Distress may come from the lack of interest/arousal itself or from its interference with life and well-being.

## Associated features
- Orgasm problems, pain during sex, infrequent sex, couple desire discrepancies.
- Relationship difficulties, chronic stress, mood disorders.
- Unrealistic expectations about "appropriate" desire, poor sexual techniques, lack of sexual information, normative gender-role beliefs.
- Five assessment factors: partner (incl. partner-related distress); relationship (incl. duration); individual vulnerability/psychiatric comorbidity/stressors; cultural/religious; medical.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- ~**30%** of women have chronic low desire; ~half of those have significant partner-related distress and ~a quarter personal distress.
- Prevalence much lower when distress is required and when persistence is required. Low desire rises with age, but related distress falls with age.
- Normative changes across the life span; in longer relationships, women more often begin sex without initial desire (responsive pattern).
- **Temperamental:** negative cognitions/attitudes about sexuality, past mental disorders, sexual excitation/inhibition propensity.
- **Environmental:** relationship difficulties, partner sexual function, developmental history (early caregiver relationships, childhood stressors).
- **Genetic/physiological:** diabetes, thyroid dysfunction; strong genetic influence; vaginal photoplethysmography doesn't differ by perceived arousal.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- Low desire prevalence 26–43% across world regions; some ethnoracial and migrant groups report lower desire, possibly an artifact of measures and reporting norms (e.g., for unmarried, menopausal or widowed women). Judge against the person's cultural norms.
- The individual section uses sex-labeled counterparts; the chapter introduction explicitly permits either desire diagnosis in gender-diverse people by clinical judgment because these symptom criteria are not inherently sex-dependent (pp. 673, 690).
- No data showing different rates/expressions in lesbian vs heterosexual women.

## Suicide risk
- Arousal/satisfaction dysfunctions associated with suicidal thoughts in female veterans/service members after adjusting for probable PTSD, depression and other factors.

## Functional consequences
- Often associated with decreased relationship satisfaction.

## Differential diagnosis
- **Nonsexual mental disorders** (MDD with pervasive diminished interest/pleasure) → if lack of interest is completely attributable, no FSIAD.
- **Substance/medication** (onset/offset) → substance/medication-induced.
- **Medical** (diabetes, endothelial disease, thyroid dysfunction, CNS disease) almost exclusively explains → no FSIAD.
- **Interpersonal factors** (severe relationship distress, IPV, stressors) → no FSIAD.
- **Other sexual dysfunctions:** do not rule out FSIAD; chronic genital pain may cause low desire for painful sex; low arousal can impair orgasm.
- **Inadequate/absent sexual stimuli** → care possibly, no diagnosis. Also transient adaptive changes after a life event.
- **Asexual identity** explaining lifelong lack of desire → no diagnosis.

## Comorbidity
- Very common with other sexual difficulties; distress and dissatisfaction highly correlated.
- Depression, thyroid problems, anxiety, urinary incontinence, other medical factors; arthritis and inflammatory/irritable bowel disease (arousal); sexual/physical abuse in adulthood; alcohol use.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **A:** list which of the 6 indicators are present (need ≥3), e.g., "rarely thinks about sex (A2), no longer initiates and declines partner's invitations (A3), little pleasure during sex in nearly all encounters (A4)."
- **B:** ≥~6 months; lifelong vs acquired; generalized vs situational (e.g., only with current partner).
- **C:** client's own distress vs partner pressure; document that it is not just a desire discrepancy.
- **Rule-outs:** depression (pervasive anhedonia), medications (SSRIs, antipsychotics, hormonal contraceptives), medical (thyroid, diabetes, menopause), IPV/relationship screen, adequacy of stimulation, recent life events, asexual identity.
- Cultural/religious context for norms of desire.
