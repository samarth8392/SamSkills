# Genito-Pelvic Pain/Penetration Disorder

> **Spark note:** Persistent or recurrent difficulty in ≥1 of four overlapping dimensions — penetration difficulty, genito-pelvic pain, fear/anxiety about pain or penetration, pelvic floor tensing — for ~6 months with distress. It replaces dyspareunia and vaginismus. One dimension is enough to diagnose, but **assess all four**. It often coexists with a gynecological condition, and there is no reliable way to tell which is primary. There is no generalized/situational specifier.

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** Persistent/recurrent difficulty with ≥**1**:
  1. Vaginal penetration during intercourse.
  2. Marked vulvovaginal or pelvic **pain** during intercourse/penetration attempts.
  3. Marked **fear/anxiety** about vulvovaginal or pelvic pain before, during or after penetration.
  4. Marked **tensing/tightening of pelvic floor muscles** during attempted penetration.
- **B.** ≈**6 months**.
- **C.** Clinically significant distress in the individual.
- **D.** Not better explained by a nonsexual mental disorder or severe relationship distress (e.g., partner violence)/stressors; not due to substance/medication or medical condition.

## Specifiers & severity
- **Lifelong / Acquired** only (no generalized/situational). If unclear, ask whether there was any consistent period of pain-, fear- and tension-free intercourse → if yes, acquired.
- Severity = distress (mild / moderate / severe-extreme).

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- **A1 (penetration):** ranges from total inability in any setting (intercourse, gynecological exam, tampons) to difficulty in only some settings.
- **A2 (pain):** assess location and intensity; superficial (vulvovaginal/at entry) vs deep (pelvic, with deeper penetration); provoked vs spontaneous; quality (burning, cutting, shooting, throbbing). May persist after intercourse or occur with urination; usually reproducible on gynecological exam. Intensity doesn't track linearly with distress.
- **A3 (fear):** often a "normal" reaction to repeated pain → avoidance; sometimes fear is not tied to pain, resembling a phobia of penetration or pain.
- **A4 (pelvic floor):** from reflex-like spasm to voluntary guarding; with guarding, penetration may be possible when relaxed. Best evaluated by a specialist gynecologist or pelvic floor physical therapist.
- Prior terms: dyspareunia, vaginismus. Vulvodynia (chronic idiopathic vulvar pain ≥3 months) and provoked vestibulodynia may be a primary cause, and those affected usually meet GPPD criteria.

## Associated features
- Frequently with reduced desire/interest (FSIAD); desire may be preserved in non-painful, non-penetrative situations.
- Behavioral avoidance of sex and of gynecological exams (phobic-like pattern).
- Often presents for treatment only when wishing to conceive.
- Relationship/marital problems; diminished feelings of femininity.
- Five assessment factors: partner; relationship (incl. solicitous, negative and facilitative partner responses to pain); individual vulnerability/psychiatric comorbidity/stressors; cultural/religious; medical.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- True prevalence unknown. US: ~10–28% of reproductive-age females report recurrent pain during intercourse; internationally 8–28%.
- Lesbian women: similar or lower; transgender women and other minorities unknown.
- Hard to classify lifelong vs acquired; tampon difficulty/avoidance is an early predictor. Once ~6 months, spontaneous remission becomes less likely.
- Peaks: early adulthood, peri/postmenopause; may increase postpartum.
- **Temperamental:** antecedent mood/anxiety disorders → ~**4×** risk; pain catastrophizing, low pain self-efficacy, pain avoidance, negative mood; insecure attachment, negative partner responses, avoidance-focused sexual motives maintain symptoms.
- **Environmental:** history of sexual/physical abuse and fear of abuse more common (not universal).
- **Genetic/physiological:** onset after vaginal infections (pain persists after resolution); tampon pain/inability before sexual contact; early puberty, inflammation, early oral contraceptive use, vulvar nociceptor proliferation/sensitization, low touch/pain thresholds; pelvic floor hypertonicity, poor control, hypersensitivity.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- Social narratives prioritizing men's desire and penetrative sex, and sex as "easy and natural," shape experience; some women report marital unhappiness instead of pain.
- US Hispanic females report higher rates and more pain at first intercourse. Only about half seek treatment, often feeling stigmatized; heightened for sexual minorities and racialized groups given pain-treatment inequities.
- Shame and feelings of inadequacy as a woman add distress.
- The individual section describes female presentations and male urological chronic pelvic pain (2.2–9.7% worldwide), referring the latter to residual categories (p. 696). The chapter introduction directs anatomy-linked diagnoses according to **current anatomy**, not assigned sex, for gender-diverse clients (p. 673).

## Diagnostic markers
- A2 measurable in real time: cotton-swab test, vulvalgesiometer, tampon test (none replicate sexual context; self-report needed).
- A4: EMG amplitude, dynamometer, 4D ultrasound by a qualified physical therapist.
- No validated physiological measures for A1 or A3; validated psychometric inventories exist for pain/anxiety.

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed.

## Functional consequences
- Interference with romantic relationships, including starting them; sometimes with conception via penile-vaginal intercourse.

## Differential diagnosis
- **Another medical condition** (lichen sclerosus, endometriosis, PID, genitourinary syndrome of menopause, vulvovaginal atrophy): often co-diagnosed; treating it sometimes but often doesn't resolve GPPD; no reliable way to tell which is primary.
- **Somatic symptom and related disorders:** some meet both; reliable separation not yet established.
- **Specific phobia:** some also meet criteria.
- **Inadequate sexual stimuli** (inadequate foreplay/arousal), or partner's erectile disorder/premature ejaculation causing penetration difficulty → GPPD may not be appropriate.

## Comorbidity
- Other sexual difficulties; relationship distress (often from lack of intimacy rather than pain alone).
- Pelvic floor/reproductive disorders: interstitial cystitis, constipation, vaginal infection, endometriosis, IBS.
- Chronic pain conditions (fibromyalgia, chronic headache), increasing with vulvar pain severity.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **A:** address each dimension: penetration (intercourse, tampons, pelvic exam); pain location (superficial/deep), quality, provoked/spontaneous, post-coital or with urination; anticipatory fear and avoidance; reported muscle tightening/guarding.
- **B:** duration ≥~6 months; lifelong vs acquired (any period of pain-free intercourse?).
- **C:** distress, avoidance, effect on relationship/conception plans.
- **Rule-outs:** gynecological evaluation (infection, vulvodynia, endometriosis, atrophy); pelvic floor PT assessment; IPV and relationship screen; adequacy of arousal/foreplay; partner sexual function; trauma history (sensitively).
- Note comorbid anxiety/mood, phobic avoidance, and chronic pain.
- Hypothetical example (not client evidence): "Reports burning pain at vaginal entry with all penetration attempts for ~2 years, plus marked anticipatory fear and avoidance of intimacy; unable to tolerate speculum exam. Desire intact for non-penetrative activity. GYN referral for vestibulodynia evaluation."
