# Female Orgasmic Disorder

> **Spark note:** Marked delay, infrequency or absence of orgasm, or markedly reduced orgasm intensity, on ~75–100% of sexual occasions for ~6 months, with distress. Reaching orgasm through clitoral stimulation but not during intercourse is **not** a disorder, and neither is a problem caused by inadequate stimulation. Sexual satisfaction is not strongly tied to orgasm; many women are satisfied despite rarely having orgasms, so distress is essential.

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** Either, on almost all/all (~75–100%) occasions of sexual activity (in identified situations or, if generalized, all contexts):
  1. Marked delay in, marked infrequency of, or absence of orgasm.
  2. Markedly reduced intensity of orgasmic sensations.
- **B.** ≈**6 months** minimum ("approximately" allows clinician judgment).
- **C.** Clinically significant distress in the individual.
- **D.** Not better explained by a nonsexual mental disorder or severe relationship distress (e.g., partner violence)/stressors; not attributable to substance/medication or another medical condition.

## Specifiers & severity
- Lifelong / Acquired; Generalized / Situational.
- **Specify if: Never experienced an orgasm under any situation.**
- Severity = distress (mild / moderate / severe-extreme).

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Wide variability in the stimulation that elicits orgasm and in how orgasm is experienced, across women and across occasions (p. 683).
- Minimum severity/duration criteria separate transient problems from persistent dysfunction.
- Causes often multifactorial or undetermined.
- Excluded if better explained by another mental disorder, substance, medical condition, or severe relationship distress/IPV/stressors.
- Many women need clitoral stimulation; few always reach orgasm during penile-vaginal intercourse.

## Associated features
- No consistent link with specific personality traits or psychopathology.
- May have more difficulty communicating about sex.
- Often co-occurs with sexual interest/arousal problems.
- Five assessment factors: partner; relationship; individual vulnerability (poor body image, abuse history)/psychiatric comorbidity/stressors; cultural/religious; medical.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- Orgasm problems in premenopausal women: 8–72% (distress not accounted for; only some are distressed).
- ~10% of women internationally never experience orgasm.
- Orgasm consistency is higher with masturbation than with a partner. Many women learn orgasm through varied stimulation and body knowledge.
- **Temperamental:** anxiety, concerns about pregnancy.
- **Environmental:** relationship problems, physical and mental health; sociocultural factors (gender-role expectations, religious norms).
- **Genetic/physiological:** MS, pelvic nerve damage from radical hysterectomy, spinal cord injury; **SSRIs** delay/inhibit orgasm; vulvovaginal atrophy linked to more orgasm difficulty; menopause **not** consistently linked; likely genetic contribution.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- Whether absent orgasm is seen as needing treatment depends on culture; views undervaluing women's sexual satisfaction or treating marital sex as duty reduce help-seeking.
- Women differ in how much orgasm matters to satisfaction; reported inability to reach orgasm varies about twofold across world regions.
- The individual section uses women/men labels, but p. 673 directs anatomy-linked diagnoses by current anatomy in gender-diverse people. Orgasm sensation and ejaculation are distinct: reduced pleasure with normal ejaculation belongs to residual categories rather than automatically delayed ejaculation (pp. 677, 685).

## Diagnostic markers
- Physiological changes (hormones, pelvic floor, brain activation) vary too much; diagnosis is by **self-report**.

## Suicide risk
- Arousal and satisfaction dysfunctions are associated with suicidal thoughts among female veterans/service members, even after adjusting for probable PTSD, depression and other factors.

## Functional consequences
- Unclear. Strong association with relationship problems, but direction (cause vs consequence) is unknown.

## Differential diagnosis
- **Nonsexual mental disorders** (e.g., MDD) better explain → no FOD.
- **Substance/medication** onset/offset linked → substance/medication-induced.
- **Medical condition** (MS, spinal cord injury) → no FOD.
- **Interpersonal factors:** severe relationship distress, IPV, significant stressors → no FOD.
- **Other sexual dysfunctions** (e.g., FSIAD): can co-occur; another dysfunction doesn't rule out FOD.
- Occasional, short-term, nondistressing difficulty, or **inadequate stimulation** → no diagnosis.

## Comorbidity
- Sexual interest/arousal difficulties; MDD may lower interest/arousal and indirectly raise orgasm difficulty.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **A:** describe delay/infrequency/absence vs reduced intensity; % of occasions; with partner vs masturbation; types of stimulation tried (rules out inadequate stimulation).
- **B:** duration ≥~6 months; lifelong (never/always) vs acquired; note "never experienced orgasm under any situation" if true.
- **C:** client's own distress (not just partner's).
- **Rule-outs:** SSRI/other medications and timing; medical history (MS, pelvic surgery, spinal injury, vulvovaginal atrophy) with GYN referral; depression screen; relationship safety/IPV screen; cultural/religious context.
- Hypothetical example (not client evidence): "Reports orgasm on <20% of partnered occasions for >1 year despite clitoral stimulation; previously orgasmic. Onset predates sertraline. Endorses moderate distress and frustration. Denies IPV."
