# Substance/Medication-Induced Sexual Dysfunction

> **Spark note:** A distressing sexual disturbance that predominates and is caused by a substance or medication, with onset during or soon after intoxication, withdrawal or medication exposure/change. Antidepressants and antipsychotics are the classic causes. Severity is graded by **% of sexual occasions** affected, not by distress. Symptoms predating exposure or persisting about a month beyond acute withdrawal/severe intoxication raise an independent explanation, but the course text also recognizes persistent SRI-associated cases after discontinuation. A start–stop–recurrence history supports attribution when already available; deliberate rechallenge is not required (pp. 705, 708–710).

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** Clinically significant disturbance in sexual function **predominates** in the clinical picture.
- **B.** Evidence (history, exam, labs) of **both**:
  1. Onset during/soon after intoxication or withdrawal, or after exposure to or withdrawal from a medication.
  2. The substance/medication is capable of producing the symptoms.
- **C.** Not better explained by an independent (non-induced) sexual dysfunction. Evidence of independence: symptoms **precede** use; persist a substantial time (e.g., **~1 month**) after acute withdrawal/severe intoxication ends; or other evidence (e.g., recurrent non-substance-related episodes).
- **D.** Not exclusively during **delirium**.
- **E.** Clinically significant distress in the individual.
- **Note:** diagnose instead of intoxication/withdrawal only when sexual symptoms predominate and warrant clinical attention.

## Specifiers & severity
- Onset (see Table 1 of Substance-Related chapter for which applies to each class):
  - **With onset during intoxication** (intoxication criteria met; onset during intoxication).
  - **With onset during withdrawal** (withdrawal criteria met; onset during/shortly after withdrawal).
  - **With onset after medication use** (at initiation, with a change in use, or during withdrawal of medication).
- **Severity (by frequency):** Mild = 25–50% of occasions; Moderate = 50–75%; Severe = ≥75%. The source bands overlap at 50% and 75%; it gives no boundary-resolution instruction. Document the actual estimate and judgment (p. 707).

## Coding
4th character: **1** = comorbid mild use disorder; **2** = moderate/severe use disorder; **9** = no use disorder. No separate SUD diagnosis is given.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- For prescribed treatment, onset must be while taking the medication (or during its withdrawal). After stopping, usually improves/remits within **days to several weeks** depending on half-life and withdrawal.
- The diagnostic narrative cautions against attribution when symptoms precede exposure or persist >~1 month after severe intoxication/withdrawal (p. 708), yet the course section reports SRI-related persistence after discontinuation (p. 709). Preserve this tension: chronology supports evaluation, but a fixed month is not proof of independence for every medication-related presentation.

## Associated features
- **Intoxication-related classes:** alcohol; opioids; sedatives/hypnotics/anxiolytics; stimulants incl. cocaine; other/unknown.
- **Withdrawal-related classes:** alcohol; opioids; sedatives/hypnotics/anxiolytics; other/unknown.
- **Medications:** antidepressants, antipsychotics, hormonal contraceptives.
- **Antidepressants:** most common = orgasm/ejaculation difficulty in men, arousal difficulty in women; desire and erection problems less frequent. Effects occur regardless of depression level. ~30% of complaints clinically significant. Lower rates with **bupropion, mirtazapine, nefazodone, vilazodone**.
- **Antipsychotics:** desire, erection, lubrication, ejaculation, orgasm problems with typical and atypical agents; less with prolactin-sparing or non-dopamine-blocking agents.
- **Mood stabilizers:** lithium and anticonvulsants (possible exception lamotrigine) may lower desire; gabapentin → orgasm problems. Benzodiazepines → more erectile/orgasm problems; not reported with buspirone.
- **Nonpsychiatric:** cardiovascular, cytotoxic, GI, hormonal agents; **5-α-reductase inhibitors** (finasteride, dutasteride) → reduced erection, ejaculation, libido.
- **Illicit substances:** lower desire, ED, orgasm difficulty. Methadone → frequent dysfunction; buprenorphine → seldom. Chronic nicotine/alcohol → erectile problems. Cannabis may be a risk factor, though possibly improves orgasm satisfaction.

## Prevalence · Development & course
- Unclear, likely underreported. MAOIs, TCAs, serotonergic and SNRI antidepressants: **25–80%** report sexual side effects; highest with citalopram, fluoxetine, fluvoxamine, paroxetine, sertraline, venlafaxine.
- Antipsychotics: ~**50%**.
- High-dose opioid analgesics: elevated rates. Heroin: ~60–70% (higher than amphetamine-type/MDMA).
- Antidepressant onset can be as early as **8 days**; ~30% with mild–moderate orgasm delay remit spontaneously within 6 months; SRI-induced dysfunction may sometimes persist after stopping.
- Nicotine/alcohol effects may take years. PE can occur after stopping opioids. Increases with age.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- Culture may interact with medication effects and the person's response.
- Men may report more desire/orgasm impairment on antidepressants; women more arousal difficulty.

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed.

## Functional consequences
- **Medication nonadherence** (stopping or irregular use), which can undermine antidepressant efficacy.

## Differential diagnosis
- **Non-substance/medication-induced sexual dysfunctions:** depressive, bipolar, anxiety, psychotic disorders themselves disturb sexual function — hard to separate. A close temporal relation to starting/stopping supports the induced diagnosis; clear diagnosis if onset after start, resolution after stop, recurrence on reintroduction. Dysfunction emerging only after chronic use is very hard to attribute.
- **Intoxication/withdrawal:** use this diagnosis only when sexual symptoms predominate and need attention.
- **Delirium:** excluded if only during delirium.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **A/E:** specific sexual symptom(s), distress, and that it is a predominant clinical concern.
- **B:** medication/substance name, dose, start/change/stop dates; symptom onset date relative to these; known sexual side-effect profile.
- **C:** pre-existing sexual function (baseline before medication); course after dose reduction/discontinuation; any rechallenge.
- **Severity:** % of sexual occasions affected (25–50 / 50–75 / ≥75%).
- Record comorbid SUD severity to pick 4th character; do not list SUD separately.
- Document adherence risk and discussion with prescriber (e.g., consider lower-liability agent).

Distressed; considering stopping medication. Dx F19.981 paroxetine-induced sexual dysfunction, with onset after medication use, severe. Coordinating with prescriber."
