# Cultural Concepts of Distress

> **Spark note:** A cultural concept may describe an idiom, an explanation, a syndrome, or several at once. Its relationship to DSM diagnoses is many-to-many; related diagnoses below are assessment possibilities, not automatic translations.

## Assessment principles
Idioms communicate suffering without necessarily defining a syndrome; explanations attribute cause or meaning; syndromes group locally recognizable experiences. All may change over time, occur without a mental disorder, or coexist with one. Using the person's vocabulary can improve detection, rapport, treatment negotiation, and assessment of risk and resilience. Determine clinical syndrome and impairment separately, then retain cultural meanings in the formulation. (pp. 1135–1137)

## Ten illustrations in source order
| Concept and pages | Core presentation / clinical distinction | Related DSM assessment directions |
|---|---|---|
| **Ataque de nervios**, pp. 1137–1138 | Latinx contexts: intense, episodic emotional upset and loss of control, often after family stress; crying, shouting, shaking, anger, sometimes dissociation, fainting/seizure-like episodes, or suicidal behavior. May be a normative grief expression. | Panic, dissociative, functional neurological, intermittent explosive, anxiety, and trauma-related conditions; distinguish substance blackouts from similarly named regional idioms. |
| **Dhat syndrome**, pp. 1138–1139 | South Asian explanation linking distress, weakness, sexual concerns, or somatic symptoms to perceived semen loss; not one discrete syndrome. Related vaginal-discharge concerns occur in women. | Depression, anxiety, somatic symptom/illness anxiety, erectile/ejaculatory or other sexual dysfunction; distinguish infection and obtain relevant medical assessment. |
| **Hikikomori**, p. 1139 | Marked sustained withdrawal from in-person social life, often with school/work nonparticipation and online contact. Japanese guideline cited uses 6 months; this is not a DSM diagnostic threshold. | Social anxiety, depression, GAD, PTSD, autism, schizoid/avoidant personality patterns, psychotic disorders; gaming and school refusal may accompany it. |
| **Khyâl cap**, pp. 1139–1140 | Cambodian wind attacks: autonomic symptoms plus feared bodily ascent of wind/blood and catastrophic consequences. Orthostasis, odors, worry, or crowded settings can trigger attacks. | Often panic-attack phenomenology; assess panic disorder, agoraphobia, GAD, PTSD, illness anxiety. |
| **Kufungisisa**, pp. 1140–1141 | Shona idiom/explanation of excessive thinking linked to distress, social adversity, worries, rumination, or trauma. Similar terms vary substantially across cultures. | Depressive disorders, GAD, PTSD, OCD, prolonged grief; do not assume one universal meaning. |
| **Maladi dyab**, p. 1141 | Haitian explanation of illness or misfortune as intentionally sent spiritual harm, often understood through envy and interpersonal conflict. Defined by causal attribution rather than a symptom set. | Broad psychiatric or nonpsychiatric difficulties; unfamiliar spiritual attribution alone does not establish persecutory delusion or schizophrenia. |
| **Nervios**, pp. 1141–1142 | Latinx idiom/explanation encompassing vulnerability, emotional and somatic distress, and difficulty functioning; local variants differ. | Ranges from no disorder through depression, anxiety, dissociation, somatic symptoms, or psychosis. |
| **Shenjing shuairuo**, pp. 1142–1143 | Chinese neurasthenia-related syndrome linking fatigue, irritability/worry, arousal, pain, and sleep difficulty to weakness/imbalance and social stress. Historical CCMD-2-R used 3 of 5 clusters; these are not DSM criteria. | Mood, anxiety, somatic symptom, social/specific phobia, PTSD. Text notes declining use and increasing depression/anxiety idioms, especially urban settings. |
| **Susto**, p. 1143 | Some mainland Latinx contexts: fright interpreted as soul loss, with varied emotional, somatic, and role difficulties; onset days to years later. Not universal across Latinx communities. | Interpersonal-loss pattern may resemble MDD; trauma-organized symptoms PTSD; recurrent somatic presentation somatic symptom disorder. |
| **Taijin kyofusho**, p. 1144 | Japanese interpersonal-fear construct: sensitive form centers on social sensitivity; offensive form on distressing others through appearance, odor, gaze, movement, or perceived deformity. Broader than social anxiety. | Social anxiety, BDD, delusional disorder, OCD, olfactory reference syndrome (other specified OCRD); assess conviction and insight. |

## Quantitative and contextual pearls
- Ataque reports in U.S. Latinx community samples: 7%–15% adults, 4%–9% youth; ranges vary by region/subgroup. Associated suicidal thoughts, disability, and service use do not make every ataque pathological. (p. 1138)
- Dhat clinic estimates (e.g., 64% in Indian psychiatric sexual-complaint clinics, 30% in Pakistani general medical clinics) are selected-setting figures, not population prevalence. (p. 1139)
- Up to 45% of Chinese medical-clinic patients described with shenjing shuairuo did not meet any DSM-IV disorder criteria in studies cited. (p. 1142)
- Related international terms illustrate similarity, not interchangeability: falling out/indisposition, koro/shen-k'uei, wind idioms, thinking-too-much idioms, evil-eye explanations, nerves/tension, neurasthenia forms, soul loss, and Korean taein kong po. (pp. 1138–1144)

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Elicit what the term means to this person, who else uses it, the events surrounding it, expected consequences, and desired response.
- Record symptoms, timing, insight/conviction, culturally relevant norms, role impairment, and safety concerns independently of the label.
- Assess both medical explanations and psychiatric alternatives where indicated; neither endorse a supernatural cause as fact nor equate cultural attribution with psychosis.
- Use language such as “Client attributes [reported experience] to [elicited explanation]”; describe corroboration and unresolved questions separately.
- No independent DSM diagnostic or billing codes are supplied for these ten cultural concepts.
