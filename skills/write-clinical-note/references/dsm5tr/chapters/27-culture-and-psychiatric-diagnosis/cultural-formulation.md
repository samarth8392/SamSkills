# Cultural Formulation and Key Terms

> **Spark note:** Culture shapes every presentation, including familiar DSM syndromes. A formulation connects a particular person's meanings, social position, resources, and care relationships to assessment; ethnicity alone does not supply those findings.

## Framework at a glance
- **Culture:** learned, changing systems of meanings, norms, practices, and institutions; people participate in multiple contexts. Background assumptions may be hard to articulate.
- **Race:** a social classification, not a biological division of humanity. Racism, exclusion, and diagnostic bias have clinical consequences.
- **Ethnicity:** socially constructed community identity linked to shared history, ancestry, place, language, religion, or other features; self-identification and outsider assignment may differ.
- Identity can support belonging and resilience or intersect with inequity and conflict. Assess the individual rather than assigning fixed group characteristics. (pp. 1125–1127)

## Five components of the outline
1. **Identity:** elicited priorities and intersections among age, gender, ethnoracial identity, spirituality, class/caste, migration, occupation, sexuality, and other relevant affiliations. Record language preferences, fluency, interpreter needs, and relationships with origin and current communities.
2. **Concepts of distress:** the person's terms, symptoms of greatest concern, explanations, stigma, expected course, and family/community interpretations. Assess severity against relevant norms and connect these meanings to coping and desired help.
3. **Stressors, vulnerability, resilience:** housing, transportation, education, employment, material resources, racism/discrimination, marginalization, religion, relationships, and local or distant events. Identify concrete emotional, practical, and informational supports.
4. **Care relationship:** language, status, background, values, and power differences involving clinician, team, and institution. Prior oppression or discrimination may affect trust, communication, and engagement.
5. **Synthesis:** state how these findings change the differential, impairment judgment, treatment negotiation, or management. (pp. 1127–1128)

## TR emphasis
The text explicitly identifies an expanded Outline relative to DSM-5. It highlights intersectionality, social determinants, systemic disadvantage, racism, institutional context, and sources of resilience. The CFI has been field-tested for feasibility, acceptability, and usefulness; it does not independently establish a psychiatric diagnosis. (pp. 1127–1129)

## Diagnostic pitfalls
- Do not equate culturally accepted spiritual beliefs with delusions or reasonable vigilance after discrimination with paranoia.
- Apparent cultural similarity between clinician and client does not eliminate differences relevant to assessment.
- A cultural explanation neither proves nor excludes a DSM disorder. Evaluate the full syndrome and contextual impairment.
- A relational, acculturation, or religious/spiritual concern may warrant a Section II focus-of-attention code even without a mental disorder; use that chapter's definitions rather than inventing a code here.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Attribute each finding: client report, named collateral source, observation, or clinician interpretation. Preserve the client's own short terms when clinically useful.
- Describe the specific discrimination or access barrier reported and its reported effects; do not infer an experience from demographic identity.
- Record identified strengths and the client's priorities, not only barriers.
- Use a conditional structure: “Client describes [elicited term] as [meaning]; reports [actual context]. This informs [specific diagnostic/treatment consideration].” Populate only with obtained information.
- State unresolved cultural or language questions, interpreter involvement, and any consultation needed. Do not document unasked domains as negative findings.
