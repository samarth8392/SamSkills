# Bipolar I Disorder

> **Spark note:** One lifetime **manic episode** (≥1 week, or any length if hospitalized, with marked impairment/hospitalization/psychosis) is both necessary and sufficient. Neither psychosis nor a major depressive episode (MDE) is required, though most people with mania also have MDEs. The common error runs the other way: people usually present depressed, so bipolar I gets missed and is called unipolar depression. Always ask about past mania or hypomania, and get collateral history.

Full specifier definitions are in [specifiers.md](specifiers.md).

## Criteria at a glance

### Manic Episode (Criteria A–D) (p. 256)
- **A. Mood + energy, both required:** a distinct period of abnormally and persistently elevated, expansive **or** irritable mood **AND** abnormally and persistently increased goal-directed activity or energy.
  - Duration **≥1 week**, present most of the day, nearly every day. **Any duration counts if hospitalization is necessary.**
- **B. ≥3 of 7 symptoms (≥4 if mood is *only* irritable).** They must occur during the mood/energy period, be present to a significant degree and be a noticeable change from usual behaviour:
  1. Inflated self-esteem / grandiosity
  2. Decreased need for sleep (e.g., feels rested after ~3 h)
  3. More talkative than usual / pressured speech
  4. Flight of ideas or subjectively racing thoughts
  5. Distractibility (attention pulled to irrelevant external stimuli), reported or observed
  6. ↑ goal-directed activity (social, work/school, sexual) **or** psychomotor agitation (purposeless activity)
  7. Excessive involvement in activities with high potential for painful consequences (buying sprees, sexual indiscretions, foolish investments)
- **C. Severity threshold, any one of:** marked impairment in social/occupational functioning **or** hospitalization needed to prevent harm to self/others **or** psychotic features. Psychosis automatically satisfies C.
- **D. Exclusion:** not attributable to the physiological effects of a substance (drug of abuse, medication, other treatment) or another medical condition.
  - **Antidepressant-emergent exception:** a full manic episode that appears during antidepressant treatment (medication, ECT) and **persists at a fully syndromal level beyond the treatment's physiological effect** counts as a manic episode, so the diagnosis is bipolar I.
- At least **1 lifetime manic episode** is required for bipolar I.

### Hypomanic Episode (Criteria A–F) (pp. 256–257); not required for BP-I
- **A.** Same mood + energy requirement as mania, lasting **≥4 consecutive days**, most of the day, nearly every day.
- **B.** The same 7-item list; **≥3 (≥4 if only irritable)**. Symptoms have persisted, are a noticeable change and are present to a significant degree.
- **C.** An unequivocal change in functioning that is uncharacteristic of the person when well.
- **D.** The mood and functional change are **observable by others**.
- **E.** **Not** severe enough to cause marked impairment or require hospitalization. **Any psychosis makes the episode manic by definition.**
- **F.** Not due to a substance or medical condition. The same antidepressant-emergent exception applies, **but** 1–2 symptoms (especially irritability, edginess or agitation after an antidepressant) are not enough, and do not by themselves indicate a bipolar diathesis.

### Major Depressive Episode (Criteria A–C) (pp. 257–258); not required for BP-I
- **A. ≥5 of 9 sx in the same 2-week period**, a change from prior functioning; **≥1 must be (1) depressed mood or (2) anhedonia**. Do not count symptoms clearly due to another medical condition.
  1. Depressed mood most of the day, nearly every day, by self-report or observation. **In children/adolescents it can be irritable mood.**
  2. Markedly diminished interest/pleasure in all or almost all activities, most of the day, nearly every day
  3. Significant weight loss (not dieting) or gain (>5% body weight/month), or appetite ↓/↑ nearly every day. In children: failure to make expected weight gain.
  4. Insomnia or hypersomnia nearly every day
  5. Psychomotor agitation or retardation nearly every day, **observable by others** (not just subjective)
  6. Fatigue / loss of energy nearly every day
  7. Worthlessness or excessive/inappropriate guilt (may be delusional) nearly every day, beyond self-reproach about being ill
  8. ↓ concentration / indecisiveness nearly every day (subjective or observed)
  9. Recurrent thoughts of death (not just fear of dying), SI without a plan, a suicide attempt, or a specific plan
- **B.** Clinically significant distress or impairment.
- **C.** Not attributable to a substance or another medical condition.
- **Loss note:** after a significant loss (bereavement, financial ruin, disaster, serious illness), sadness, rumination, insomnia, poor appetite and weight loss can resemble an MDE. Consider an MDE *in addition to* the normal response, using clinical judgment and cultural norms (see the grief-vs-MDE footnote under Pearls).

### Bipolar I Disorder (p. 259)
- **A.** Criteria met for **≥1 manic episode**.
- **B.** At least one manic episode is **not better explained by schizoaffective disorder** and **not superimposed on** schizophrenia, schizophreniform, delusional disorder, or other specified/unspecified schizophrenia spectrum disorder.
- Hypomanic and major depressive episodes may come before or after the mania but are not required.

## Specifiers & severity
Definitions are in [specifiers.md](specifiers.md).
- **Episode type (required):** current or most recent episode **manic / hypomanic / depressed / unspecified**.
- **Severity** (mild/moderate/severe) and **with psychotic features** are coded **only if full criteria are currently met** for a manic episode or MDE.
  - Manic severity: mild = minimum criteria met; moderate = very significant ↑ activity or impaired judgment; severe = near-continual supervision needed to prevent physical harm.
  - MDE severity: anchored on symptom count beyond threshold, intensity and impairment (see specifiers.md).
- **Remission** (partial/full) is coded **only if full criteria are NOT currently met** for a manic, hypomanic or MDE.
  - Partial: some symptoms remain without full criteria, **or** the symptom-free stretch after the episode is <2 months.
  - Full: no significant signs or symptoms for the past 2 months.
- **Uncoded specifiers (list after the coded ones; they apply to the current or most recent episode):** with anxious distress; with mixed features; with rapid cycling; with melancholic features; with atypical features; with mood-congruent / mood-incongruent psychotic features (manic and/or MDE); with catatonia (**add F06.1**); with peripartum onset; with seasonal pattern.
  - Rapid cycling and seasonal pattern describe the **pattern** of episodes, not a single episode.
- Melancholic and atypical features apply to depressive episodes only.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- **Mood:** euphoric, "on top of the world" or infectiously cheerful. Often **irritable** instead, especially when thwarted or using substances. **Lability** means swings between euphoria, dysphoria and irritability.
- **Children:** silliness or goofiness counts only if it is recurrent, inappropriate to context, beyond developmental expectations, **distinctly up from the child's own baseline**, and accompanied by ↑ activity/energy and Criterion B symptoms.
  - Childhood grandiosity counts when beliefs persist despite clear contrary evidence, the child attempts dangerous feats, and **it is a change from baseline**.
  - Increased activity in kids: many simultaneous tasks, elaborate unrealistic plans, or new developmentally inappropriate sexual preoccupation (not explained by abuse or exposure).
- **Grandiosity** ranges from uncritical self-confidence to delusions (e.g., a special relationship with a famous person). People may take on a novel or an impractical invention with no relevant skill.
- **↓ need for sleep ≠ insomnia.** The person feels rested and energetic on little sleep and may go days without sleeping. This is **often the herald of a manic episode**.
- **Speech:** rapid, loud, hard to interrupt, intrusive; may include jokes, puns, theatricality or singing. In irritable mania it shows as complaints and angry tirades. Severe flight of ideas → disorganized or incoherent speech.
- **Distractibility:** pulled toward the interviewer's clothing, background noise or furnishings, so the person can't follow instructions.
- **Activity:** multiple overlapping new projects, ↑ sexuality, intrusive sociability (contacting strangers or old acquaintances), excessive texts/emails/letters, pacing, several conversations at once. Activity may happen at unusual hours.
- **Recklessness:** spending sprees, giving possessions away, reckless driving, investments, infidelity or indiscriminate sex.
- **Criterion C examples:** financial loss, job loss, school failure, divorce, or hospitalization for exhaustion/hyperthermia or self-injury.
- **Substance/treatment rule:** mania from cocaine/amphetamine intoxication, steroids, L-dopa, antidepressants or stimulants does **not** count. Treatment-emergent mania (meds, ECT, light therapy) that **persists after the agent has cleared** does count.
- **Grief vs MDE footnote (p. 300):**
  - Grief: emptiness and loss dominate, pangs come in waves tied to reminders, humor and positive emotion remain possible, self-esteem is preserved, and death thoughts are about joining the deceased.
  - MDE: persistent depressed mood and inability to anticipate pleasure, self-critical rumination, worthlessness, and suicidal thoughts driven by feeling worthless or unable to cope.

## Associated features
- **Poor insight**; people often vehemently resist treatment.
- Flamboyant or sexually suggestive change in dress; sharpened senses; gambling, antisocial acts.
- Rapid shifts to anger or depression; hostility and threats; when delusional, possible assault or suicide.
- **~35% of manic episodes have depressive symptoms (mixed features)** → poorer outcome, ↑ suicide attempts.
- Trait associations: hyperthymic, depressive, cyclothymic, anxious or irritable temperament; circadian and sleep disturbance; reward sensitivity; creativity.
- A first-degree relative with bipolar disorder → risk **~10-fold**.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
**Prevalence**
- US 12-month prevalence **1.5%** (men 1.6%, women 1.5%).
- Higher in Native Americans; lower in African Americans, Hispanics and Asians/Pacific Islanders (vs non-Hispanic Whites).
- 11-country DSM-IV 12-month prevalence: 0.0–0.6%, higher in high-income countries (Japan was low at 0.01%).
- Lifetime male:female ratio ~1.1:1.

**Development & course**
- Peak onset 20–30 y; US mean 22 y (women 21.5, men 23.0); international median 24.3 y.
- Onset in the 60s–70s happens, but new mania in late mid-life or late life → consider **medical/neurological causes (e.g., frontotemporal NCD) or substances**.
- **>90%** of people with one manic episode have recurrent episodes.
- **~60%** of manic episodes come immediately before an MDE.
- **≥4 episodes in 12 months = rapid cycling**, which is common and linked to poorer outcomes.
- Predominant polarity (one study): 31.3% mania, 21.4% depression, 47.3% none.
- Episode features tend to repeat (e.g., psychotic mania → future psychotic mania). Depressive onset → more depressive episodes and suicidality.
- Mixed features in mania → poorer prognosis, poorer **lithium response**, more suicidal behaviour.

**Risk & prognostic factors**
- *Environmental:*
  - Childhood adversity (early emotional trauma, parental psychopathology, family conflict) → risk, earlier onset, worse course, comorbidity, suicide and psychosis.
  - Recent negative life events → **depressive** relapse. **Goal-attainment events** (marriage, finishing a degree) → **manic** relapse.
  - Cannabis and other substances → worsen mania and are linked to first-onset mania.
  - Being currently married is less common than in the general population.
- *Genetic:*
  - Heritability up to ~90% in twin studies. General-population risk ~1%; first-degree relatives **5–10%**; MZ concordance 40–70%.
  - Polygenic and non-Mendelian. Mania- and depression-proneness may be inherited separately. Shares genetic origin with schizophrenia.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- Symptoms are broadly consistent across cultures. Prevalence of flight of ideas and delusion content (grandiose, persecutory, sexual, religious, somatic) varies.
- Cultures that value reward-seeking have higher prevalence. US cases have earlier onset and more family history than European cases.
- **African Americans with BP-I are at higher risk of misdiagnosis as schizophrenia.** Reasons include under-recognized mood symptoms, cultural mistrust misread as paranoia, delayed care leading to more florid psychosis, and short assessments.
- **Women:**
  - More rapid cycling and mixed states; more depressive symptoms; more lifetime eating disorders; higher lifetime alcohol use disorder than men or than women in general.
  - Premenstrual worsening is linked to a worse course. Perimenopausal disturbance is common.
  - Pregnancy itself doesn't raise episode risk **unless medication is stopped**. The **postpartum period carries strong risk** of mania and depression.
  - "Postpartum psychosis" usually looks like a manic or mixed episode with psychosis and is strongly tied to BP-I.

## Suicide risk
- Lifetime suicide risk is **20–30×** the general population; **5–6%** die by suicide.
- Attempts are more common in women; lethal suicide is more common in men.
- Predictors: **past attempt** and **% of days depressed in the past year**.
- **~Half have an alcohol use disorder**; the combination further raises risk of attempt and death.
- Mixed features → ↑ attempts.

## Functional consequences
- **~30%** have severe impairment in work role functioning; many return to full function between episodes.
- **Functional recovery lags symptomatic recovery**, especially occupationally, leading to lower SES despite equivalent education.
- Cognitive deficits persist even when euthymic.
- Higher self-perceived stigma → lower functioning.
- Quality of life and well-being are reduced.

## Differential diagnosis
- **MDD:**
  - BP-I is often misdiagnosed as MDD because the first episode is often depressive, depression dominates the course, and people seek help for depression.
  - Bipolar clues: family history of BP, onset in early 20s, many past episodes, psychosis, antidepressant non-response, and mania emerging on antidepressants or ECT.
- **BP-II, cyclothymia, other specified BRD:** these include hypomanic periods but **no manic episode ever**.
- **GAD, panic, PTSD and other anxiety disorders:** anxious rumination can be mistaken for racing thoughts, and anxiety-avoidance for impulsivity. Check for **episodicity** (classic BP-I is episodic) and triggers.
- **BRD due to another medical condition:** use this when mania is the direct physiological effect of a medical condition (e.g., Cushing's, MS), based on history, labs or exam.
- **Substance/medication-induced BRD:** a substance (stimulants, PCP) or medication (steroids) is etiologic.
  - People in mania overuse substances, so establish which came first. Sometimes this needs confirmation that mania persists off the substance.
  - Antidepressant-emergent mania that persists beyond the drug effect = **BP-I**.
- **Schizoaffective disorder:** requires delusions/hallucinations for **≥2 weeks without a mood episode**. If psychosis occurs **only** during mood episodes → **BP-I with psychotic features**.
- **ADHD:** persistent, non-episodic, onset **by age 12**. Mania is episodic and usually begins in late adolescence or early adulthood.
- **DMDD:** severe **persistent** irritability in youth. Diagnose BP-I only when there is a distinct episode of the required duration that differs from baseline and comes with the other manic symptoms.
- **Borderline (and other) personality disorders:** lability and impulsivity overlap. BP-I requires a **distinct episode** or a clear increase over baseline.

## Comorbidity
- Comorbidity is the norm; **most have ≥3 disorders**.
- Most common: **anxiety disorders, alcohol use disorder, other SUD, ADHD**. Sociocultural factors shape the pattern (e.g., less SUD where use is culturally prohibited).
- Personality: **borderline**, schizotypal, antisocial. BPD–BP-I overlap may reflect misdiagnosis, shared vulnerability or shared childhood adversity.
- Medical: cardiovascular, autoimmune, OSA, **metabolic syndrome**, migraine, **overweight/obesity** (linked to poor treatment outcome). These are often untreated and **explain much of the shortened life expectancy**.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **Establish the index manic episode (Crit A):**
  - Record onset and end dates, total duration (≥7 days) or hospitalization.
  - Describe the mood quality (elevated/expansive vs irritable only, which needs ≥4 B sx) and the energy/activity change.
  - Note that it was "most of the day, nearly every day".
- **Count B symptoms explicitly:** e.g., "slept 2–3 h/night × 6 nights without fatigue (B2)"; "speech pressured, difficult to interrupt (B3)"; "tangential with rapid topic shifts / flight of ideas (B4)"; "distracted by hallway noise, unable to complete intake (B5)"; "started 3 new businesses this week (B6)"; "spent $8k on credit cards (B7)".
- **Severity (Crit C):** document marked impairment (job loss, arrest, financial loss), the reason for hospitalization (danger to self or others, exhaustion), **or** any psychotic symptoms with their content (congruent vs incongruent).
- **Rule-outs (Crit D / Crit B of BP-I):**
  - Urine drug screen and substance history with timeline relative to onset.
  - Medication review (steroids, stimulants, antidepressants, L-dopa). If an antidepressant was involved, document that the syndrome **persisted beyond its expected physiological effect**.
  - Medical work-up when indicated (thyroid, neuro, especially if onset is late in life).
  - Psychosis outside mood episodes (schizoaffective check).
  - Delirium.
- **Collateral:** family or partner reports of behaviour change, which matter because insight is poor.
- **MSE elements supporting mania:**
  - Appearance: flamboyant or disheveled dress.
  - Behaviour: psychomotor agitation, intrusive or overfamiliar.
  - Speech: rapid, loud, pressured.
  - Mood "fantastic" or irritable; affect expansive and labile.
  - Thought process: flight of ideas, tangential.
  - Thought content: grandiose (± delusional).
  - Perception: any hallucinations.
  - Insight and judgment: poor.
- **Coding elements to record:** current or most recent episode type; severity or psychotic features (if currently full criteria) **or** remission status; each applicable specifier (mixed features, anxious distress with its severity, rapid cycling with the episode count in 12 months, peripartum, seasonal); F06.1 if catatonia.
- **Risk:** suicide screen (SI, past attempts, % time depressed, alcohol use), violence risk, financial or sexual risk behaviours, ability to care for self.
- **If presenting depressed:** document a **screen for prior mania or hypomania** and family history of bipolar disorder.
