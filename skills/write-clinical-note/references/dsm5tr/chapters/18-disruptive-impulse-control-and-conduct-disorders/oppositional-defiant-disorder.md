# Oppositional Defiant Disorder

> **Spark note:** A ≥6-month pattern of angry or irritable mood, argumentative or defiant behavior, or vindictiveness (≥4 of 8 symptoms) that occurs with at least one person **who is not a sibling**. Severity depends on **how many settings** are involved, not on how bad the behavior is. Common errors are diagnosing it when **DMDD** criteria are met (DMDD always takes precedence) and missing it because the child is cooperative in the office.

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** A pattern of angry/irritable mood, argumentative/defiant behavior or vindictiveness lasting **≥6 months**, shown by **≥4 symptoms** from any category below. It must show up in interaction with **≥1 person who is not a sibling** (p. 728).
  - *Angry/Irritable Mood*
    1. Often loses temper.
    2. Often touchy or easily annoyed.
    3. Often angry and resentful.
  - *Argumentative/Defiant Behavior*
    4. Often argues with authority figures (for youth: with adults).
    5. Often actively defies or refuses to comply with authority figures' requests or with rules.
    6. Often deliberately annoys others.
    7. Often blames others for own mistakes or misbehavior.
  - *Vindictiveness*
    8. Spiteful or vindictive **≥2 times in the past 6 months**.
  - **Frequency note** (p. 729):
    - Age **<5 y**: the behavior occurs on **most days** for ≥6 months (except A8).
    - Age **≥5 y**: the behavior occurs **≥1×/week** for ≥6 months (except A8).
    - These are minimums. Also judge whether frequency and intensity exceed the norm for developmental level, gender and culture.
- **B.** Linked to distress in the individual or in others in the immediate social context (family, peers, co-workers), **or** harms social, educational, occupational or other functioning (p. 729).
- **C.** Does not occur **exclusively** during a psychotic, substance use, depressive or bipolar disorder. **DMDD criteria are not met** (p. 729).

## Specifiers & severity
Current severity is graded by **pervasiveness** (p. 729):
| Severity | Anchor |
|---|---|
| Mild | Symptoms in **only one setting** (home, school, work or with peers) |
| Moderate | Some symptoms in **≥2 settings** |
| Severe | Some symptoms in **≥3 settings** |

Home-only symptoms with family members are common. Pervasiveness is the severity marker (p. 729).

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- The behavioral features can appear **without** negative mood. However, people with the angry/irritable mood symptoms usually show the behavioral ones too (p. 729).
- The **home** is the most common single setting. Even home-only cases can be significantly impaired, so assess **multiple settings and relationships** (pp. 729–730).
- Symptoms are most evident with **familiar** adults and peers and **may not appear during the clinical exam** (p. 730).
- Thresholds that separate a disorder from normal behavior (p. 730):
  1. ≥4 symptoms in the past 6 months.
  2. Persistence and frequency beyond age, gender and culture norms.
  3. The symptoms contribute to impairment.
  - *Example:* a preschooler's temper outbursts count only if they occur most days for 6 months, come with ≥3 other symptoms, and drive impairment (e.g. property destruction, being asked to leave preschool).
- Temper loss need not be a tantrum. It can be angry facial expressions, angry words or subjective anger (p. 730).
- People with ODD usually **do not see themselves as angry or defiant**. They justify the behavior as a response to unreasonable demands (p. 730).
- **Hostile parenting and the child's behavior influence each other**, and the causal direction often cannot be untangled. That uncertainty **should not affect** whether the diagnosis is made. In neglectful or institutional settings, target the environment (p. 730).

## Associated features
- The most common co-occurring conditions are **ADHD** and **CD** (p. 730).
- **Increased risk of suicide attempts**, even after controlling for comorbid disorders (p. 730).

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- **Prevalence:** 1–11% across countries, averaging **≈3.3%**. Before adolescence it is more common in boys (**1.59:1**). The male excess is **not consistent** in adolescents or adults (p. 731).
- **Onset:** usually **preschool**, rarely later than early adolescence (p. 731).
- **ODD → CD:** ODD often precedes CD, especially **childhood-onset CD**, but many children with ODD never develop CD (p. 731).
- **Symptom dimensions predict different outcomes** (p. 731):
  - The **defiant, argumentative and vindictive** symptoms carry most of the risk for **CD**.
  - The **angry/irritable** symptoms carry most of the risk for **anxiety and depression**.
- **Adult outcomes:** relationship problems, lower education, workplace stress, persistent ODD, antisocial behavior, impulse-control problems, substance misuse, anxiety and depression (p. 731).
- These behaviors **rise normally in the preschool years and in adolescence**, so compare against developmental norms then. Weekly preschool tantrums are common; **daily tantrums occur in ~10%** (p. 731).
- **Temperamental risk:** high emotional reactivity, low frustration tolerance (p. 731).
- **Environmental risk:** harsh, inconsistent or neglectful parenting, which also works in reverse (symptoms → harsher parenting); a **succession of caregivers**; both **bullying peers and being bullied** (p. 731).
- **Genetic and physiological:** lower heart rate and skin-conductance reactivity, reduced basal cortisol reactivity, prefrontal and amygdala abnormalities. The irritability and anger symptoms **share genetic influence with depression and GAD**. Most studies have not separated ODD from CD (p. 732).

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- **Misdiagnosis or overdiagnosis** is possible in some cultural groups. Social norms affect prevalence and the male predominance (p. 732).
- In middle childhood it is more common in boys in **Western** cultures but **similar across genders in non-Western** cultures (p. 732).
- First-generation **migrants and refugees** may have **lower** risk despite adversity (p. 732).
- Gender differences are few compared with CD. **Harsh parenting is more strongly linked to ODD in girls** (p. 732).

## Suicide risk
- Increased risk of **suicide attempts** independent of comorbidity (p. 730).

## Functional consequences
- When ODD persists across development: frequent conflict with parents, teachers, supervisors, peers and partners, leading to emotional, social, academic and occupational impairment (p. 732).

## Differential diagnosis
- **Conduct disorder:** CD is more severe and includes aggression to people or animals, property destruction, and theft or deceit. ODD includes emotion dysregulation (anger, irritability), which CD criteria do not. ODD's impairment can **equal or exceed** CD's. **Both may be diagnosed** (pp. 732, 746).
- **Adjustment disorder:** a temporal link to a stressor, and symptoms lasting **<6 months after the stressor resolves**, suggest adjustment disorder (pp. 732–733).
- **PTSD:** in children <6, PTSD can first appear as dysregulation, opposition and tantrums. The trauma link and traumatic play are the key clues. In adolescents, **reenactment and risk taking** can look like defiance or conduct problems (p. 733).
- **ADHD:** often comorbid. Add ODD only if the noncompliance is **not limited to** situations that demand sustained effort or attention, or sitting still (p. 733).
- **Depressive or bipolar disorders:** do not diagnose ODD if the symptoms occur **only** during the mood disorder (p. 733).
- **DMDD:** shares chronic irritability and temper outbursts. **If DMDD criteria are met, ODD is not given, even if ODD criteria are met** (p. 733).
- **IED:** also high anger, but IED involves **serious aggression toward others**, which is not part of ODD (p. 733).
- **Intellectual developmental disorder:** diagnose ODD only if the oppositionality **markedly exceeds** that of peers of comparable mental age and IDD severity (p. 733).
- **Language disorder / hearing loss:** failure to follow directions because of impaired comprehension is not ODD (p. 733).
- **Social anxiety disorder:** defiance driven by **fear of negative evaluation** is not ODD (p. 733).

## Comorbidity
- **ADHD:** much higher rates, possibly from shared temperament (p. 733).
- **CD:** ODD often precedes CD, most commonly in the childhood-onset subtype (p. 733).
- **Anxiety and MDD:** driven mainly by the angry/irritable symptoms (p. 733).
- **DMDD:** most people with DMDD meet ODD symptom criteria, but **only DMDD is diagnosed** (p. 734).
- **SUDs** in adolescents and adults. Whether this is independent of CD is unclear (p. 734).

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **Criterion A evidence:** list each endorsed symptom (≥4) with its **frequency** (most days if <5 y; ≥weekly if ≥5 y), the **6-month span** and **who** it occurs with. Name a non-sibling target, e.g. "argues with mother and teacher about chores and assignments ~4×/week since [month]." For A8, document **≥2 spiteful or vindictive acts in 6 months** with examples.
- **Informants:** note parent, teacher and self-report. State plainly when the child was cooperative in session but collateral describes defiance.
- **Severity:** name each setting where symptoms occur (home, school, peers, work) → mild, moderate or severe.
- **Criterion B:** document the impairment or distress (school discipline referrals, family conflict, lost friendships).
- **Rule-outs to document:**
  - DMDD screen: is there persistent irritable mood between outbursts most of the day, nearly every day? Onset <10?
  - Mood episodes: symptoms not confined to a depressive or manic episode.
  - Psychosis and substance use.
  - ADHD-limited noncompliance.
  - Language or hearing problems.
  - Social anxiety.
  - Trauma history.
  - Stressor timeline (adjustment disorder).
- **Session/MSE wording:**
  - "Irritable affect, quick to anger when limits discussed."
  - "Externalizes blame for school suspension to teacher."
  - "Minimizes conflict; describes parental requests as unfair."
  - "Behavior in session cooperative; collateral reports persistent defiance at home."
- **Risk:** note suicide-attempt screening, given the elevated risk.
