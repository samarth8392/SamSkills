# Conduct Disorder

> **Spark note:** A repetitive, persistent pattern of violating others' **basic rights** or **major age-appropriate norms**, with ≥3 of 15 behaviors in the past 12 months across four groups: aggression, property destruction, deceit/theft and serious rule violations. **Onset type** (before vs after age 10) sets the ICD code and predicts the prognosis. **"With limited prosocial emotions"** (callous-unemotional traits) flags the highest-risk group. Common errors are relying on self-report (youth minimize), misreading context-normative or **racism-reactive** behavior as CD, and forgetting that CD **cannot** be diagnosed at ≥18 if ASPD criteria are met.

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** A repetitive, persistent pattern of violating others' basic rights or major age-appropriate norms or rules. Requires **≥3 of the 15 criteria in the past 12 months** from any category, with **≥1 in the past 6 months** (pp. 738–739).
  - **Aggression to people and animals**
    1. Often bullies, threatens or intimidates others (includes online or social-media bullying, p. 742).
    2. Often starts physical fights.
    3. Has used a weapon capable of serious harm (e.g. bat, brick, broken bottle, knife, gun).
    4. Physically cruel to people.
    5. Physically cruel to animals.
    6. Stole while confronting a victim (mugging, purse snatching, extortion, armed robbery).
    7. Forced someone into sexual activity.
  - **Destruction of property**
    8. Deliberate fire setting **intended to cause serious damage**.
    9. Deliberately destroyed others' property by means other than fire.
  - **Deceitfulness or theft**
    10. Broke into someone's house, building or car.
    11. Often lies to get goods or favors or to avoid obligations ("cons").
    12. Stole items of **nontrivial value** without confronting a victim (shoplifting without breaking in, forgery).
  - **Serious violations of rules**
    13. Often stays out at night despite parental prohibition, **starting before age 13**.
    14. Ran away overnight **≥2 times** while living in the parental or surrogate home, **or once** without returning for a long period.
    15. Often truant, **starting before age 13**.
- **B.** Clinically significant impairment in social, academic or occupational functioning (p. 739).
- **C.** If age **≥18**, ASPD criteria are **not** met (p. 739).

## Specifiers & severity
**Onset subtype (required; sets the code)** (pp. 739–740):
| Subtype | Definition |
|---|---|
| Childhood-onset type | **≥1** CD symptom **before age 10** |
| Adolescent-onset type | **No** CD symptom before age 10 |
| Unspecified onset | Criteria met, but not enough information to date the first symptom relative to age 10 |

Subtype profiles (p. 741):
- **Childhood-onset**
  - Usually male, with disturbed peer relationships.
  - Often had **ODD** earlier and usually meets full CD criteria **before puberty**.
  - More aggression toward others; often **ADHD** or other neurodevelopmental problems.
  - **More likely to persist into adulthood.**
- **Adolescent-onset**
  - More **normative peer relationships**, though misbehavior often happens in the company of others.
  - Better adult adjustment.

**With limited prosocial emotions** (pp. 740, 741–742):
- Requires **≥2 of 4** traits, shown **persistently for ≥12 months** across **multiple relationships and settings**.
- The traits must reflect the person's **typical** functioning, not occasional moments.
- **Multiple informants are required**: self-report **plus** people who have known the person for a long time (parents, teachers, co-workers, extended family, peers).
- The 4 traits:
  1. **Lack of remorse or guilt.** Does not feel bad after wrongdoing; remorse shown only when caught or facing punishment does not count. General unconcern about the negative consequences of their actions.
  2. **Callous, lacking empathy.** Indifferent to others' feelings; described as cold. More concerned with effects on self than harm to others.
  3. **Unconcerned about performance.** Indifferent to poor school or work performance, puts in little effort even with clear expectations, and blames others.
  4. **Shallow or deficient affect.** Emotion shown only superficially or insincerely (e.g. turned on and off quickly, contradicted by actions), or used **instrumentally** to manipulate or intimidate.
- These traits correspond to the research construct of **callous-unemotional traits**. Related features include thrill seeking, fearlessness and insensitivity to punishment.
- Seen in a **minority** of people with CD. They are more likely to show **planned, instrumental aggression**, childhood onset and severe ratings, but the specifier can apply to any subtype or severity.
- People with these traits **may not admit them** in interview.

**Current severity** (pp. 740–741):
| Level | Anchor |
|---|---|
| Mild | Few or no problems beyond the diagnostic minimum; relatively minor harm (lying, truancy, staying out after dark without permission, other rule breaking) |
| Moderate | Number of problems and harm between mild and severe (e.g. stealing without confrontation, vandalism) |
| Severe | Many problems beyond the minimum, **or** considerable harm (forced sex, physical cruelty, weapon use, stealing with confrontation, breaking and entering) |

Record order: name + onset subtype (code) + "with limited prosocial emotions" if present + severity. Example: "F91.1 conduct disorder, childhood-onset type, with limited prosocial emotions, severe."
**Caution:** F91.9 is also the code for *unspecified disruptive, impulse-control, and conduct disorder*. Always write out the name.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- **Four groupings** (p. 742):
  - Aggression causing or threatening physical harm (A1–A7).
  - Nonaggressive property loss or damage (A8–A9).
  - Deceit or theft (A10–A12).
  - Serious rule violations (A13–A15).
- The behavior usually occurs **across settings** (home, school, community) (p. 742).
- Youth **minimize** their conduct problems, so rely on **additional informants**. Informants may also know little if supervision was poor or the behavior was concealed (p. 742).
- Violence can include rape, assault and, rarely, homicide (p. 742).
- **Running away (A14):** episodes that are a **direct consequence of physical or sexual abuse do not typically count** (p. 742).
- Rule violations can involve school, parental or workplace rules (p. 742).

## Associated features
- **Hostile attribution bias:** in ambiguous situations, the person misreads others' intent as hostile and then feels the aggression was justified (p. 743).
- **Personality features:**
  - Trait negative emotionality and poor self-control: low frustration tolerance, irritability, temper outbursts, suspiciousness.
  - Insensitivity to punishment, thrill seeking, recklessness.
  - (p. 743)
- **Substance misuse** is common, **particularly in adolescent girls** (p. 743).

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- **Prevalence:** 1-year rates of **2% to >10%** (median **4%**) in the US and other mostly high-income countries. US lifetime: **12.0% of men, 7.1% of women**. Rates rise from childhood to adolescence (p. 743).
- **Adolescent-onset** CD is more often tied to psychosocial stressors, e.g. belonging to an oppressed ethnic group that faces discrimination. **Few** children with impairing CD receive treatment (p. 743).
- **Onset:** as early as preschool, but usually **middle childhood through middle adolescence**. **Rare after age 16**. **ODD is a common precursor** of childhood-onset CD (p. 743).
- **Symptom shift with age:** physical aggression is more common in childhood; nonaggressive symptoms become more common in adolescence (p. 743).
- **Symptom order:** less serious behaviors (lying, shoplifting) tend to come first and more severe ones (rape, theft with confrontation) last. **Early severe behavior predicts a worse prognosis** (pp. 743–744).
- **Course:** variable, and **remits by adulthood in the majority**. Adolescent-onset and mild cases usually adjust adequately (p. 743).
  - **Childhood-onset predicts** adult criminality, persistent conduct problems and substance-related disorders.
  - Later risk includes mood, anxiety, PTSD, impulse-control, psychotic, somatic symptom and substance disorders.
- **Into adulthood:** aggression, deceit and rule violation can show up at work and home, including violence toward co-workers, partners and children. At that point **consider ASPD** (p. 744).
- **Temperamental risk:** difficult, undercontrolled infant temperament; **below-average IQ, especially verbal** (p. 744).
- **Family risk:**
  - Parental rejection or neglect, inconsistent or harsh discipline, physical or sexual abuse, lack of supervision.
  - Early institutional living, frequent caregiver changes, large family size.
  - Parental criminality, familial psychopathology (e.g. SUDs).
  - (pp. 744)
- **Community risk:** peer rejection, delinquent peers, neighborhood disadvantage, exposure to violence. Family and community risks are more common and severe in the **childhood-onset** subtype (p. 744).
- **Migration:**
  - Parental migration is a risk both for children left behind and for those who migrate, through acculturation.
  - First-generation immigrants and refugees often have **fewer** conduct problems.
  - (p. 744)
- **Genetic risk:** genes and environment both contribute, and genes more so for aggressive symptoms. Risk rises with a biological or adoptive parent or sibling with CD. More common in children of biological parents with severe AUD, depressive or bipolar disorders, schizophrenia, ADHD or CD. Family history especially marks **childhood-onset** (p. 744).
- **Physiological markers** (not diagnostic) (p. 744):
  - **Slower resting heart rate**, reliably found and **not characteristic of any other mental disorder**.
  - Reduced autonomic fear conditioning (low skin conductance).
  - Ventral prefrontal–amygdala (frontotemporal-limbic) differences.
- **Course modifiers:** persistence is more likely with **childhood-onset + limited prosocial emotions**, co-occurring **ADHD** and **substance abuse** (p. 744).

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- CD may be **misapplied** where disruptive behavior is near-normative, e.g. very threatening high-crime areas or war zones. Consider the context (p. 745).
- In youth from underserved ethnic and racialized groups, **anger or resistance-based coping in response to racism** may be misdiagnosed as CD by uninformed clinicians (p. 745).
- **Boys and men:** more fighting, stealing, vandalism and school discipline problems (p. 745).
- **Girls and women:** more lying, truancy, running away and prostitution. Both sexes show **relational aggression**; females show much less **physical** aggression (p. 745).

## Suicide risk
- Suicidal thoughts, attempts and deaths occur at **higher-than-expected** rates (p. 745).
- A 10-year Taiwan cohort found more attempts **even after adjusting** for mood, anxiety and substance disorders (pp. 745–746).

## Functional consequences
- **School and work:** suspension or expulsion, work-adjustment problems, legal trouble.
- **Health:** STIs, unplanned pregnancy, injuries from accidents or fights, higher accident rates.
- **Early-onset behaviors:** sex, alcohol, tobacco, illegal drugs and reckless acts.
- **Placement:** may rule out ordinary school or parental or foster home placement.
- **Long term:** raised midlife health risks; frequent justice-system contact.
- **Clinical picture:** a common referral reason, especially in forensic practice; impairment is more severe and chronic than in other clinic-referred children.
- (p. 746)

## Differential diagnosis
- **ODD:** less severe, with no aggression to people or animals, property destruction, or theft and deceit. ODD includes emotion dysregulation (anger, irritability), which CD does not. **Both may be diagnosed** (pp. 745–746).
- **ADHD:** hyperactive and impulsive behavior can be disruptive but does not itself violate norms or rights. **Both are diagnosed if criteria are met** (p. 746).
- **Depressive, bipolar disorders and DMDD:** distinguish by **course**. In CD, substantial conduct problems occur **outside** mood disturbance: they predate the mood onset, or include **premeditated** acts that are not tied to intense arousal. **Both may be diagnosed** (p. 746).
- **IED:** IED aggression is impulsive only, not premeditated and not for a tangible goal, and IED lacks the nonaggressive CD symptoms. Add IED only when the impulsive outbursts **warrant independent clinical attention** (p. 746).
- **Adjustment disorder** (with disturbance of conduct, or mixed emotions and conduct): consider it if conduct problems start with a stressor and do not meet another disorder's criteria (p. 746).
  - The text says adjustment disorder applies when such problems "do not resolve within 6 months" of the stressor ending. This reads oddly; adjustment disorder normally requires symptoms to resolve within 6 months. See *Ambiguity* below.
  - CD requires a **repetitive, persistent** pattern with impairment.
- **ASPD:** CD is not diagnosed in adults (≥18) who meet ASPD criteria. People with antisocial features often meet CD criteria (pp. 739, 747).
- **PTSD** (from the ODD discussion, p. 733): adolescent trauma reenactment and risk taking can be mistaken for conduct problems.
- **Running away directly because of physical or sexual abuse** does not typically qualify for A14; establish the context rather than automatically counting escape from abuse as conduct pathology (p. 742).

> *Ambiguity:* The adjustment-disorder sentence on p. 746 says the problems "do not resolve within 6 months of the termination of the stressor." This conflicts with the adjustment disorder criteria, under which symptoms resolve within 6 months. It is likely a misprint or extraction artifact. In practice, a stressor-linked onset with **resolution** within 6 months favors adjustment disorder, which matches the ODD section (p. 733).

## Comorbidity
- **ADHD** and **ODD** are common, and comorbid presentations predict **worse outcomes** (p. 746).
- **ASPD features** often meet CD criteria (p. 747).
- Also: **specific learning disorder**, anxiety, depressive or bipolar disorders, and substance-related disorders (p. 747).
- **Achievement:** reading and verbal skills often fall below age and IQ expectations. This may justify a **specific learning disorder** or **communication disorder** diagnosis (p. 747).

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **Criterion A:** list each endorsed criterion number with **date or approximate age** and **source**, e.g. "A2 initiates fights (school incident reports ×4 this year); A12 shoplifting (juvenile court record, 8 mo ago); A15 truancy since age 12 (attendance record)." Confirm **≥3 in 12 mo** and **≥1 in 6 mo**.
- **Onset subtype:** document the **earliest symptom and the age it occurred** (e.g. "cruelty to family pet at age 8 per mother") → childhood-onset. If records are inadequate, state "unspecified onset" and why.
- **Limited prosocial emotions:** name the ≥2 traits, the **≥12-month** span, **≥2 settings or relationships**, and **which informants** endorsed them. Note when the youth denies the traits but collateral endorses them.
  - Wording for remorse: "expresses regret only regarding consequences to self."
  - Wording for empathy: "indifferent when victim's injury described."
  - Wording for affect: "affect shallow, rapidly shifts to charm when requesting privileges."
- **Severity:** count symptoms beyond 3 and rate the **harm** (e.g. weapon use → severe).
- **Criterion B:** suspensions, expulsions, legal charges, placement disruptions.
- **Criterion C:** if age ≥18, document the ASPD assessment.
- **Rule-outs to document:**
  - Conduct problems present **outside** mood episodes, including premeditated acts.
  - Trauma, and abuse-driven running away.
  - Stressor timeline.
  - Context-normative behavior (dangerous environment, war zone) and **reaction to discrimination**.
  - ADHD-only disruptiveness.
- **Session/MSE wording:**
  - "Minimizes or denies documented incidents."
  - "Attributes hostile intent to peers in ambiguous scenarios."
  - "Describes thrill seeking; low anticipatory anxiety about consequences."
- **Risk:** screen for suicide, violence toward others, weapon access, substance use and sexual risk. Note child-protection concerns.
