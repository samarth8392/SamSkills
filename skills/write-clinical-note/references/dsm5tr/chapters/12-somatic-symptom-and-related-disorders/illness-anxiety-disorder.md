# Illness Anxiety Disorder

> **Spark note:** Health anxiety about *having or getting* a serious illness, with no or only mild somatic symptoms, lasting ≥6 months, plus excessive checking or maladaptive avoidance. Distress comes from what the person thinks a sensation **means**, not from the sensation itself. It covers about a third of former hypochondriasis; when symptoms are distressing, think SSD. It is not delusional: the person can admit they might not be ill (p. 529).

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** Preoccupation with having or acquiring a serious illness (p. 525).
- **B.** Somatic symptoms absent or only mild. With a medical condition or high risk (e.g., strong family history), preoccupation is clearly excessive/disproportionate (p. 525).
- **C.** High health anxiety; easily alarmed about personal health (p. 525).
- **D.** Excessive health-related behaviors (e.g., repeated body checking) **or** maladaptive avoidance (e.g., avoiding appointments/hospitals) (p. 525).
- **E.** Preoccupation **≥6 months**; the specific feared illness may change (p. 525).
- **F.** Not better explained by SSD, panic disorder, GAD, BDD, OCD or delusional disorder, somatic type (p. 525).

## Specifiers & severity
- **Care-seeking type**: frequent medical care — visits, tests, procedures (p. 526).
- **Care-avoidant type**: medical care rarely used (p. 526). (Minority of cases are too anxious to seek care (p. 527).)
- No severity specifier.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Thorough evaluation fails to find a serious condition explaining the concern (p. 526).
- Any sign present is usually a normal sensation (orthostatic dizziness), benign self-limited dysfunction (transient tinnitus) or non-disease discomfort (belching) (p. 526).
- Most evidence comes from DSM hypochondriasis/health anxiety literature; applicability to IAD is unclear (p. 526).
- Easily alarmed by hearing of someone's illness or health news; unresponsive to reassurance, negative tests or benign course; reassurance may *heighten* anxiety (p. 526).
- Illness becomes central to identity, social talk and response to stress; may lead to invalidism (p. 526).
- Behaviors: self-examination (e.g., throat in the mirror), excessive internet research, reassurance-seeking from family/friends/doctors → family strain; avoidance of sick relatives or of exercise (p. 526).

## Associated features
- Far more often seen in medical than mental health settings; extensive but unsatisfying care; elevated medical and mental health utilization (p. 527).
- Multiple physicians, repeatedly negative tests; paradoxical anxiety increase or iatrogenic complications from testing (p. 527).
- Dissatisfied, feel not taken seriously — sometimes justified, as dismissive/hostile physician responses can lead to **missed real medical conditions** (p. 527).

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- Based on hypochondriasis/health anxiety data: community 1–2-year prevalence **1.3–10%** (U.S., Germany); ambulatory medical 6-month/1-year **2.2–8%**, weighted mean **3%**; specialty clinics ~**1/5** report illness anxiety (p. 527).
- **Similar in men and women** (p. 527).
- Thought to be chronic, episodic and relapsing; onset early/middle adulthood; rare in children; age trends inconsistent (rising with age vs peaking midlife); older adults focus on memory and sensory loss (p. 527).
- **Environmental**: may follow major life stress or a serious but ultimately benign health scare; childhood abuse, serious childhood illness, serious parental illness, or death of an ill parent in childhood predispose (pp. 527–528).
- **Course modifiers**: ~**⅓–½ have a transient form** — less psychiatric comorbidity, more medical comorbidity, milder (p. 528).

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- Diagnose cautiously when disease ideas match widely held cultural beliefs; prevalence similar across countries; phenomenology variation little studied (p. 528).
- Sex: prevalence similar in men and women (p. 527).

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed.

## Functional consequences
- Substantial role impairment, reduced physical function and health-related QoL; disrupts relationships, family life, work (p. 528).

## Differential diagnosis
- **Other medical conditions** (first consideration): neurological, endocrine, occult malignancy, multisystem diseases. Medical illness doesn't exclude IAD if anxiety is clearly disproportionate; transient illness-related preoccupation isn't IAD (p. 528).
- **Adjustment disorders**: health anxiety is a normal response to serious illness; if clinically significant → adjustment disorder; if disproportionate anxiety persists **>6 months**, IAD may apply (p. 528).
- **SSD**: requires distressing/disruptive somatic symptoms; IAD has none or mild (p. 528).
- **GAD**: health is one of many worries (p. 528).
- **Panic disorder**: health worry tied to attacks, acute and episodic; IAD persistent; IAD can include panic attacks triggered by illness concerns (pp. 528–529).
- **OCD**: OCD intrusive thoughts usually about *getting* a disease in the future, plus other obsessions/compulsions; IAD focuses on *having* a disease (pp. 528–529).
- **BDD**: concerns limited to perceived appearance flaw (p. 529).
- **MDD**: no separate IAD diagnosis if illness worry occurs only during MDEs; consider IAD if it persists after remission (p. 529).
- **Psychotic disorders**: IAD ideas are plausible and the person can acknowledge the illness may not exist; somatic delusions are rigid and often bizarre (organ rotting/dead) — schizophrenia, delusional disorder somatic type, MDD with psychotic features (p. 529).

## Comorbidity
- Anxiety disorders (esp. GAD, panic), OCD, depressive disorders; ~**⅔ have ≥1 other major mental disorder**; possible elevated PD risk (p. 529).

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **A/C**: name the feared illness(es) and how they've shifted; document anxiety intensity and triggers (news stories, others' illness).
- **B**: record that somatic symptoms are absent or mild (describe them, e.g., occasional benign palpitations) and medical findings; if a real condition exists, state why concern is disproportionate.
- **D**: quantify checking (frequency), internet research, reassurance-seeking, appointments/tests in past 6–12 months — or document avoidance (skipping screenings, avoiding hospitals/sick relatives). This determines care-seeking vs care-avoidant type and whether full IAD vs OSSRD.
- **E**: document ≥6 months duration.
- **Rule-outs (F)**: SSD (symptom severity), panic, GAD breadth of worry, OCD (other obsessions; future-contamination focus), BDD, MDE timing, psychosis (ability to entertain that illness may be absent).
- **MSE language**: "Preoccupied with fear of having [illness]; acknowledges negative workup reduces worry only briefly"; "Insight partial — can consider that the illness may not be present; no delusional conviction"; "Anxious affect; seeks reassurance repeatedly in session."
- Note the caution about cultural beliefs and avoid dismissive language; flag need for appropriate medical follow-up so real conditions are not missed.
