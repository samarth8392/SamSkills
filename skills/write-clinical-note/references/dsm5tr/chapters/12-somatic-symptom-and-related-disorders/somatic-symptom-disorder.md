# Somatic Symptom Disorder

> **Spark note:** Distressing or life-disrupting somatic symptoms **plus** an excessive cognitive, emotional or behavioral response to them, persisting (typically >6 months). It is defined by positive features, not by the absence of a medical explanation: unexplained symptoms alone are insufficient, and a real disease (MI, diabetes) doesn't rule it out. The suffering is authentic and the symptoms are not feigned (p. 519).

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** ≥1 somatic symptom that is distressing or significantly disrupts daily life (p. 518).
- **B.** Excessive thoughts, feelings or behaviors about the symptoms or related health concerns — **≥1 of 3** (p. 518):
  1. Disproportionate, persistent thoughts about how serious the symptoms are.
  2. Persistently high anxiety about health or symptoms.
  3. Excessive time/energy devoted to symptoms or health concerns.
- **C.** Any single symptom may come and go, but being symptomatic is **persistent (typically >6 months)** (p. 518).
- No explicit exclusion criterion; if a medical condition or high risk (e.g., strong family history) exists, the B response must be excessive for it (p. 519).

## Specifiers & severity
- **With predominant pain** (formerly pain disorder): symptoms mainly involve pain (p. 518).
- **Persistent**: severe symptoms, marked impairment, long duration (>6 months) (p. 519).
- **Severity** (p. 519):
  - **Mild**: 1 B symptom.
  - **Moderate**: ≥2 B symptoms.
  - **Severe**: ≥2 B symptoms **plus** multiple somatic complaints (or one very severe symptom).
- Mild is most prevalent; moderate/severe more impaired; severe + persistent can lead to invalidism (pp. 519–520).

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Usually multiple current symptoms; sometimes one severe symptom, **most commonly pain**. Symptoms may be specific (localized pain) or nonspecific (fatigue), sometimes normal sensations (p. 519).
- Example: disabled by SSD after an uncomplicated MI that itself caused no disability (p. 519).
- Worry about illness very high; catastrophic appraisal; persisting fear despite contrary evidence; in severe cases health concerns become **central to identity and relationships** (p. 519).
- Distress is principally focused on the symptoms; some deny any other source of distress (p. 519).
- **High utilization**, rarely reassuring; doctor shopping; poor response to interventions, new ones may worsen symptoms; unusual sensitivity to medication side effects; feel care has been inadequate (p. 520).

## Associated features
- **Cognitive**: attention focused on symptoms, attributing normal sensations to illness (catastrophizing), illness worry, self-concept of bodily weakness, intolerance of bodily complaints (p. 520).
- **Emotional**: health anxiety, negative affectivity, desperation, demoralization (p. 520).
- **Behavioral**: repeated body checking, repeated help- and reassurance-seeking, avoiding physical activity; most marked in severe/persistent cases (p. 520).
- Reassurance short-lived or felt as not being taken seriously; consultations hard to redirect; mental health referral met with surprise or refusal (p. 520).

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- Expected above DSM-IV somatization disorder (<1%) and below undifferentiated somatoform (~19%); questionnaire studies **6.7–17.4%**; general adult population ~**4–6%** (p. 520).
- Primary care 12-month ~**10–20%**; psychosomatic/functional specialty settings **40–60%** (p. 521).
- Likely higher in women (report more symptoms) (p. 521).
- Danish children 5–7 y: ~1/5 of those with functional complaints had distress/impairment/school absence/help-seeking (p. 521).
- Course likely **chronic and fluctuating**; influenced by symptom number, age, impairment, comorbidity; **less harm avoidance and more cooperativeness → faster remission** (p. 521).
- Children: recurrent abdominal pain, headache, fatigue, nausea; single prominent symptom more common; gather family/school input; parental interpretation shapes distress, testing demands and school absence (p. 521).
- Older adults: multisite pain most common; prevalence stable until 65, may fall afterward; risk of underdiagnosis because pain/fatigue seen as "normal aging" or worry seen as understandable (p. 521).
- **Temperamental**: negative affectivity (neuroticism) independently linked to high symptom count; comorbid anxiety/depression worsen (p. 521).
- **Environmental**: low education/SES, recent stressful or health-related events; early adversity (e.g., childhood sexual abuse) (pp. 521–522).
- **Course modifiers (persistence)**: female, older, less education, low SES, unemployment, sexual abuse/childhood adversity, chronic physical illness or mental disorder (depression, anxiety, PDD, panic), social stress, reinforcing factors (illness benefits). Cognitive: pain sensitization, bodily hypervigilance, attributing sx to illness rather than normal/stress (p. 522).

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- High somatic symptom counts worldwide with similar common symptoms, impairment and help-seeking; illness worry–impairment link is cross-cultural; depression often presents somatically (p. 522).
- Differences driven partly by mental illness stigma; **idioms of distress** — heaviness, "gas," heat in the body, burning in the head; attributions to family/work stress (burnout), suppressed anger, semen loss; **shenjing shuairuo** (weakness of nerves, hot-cold imbalance, fatigue) in China (p. 522).
- Use of traditional/alternative healing and access to care vary; cultural beliefs, insurance, health literacy shape perception (p. 522).
- **Sex/gender**: women report more and more severe symptoms; sexual trauma, IPV, childhood trauma raise symptom expression in both sexes, multiple ACEs especially in women; women more likely to develop persistent SSD; gender not linked to untreated duration or treatment response (pp. 522–523).

## Suicide risk
- Associated with suicidal thoughts and attempts; partly via comorbid depression, but **dysfunctional illness perceptions and symptom severity are independently associated** with suicidal ideation (p. 523).

## Functional consequences
- Marked impairment of health status and high distress; severe cases often **>2 SD below population norms** on health status; worse with multiple/severe symptoms (p. 523).

## Differential diagnosis
- **Other mental disorders**: if criteria for, e.g., panic disorder are met, consider it as alternative or additional; if both met, **diagnose both** (p. 523).
- **Other medical conditions**: unclear etiology isn't sufficient (many with IBS or fibromyalgia don't meet B); established disease doesn't exclude SSD. Pointers toward SSD: analgesic ineffectiveness, psychiatric history, unclear provoking/relieving factors, unremitting persistence, stress (p. 523).
- **PFAOMC**: requires medical condition with psych factors worsening course/treatment; SSD emphasizes the excessive response (pp. 523–524).
- **Panic disorder**: somatic sx and health anxiety come in acute episodes vs persistent in SSD (p. 524).
- **GAD**: many worries, health only one; focus not somatic symptoms (p. 524).
- **Depressive disorders**: require depressed mood (or anhedonia for MDD); some cultures underemphasize mood and present idiomatic somatic sx (e.g., "heavy heart") (p. 524).
- **Illness anxiety disorder**: extensive health worry with no/minimal somatic sx (p. 524).
- **FND**: presenting problem is loss of function vs SSD's distress about symptoms; B features help separate (p. 524).
- **Delusional disorder, somatic type**: SSD beliefs can be firm but not delusional (p. 524).
- **BDD**: concern about perceived appearance defect, not illness (p. 524).
- **OCD**: SSD ideas less intrusive; no anxiety-reducing repetitive behaviors characteristic of OCD (p. 524).
- **Factitious disorder / malingering**: falsified symptoms with intent to deceive vs authentic, non-simulated suffering (pp. 524–525).

## Comorbidity
- Anxiety and depressive disorders each in **up to 50%**; drive impairment and worse QoL (p. 525).
- Also PTSD, OCD; sexual dysfunction in men (p. 525).
- With concurrent medical illness: impairment exceeds what the illness explains; somatization worsens disease/treatment outcomes, adherence, QoL; raises utilization (p. 525).

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **A**: list the somatic symptoms (site, character, frequency), whether medically explained or not, and how they disrupt daily life (missed work, reduced activity).
- **B (count them — drives severity)**:
  - B1: catastrophic interpretations ("convinced headaches mean a tumor despite normal MRI").
  - B2: persistent health anxiety (rating, triggers).
  - B3: time/energy — hours researching, number of appointments/ER visits/specialists in past 6–12 months, body checking, activity avoidance.
- **C**: document symptomatic duration (>6 months) even if specific symptoms changed.
- **Specifiers**: note if pain predominates; whether course is persistent (severe, marked impairment, >6 months); severity with rationale (number of B criteria + number/severity of complaints).
- **Rule-outs**: coordinate with medical providers; note relevant medical diagnoses and that response is disproportionate to them; screen panic, GAD, MDD, IAD, OCD, BDD, psychosis; no evidence of falsification.
- **MSE language**: "Preoccupied with bodily symptoms; repeatedly returns conversation to symptoms despite redirection"; "Expresses that prior evaluations were inadequate"; "Beliefs firmly held but acknowledges possibility of alternative explanation; not delusional"; "Affect anxious/demoralized."
- **Risk**: screen suicidal ideation (illness perceptions and symptom severity independently raise risk).
- Language: frame symptoms as real and distressing; avoid "nothing wrong" or "all in your head" phrasing in notes.
