# Factitious Disorder Imposed on Another

> **Spark note:** Identified deception accompanies falsified signs/symptoms or induced illness in another individual, followed by presentation of that person as ill, impaired or injured. Demonstrate deceptive conduct; an unexplained presentation or difficult encounter alone is insufficient (pp. 538–541).

## Criteria at a glance
- **A:** Falsifying physical/psychological signs or symptoms, or producing injury/disease, in another individual, with identified deception.
- **B:** Presenting the victim to others as ill, impaired or injured.
- **C:** Deception occurs even without obvious external incentives; it is not fully accounted for by those rewards.
- **D:** A different mental disorder, including delusional/other psychotic disorders, does not better explain behavior (p. 538).

## Specifiers & severity
**Single episode** or **recurrent episodes** (≥2 separate falsification/induction events). No formal severity scale (p. 538).

## Coding
| Code | Use |
|---|---|
| F68.A | Factitious Disorder Imposed on Another |

Assign to the perpetrator, never the victim. The victim may receive an appropriate abuse diagnosis; the text cites T74.12X as an example, not a universal completed code. Both imposed-on-self and imposed-on-another diagnoses may apply to one perpetrator (pp. 538–539).

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Focus on objective falsification rather than speculation about motives. Genuine preexisting disease does not exclude added deception.
- Methods include false histories, record/test manipulation, simulated symptoms and deliberate induction; treatment may be sought after actual injury has been induced.
- Deception can occur toward community members online or in person without healthcare contact (p. 539).
- Imposed-on-another presentations include dependents, adults or pets; falsely asserted learning disability may also drive inappropriate interventions (pp. 538–539).

## Associated features
Harm may affect the individual, others, clinicians and community supporters. Time/resources can be misdirected and unnecessary procedures performed. Criminal conduct and mental illness can coexist; neither excludes the other (p. 539).

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- Population prevalence unknown; detection and recording are limited by deception. Nearly 1% estimate comes from general hospital inpatients referred for psychiatric consultation, not the population.
- Usually episodic, beginning in early adulthood; single or continuously persistent presentations less common. Onset may follow hospitalization of self or a dependent; recurrent contact may become lifelong (p. 540).

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
Pooled cases/series are approximately two-thirds women and one-third men; prevalence remains unknown (p. 540).

## Suicide risk
No specific suicide-risk estimate is provided. Deliberately induced injury may nevertheless produce serious harm; assess actual risk independently.

## Functional consequences
Deception/induction risks injury, psychological distress, impairment and excessive clinical intervention; supporters and professionals can also be affected (p. 539).

## Differential diagnosis
- **Avoiding legal responsibility:** lying solely to conceal abuse for self-protection is externally rewarded and insufficient; more extensive deception beyond that purpose may support factitious disorder.
- **SSD/IAD:** excessive illness concern without identified deception.
- **Malingering:** external gain explains reporting; factitious disorder is not fully explained by external reward. Both patterns and changing motives may coexist.
- **FND:** genuine neurological symptoms with positive incompatibility, rather than intentional falsification.
- **Borderline personality disorder/nonsuicidal self-injury:** injury alone is insufficient; factitious disorder requires associated deception.
- **Medical/other mental disease:** authentic disease can coexist; inconsistent presentation alone is not proof (pp. 540–541).

## Comorbidity
True medical and mental conditions can coexist with falsification; do not discard verified diagnoses because deception is identified (p. 541).

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Identify the concrete evidence of falsification or induction, its source and dates; separate verified observations from suspicions and conflicting reports.
- Record how the victim was presented to others; assess obvious rewards and alternative psychiatric explanations.
- Count events supporting single versus recurrent episodes.
- Record verified genuine illness and current harm; avoid inferring motives or dismissing reported suicidality as fictitious without evidence.
- Keep perpetrator diagnosis separate from victim assessment, abuse documentation and protection needs; do not diagnose the victim with factitious disorder.
