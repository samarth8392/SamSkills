# Functional Neurological Symptom Disorder (Conversion Disorder)

> **Spark note:** Motor or sensory symptoms require positive clinical evidence that their pattern is incompatible with recognized neurological disease. Normal tests, unusual symptoms, trauma history, or apparent indifference do not establish this diagnosis; neurological disease may coexist (pp. 529–532).

## Criteria at a glance
- **A:** At least one altered voluntary motor or sensory symptom.
- **B:** Examination findings positively establish incompatibility with recognized neurological or medical disorders.
- **C:** Another medical or mental disorder does not better explain the deficit.
- **D:** Significant distress/functional impairment **or** sufficient concern to warrant medical evaluation (pp. 529–530).

## Specifiers & severity
- Record symptom type, which determines code; mixed presentations have their own code.
- **Acute episode:** <6 months; **persistent:** ≥6 months.
- **With psychological stressor:** name it; otherwise **without psychological stressor**. A stressor is not required. No formal severity scale (p. 530).

## Coding

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Appropriate neurological expertise and the entire clinical picture are needed; no single sign is conclusive (pp. 530–531).
- Positive motor signs include strength returning with contralateral effort (Hoover/hip abductor signs), inconsistent performance across tasks, and tremor entrainment, suppression, or variability with distraction.
- Functional dystonia may start abruptly with fixed ankle inversion, clenched hand, or unilateral neck-muscle contraction.
- Functional seizure clues include prolonged eye closure, preserved awareness with bilateral movement, and events >5 minutes. Combine findings; a normal ictal EEG alone does not exclude every seizure or syncope.
- Speech inconsistency and visual-field/acuity inconsistencies may support sensory presentations (p. 531).

## Associated features
Pain/fatigue and dissociation may coexist. Stress or trauma can be absent in up to half of cases. Apparent indifference and external benefits are nonspecific and cannot establish diagnosis or deception (pp. 531–532).

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- Exact prevalence unknown; persistent symptom incidence estimated 4–12/100,000/year; neurology clinic studies approximately 5%–15% (p. 532).
- Transient or persistent; children may fare better than adolescents/adults.
- Associated factors: emotional instability, childhood adversity, physical injury and neurological disease; about one-fifth with functional seizures also have epilepsy.
- Short duration and agreement with diagnosis predict better outcomes; comorbid disease, maladaptive traits and disability benefits correlate with poorer outcomes, without proving causation or feigning (p. 532).

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
Culturally sanctioned ritual phenomena without distress/disability are not diagnosed. Most presentations are 2–3 times more common in women (pp. 532–533).

## Suicide risk
Studies generally show elevated suicidal thoughts/attempts; reported correlates include harmful alcohol use, childhood maltreatment and severe dissociation (p. 533).

## Functional consequences
Disability can equal that of recognized neurological disorders with comparable symptoms (p. 533).

## Differential diagnosis
- **Neurological disease:** assess thoroughly; reassess progressive symptoms; recognized disease and FND may coexist.
- **SSD:** requires excessive symptom response; FND instead requires positive incompatibility. Diagnose both if supported.
- **Factitious disorder/malingering:** require definite evidence of intentional deception; external rewards favor malingering. Inconsistency used to establish FND is not by itself evidence of deception.
- **Dissociative disorders:** can coexist; diagnose independently when criteria are met.
- **BDD:** appearance concern rather than loss of motor/sensory function.
- **Depression:** core mood syndrome and generalized limb heaviness rather than focal deficit.
- **Panic:** cardiorespiratory/autonomic attack symptoms with retained awareness; functional seizures may include amnesia (pp. 533–534).

## Comorbidity
Panic/other anxiety disorders, depression, SSD, personality disorders and neurological/medical disease are common (p. 534).

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Map A to the actual symptom, onset and trajectory; distinguish client report, collateral and observed examination.
- For B, record the specialist's positive signs and interpretation, rather than merely negative imaging or unexplained symptoms.
- For C, document relevant neurological assessment, coexisting diagnoses and reassessment needs if progressive.
- For D, document function, distress or medical-evaluation indication; specify duration, symptom type and stressor status.
- Assess dissociation and suicidality when indicated. Never infer intentionality from unusual symptoms or secondary benefits.
