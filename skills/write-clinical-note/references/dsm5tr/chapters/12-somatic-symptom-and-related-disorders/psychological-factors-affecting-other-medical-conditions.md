# Psychological Factors Affecting Other Medical Conditions

> **Spark note:** Psychological or behavioral factors demonstrably harm a medical condition's course, treatment or physiological functioning. The direction is psychological factor → medical harm; a distress reaction to illness alone does not establish F54 (pp. 534–537).

## Criteria at a glance
- **A:** A medical symptom or condition, excluding a mental disorder, is present.
- **B:** Psychological/behavioral factors adversely affect it through ≥1 route: close temporal relation to onset/exacerbation/delayed recovery; interference with treatment; an additional established health risk; or influence on pathophysiology that triggers/worsens symptoms or necessitates care.
- **C:** Another mental disorder does not better explain these factors (pp. 534–535).

## Specifiers & severity
- **Mild:** increased medical risk, such as inconsistent adherence.
- **Moderate:** underlying disease worsened.
- **Severe:** medical admission or emergency visit results.
- **Extreme:** severe threat to life, such as ignoring infarction symptoms (p. 535).

## Coding
| Code | Use |
|---|---|
| F54 | Psychological factors affecting other medical conditions; name the medical condition and current severity |

The chapter supplies no separate sequencing rule for F54. Do not invent one (pp. 534–537).

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Requires reasonable evidence of clinically meaningful medical effects, though direct mechanism or causation may be unprovable.
- Factors include coping, distress, interpersonal patterns, symptom denial and adherence behavior. Effects may be acute or chronic.
- Can affect established disease, functional syndromes such as migraine/IBS, or idiopathic symptoms such as pain/fatigue. Examples include anxiety worsening asthma or weight-motivated insulin manipulation (pp. 535–536).

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
Prevalence unclear. Can occur throughout life; caregiver stress may be particularly relevant in older adults. Parent/school history can clarify pediatric presentations (p. 536).

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
Consider illness explanations, communication, access and family roles. Culturally sanctioned traditional/spiritual care should not itself be pathologized; such care may complement medical treatment even when service use differs (p. 536).

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed.

## Functional consequences
Medical illness can worsen, with increased suffering, disability or mortality risk (pp. 535–536).

## Differential diagnosis
- **Mental disorder due to medical condition:** medical physiology causes mental symptoms, reversing the causal direction.
- **Adjustment disorder:** maladaptive response to medical illness as stressor. Bidirectional exacerbation can make distinction difficult.
- **Another full mental disorder:** when it accounts for the factor, its diagnosis plus the medical condition is usually sufficient; F54 applies to psychological traits/behaviors below that diagnostic threshold.
- **SSD:** emphasizes excessive symptom-related thoughts/feelings/behavior; F54 emphasizes harm to a medical condition, without requiring excessive concern.
- **IAD:** emphasizes illness fear with absent/mild bodily symptoms, rather than demonstrated worsening of medical disease (pp. 536–537).

## Comorbidity
A medical condition and a relevant psychological/behavioral syndrome or trait are required (p. 537).

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Identify medical symptom/diagnosis and the specific psychological/behavioral factor; record who supplied each observation.
- Map B to dates of worsening, adherence impact, established risk or physiological effect; distinguish evidence of association from unproven mechanism.
- Record the medical consequence supporting severity, rather than severity of distress alone.
- Document why another mental disorder does not better explain the behavior and why cultural practice or barriers to care are not being mislabeled.
