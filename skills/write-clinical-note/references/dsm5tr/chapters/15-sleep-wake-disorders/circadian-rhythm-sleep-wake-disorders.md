# Circadian Rhythm Sleep-Wake Disorders

> **Spark note:** The problem is **timing**, not the ability to sleep: the body clock is shifted, disorganized, free-running, or mismatched to a work schedule, producing insomnia at the "wrong" sleep time and sleepiness at the "wrong" wake time. The giveaway is that sleep is **normal in quality and length when people sleep on their own clock**. Most common mistake: labeling a night-owl adolescent with insomnia disorder (or a sleepy early-riser elder with depression) without a sleep diary/actigraphy covering weekends.

## Criteria at a glance (apply to all types)
- **A.** Persistent or recurrent sleep disruption mainly due to an altered circadian system **or** misalignment between endogenous rhythm and the schedule required by environment or social/professional demands.
- **B.** Disruption causes **excessive sleepiness, insomnia, or both**.
- **C.** Clinically significant distress or impairment.

## Coding
Use the type-specific code above (G47.20–G47.26; note there is no G47.25 in DSM-5-TR). Jet lag is not a DSM-5 type (ICSD-3 includes it).

## Delayed Sleep Phase Type (G47.21) — pp. 631–633
**Features:** major sleep period delayed, usually **>2 hr** vs. desired times → sleep-onset insomnia, trouble waking, early-day sleepiness. **Normal sleep quality/duration when self-scheduled.**
**Associated:** past or current mental disorders common; extreme prolonged morning confusion (sleep inertia); secondary **insomnia disorder** can develop from repeated attempts to fall asleep early.
**Prevalence:** highest in adolescents/young adults (**3.3%–4.6%**, Norway/Sweden); adults **0.2%–1.7%**. Family history often present.
**Course:** onset typically adolescence/early adulthood; months–years before diagnosis; persistent with exacerbations; severity may lessen with age; relapse common. Exacerbated by school/work schedules requiring early rising; remits if schedule can accommodate. Puberty-related delay is normal — distinguish from the disorder. Familial form persists.
**Risk:** longer intrinsic circadian period, altered light sensitivity (hypersensitive to evening light / hyposensitive to morning light), impaired homeostatic drive; genetics — **CRY1** mutation (~0.6% of population).
**Markers:** history + sleep diary or actigraphy **≥7 days including weekends**; salivary **DLMO** — only 57% of rigorously diagnosed cases show physiological delay; the 43% with earlier DLMO may be more behavioral. Phase markers may be more useful for timing treatment.
**Function:** early-day sleepiness; sleep inertia; impact depends on social/occupational demands.
**Differential:** normal late schedules without distress (adolescents/young adults); insomnia disorder; other CRSWDs; sleepiness from breathing disorders, RLS, medical/mental disorders (PSG may help). The circadian pattern differentiates.
**Comorbidity:** depressive disorders, personality disorders, somatic symptom/illness anxiety disorder, OCD, ADHD, autism spectrum disorder. Comorbid insomnia, RLS, sleep apnea, depressive/bipolar/anxiety disorders worsen symptoms. Overlaps with non-24; sighted non-24 patients often have a DSPT history.

## Advanced Sleep Phase Type (G47.22) — pp. 633–635
**Features:** sleep-wake times several hours early, usually **>2 hr** advance → early-morning insomnia, evening/daytime sleepiness; normal sleep when self-scheduled.
**Familial specifier:** autosomal dominant, persistent, may worsen with age; prevalence unknown.
**Associated:** "morning types"; melatonin and core temperature rhythms **2–4 hr earlier**. Forced late bedtimes + persistent early waking → chronic sleep deprivation. Hypnotics/alcohol for early waking and stimulants for sleepiness → **substance misuse risk**.
**Prevalence:** ~**1%** of middle-aged U.S. adults; higher in older adults.
**Course:** onset usually late adulthood (familial earlier); persistent >3 months; severity follows schedules; remits if schedule accommodates. Unclear whether age-related advance is circadian or homeostatic. Course depends on adherence to light/schedule treatments.
**Risk:** less late-afternoon/evening light and more early-morning light perpetuate advance. Genetics: **PER2** mutation (hypophosphorylation), **CKI** missense mutation; shortened circadian period in familial form.
**Markers:** sleep diary and actigraphy.
**Function:** sleepiness affects cognition, social interaction, safety; wake-promoting agents/sedatives → abuse potential.
**Differential:** behavioral factors (irregular schedule, voluntary early waking, early light) especially in older adults; **other causes of early-morning awakening — insomnia disorder, depressive and bipolar disorders**, medical conditions.
**Comorbidity:** may develop comorbid insomnia disorder.

## Irregular Sleep-Wake Type (G47.23) — pp. 635–636
**Features:** no discernible circadian rhythm; nighttime insomnia and daytime napping; **no major sleep period**; sleep fragmented into **≥3 periods/24 hr**; longest bout usually **2–6 A.M.** and **<4 hr**.
**Associated:** isolation/reclusion (lack of entraining cues); frequent naps reported by patient or caregiver; most associated with **major neurocognitive disorder** and pediatric neurodevelopmental disorders.
**Prevalence:** unknown. **Course:** persistent; variable onset; more common in older adults.
**Risk:** low light exposure and lack of structured daytime activity (hospitalized/institutionalized people; dementia patients get much less bright light); Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, Huntington's; pediatric neurodevelopmental disorders.
**Markers:** detailed history, **caregiver** sleep diary, or actigraphy.
**Function:** insomnia/sleepiness at irregular times; **caregiver sleep disruption** is important.
**Differential:** voluntary irregular schedule and poor sleep hygiene; other medical, mental, or medication causes.
**Comorbidity:** neurodegenerative/neurodevelopmental disorders (major NCD, intellectual developmental disorder, TBI); conditions with social isolation and lack of light/structure.

## Non-24-Hour Sleep-Wake Type (G47.24) — pp. 636–638
**Features:** endogenous rhythm not synchronized to 24-hr light-dark cycle → periods of insomnia/sleepiness alternating with brief asymptomatic periods. Cycle: aligned → progressive sleep-onset insomnia → sleep drifts into daytime → daytime sleepiness.
**Associated:** most common in **blind/visually impaired** people with reduced light perception. Sighted: history of delayed phase, low light exposure, little structured activity; longer sleep duration.
**Prevalence:** ~**50% of blind** individuals (U.S.); rare in sighted.
**Course:** persistent with remissions/exacerbations; onset tied to vision loss; sighted onset adolescence/early adulthood (overlap with DSPT). Adherence to light/schedule treatment drives remission.
**Risk:** decreased light exposure/sensitivity, reduced social and physical cues; social-isolating mental disorders; precipitated by schedule changes (night shift, job loss); hospitalized neurologic/psychiatric patients. Blindness; **TBI**.
**Markers:** history plus **extended** sleep diary or actigraphy; serial melatonin phase measurement.
**Function:** unpredictable sleep timing → difficulty with school/steady employment, social isolation.
**Differential:** **DSPT** (may show several days of progressive delay); **depressive disorders** (similar circadian dysregulation).
**Comorbidity:** blindness; depressive and bipolar disorders with social isolation.

## Shift Work Type (G47.26) — pp. 638–640
**Features:** regularly scheduled (non-overtime) work outside **8 A.M.–6 P.M.** (especially nights) + persistent **sleepiness at work and impaired sleep at home** — both usually required. Resolves on return to day work.
**Prevalence:** ~**5%–10%** of night workers (U.S.; night workers = 16%–20% of workforce); rises with middle age.
**Course:** any age but more common **>50 yr**; worsens if schedule continues; older adults adjust phase similarly but suffer more sleep disruption.
**Risk:** morning-type disposition; need for >8 hr sleep; competing domestic demands (parents of young children); lower risk if able to commit to nocturnal lifestyle. Shift workers more often obese → OSA may worsen symptoms.
**Markers:** history, sleep diary, actigraphy.
**Function:** poor work performance; accidents at work and on the drive home; interpersonal problems. **Bipolar disorder history: vulnerable to mania after missed nights of sleep.**
**Differential:** "normal" shift-work difficulty (depends on severity/distress); symptoms persisting during weeks of day-oriented living → look for **sleep apnea, insomnia, narcolepsy**; **jet lag** (travel history).
**Comorbidity:** alcohol use disorder, other SUDs, depression; GI disorders, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, cancer with prolonged shift work.

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed for these disorders.

## ICSD-3 relationship
ICSD-3 circadian disorders closely parallel DSM-5 but also include **jet lag type**.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **A (circadian basis):** record habitual sleep onset and wake times on **workdays vs. free days** (≥7 days diary/actigraphy including weekends); note that sleep is normal in length/quality when self-scheduled.
- **B:** specify insomnia at desired bedtime, early waking, or sleepiness at desired wake time; note sleep inertia.
- **C:** school tardiness/absences, job problems, missed morning obligations, drowsy driving after night shifts, caregiver burden (irregular type).
- **Type evidence:**
  - DSPT: typical bedtime/wake (e.g., "falls asleep ~3 A.M., sleeps soundly until 11 A.M. when unconstrained"); family history (familial).
  - ASPT: sleepy by early evening, wakes ~3–4 A.M.; family history.
  - Irregular: ≥3 sleep bouts/24 hr, no main sleep period; dementia/neurodevelopmental context; light/activity exposure.
  - Non-24: visual status; diary showing daily drift; cyclical symptom periods.
  - Shift work: shift schedule (hours, rotation), symptoms on work vs. off days, resolution on day schedule.
- **Rule-outs:** voluntary late/irregular schedule without distress; depression/bipolar as cause of early waking or irregular sleep; OSA (especially in shift workers), narcolepsy, insomnia disorder, RLS; substance/medication effects.
- **Bipolar risk:** with shift work or phase disruption, document mood monitoring for hypomanic/manic symptoms after sleep loss.
- **Course specifier** (episodic/persistent/recurrent) with dates.
- MSE phrasing: "alert and bright in late-afternoon session, reports inability to wake for morning classes"; "reports dozing at work after night shifts and only 4 hr fragmented daytime sleep."
