# Sleep-Related Hypoventilation

> **Spark note:** Under-breathing during sleep with **rising CO2** (or, if CO2 isn't measured, persistently low SpO2 not tied to discrete apneas). Usually **comorbid** — COPD, neuromuscular/chest-wall disease, obesity, or CNS depressants (opioids, benzodiazepines, alcohol). Unlike OSA/CSA, desaturation is **sustained** rather than cyclic. For mental-health settings, the actionable point is medication: sedatives and opioids blunt ventilatory drive, and symptoms (morning headache, sleepiness, cognitive dulling, depression) can look psychiatric.

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** PSG shows episodes of decreased respiration with **elevated CO2**. (Note: without objective CO2, persistent low SpO2 unassociated with apneas/hypopneas may indicate hypoventilation.)
- **B.** Not better explained by another current sleep disorder.

## Specifiers & severity
**Subtypes:**
- **Idiopathic hypoventilation** — no identifiable condition; reduced tidal volume and elevated CO2 in sleep.
- **Congenital central alveolar hypoventilation** — rare; **PHOX2B** mutation; perinatal shallow breathing, or cyanosis and apnea during sleep.
- **Comorbid sleep-related hypoventilation** — due to pulmonary disease (interstitial lung disease, COPD), neuromuscular/chest wall disorders (muscular dystrophies, post-polio, cervical cord injury, kyphoscoliosis, ALS), **medications (benzodiazepines, opiates)**, or **obesity** (obesity hypoventilation: BMI **>30** and awake hypercapnia **pCO2 >45** without other cause).

**Severity:** graded by degree of sleep hypoxemia and hypercarbia and end-organ impairment (e.g., right heart failure). **Abnormal blood gases while awake = greater severity.**

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Occurs independently or, **more often, comorbid** with medical/neurological disorders, medication use, or substance use disorder.
- Symptoms are **not required**, but commonly: excessive daytime sleepiness, frequent arousals/awakenings, **morning headaches**, insomnia.
- Mechanisms: "**won't breathe**" (blunted CO2 chemoresponsiveness — idiopathic, CNS depressants) vs. "**can't breathe**" (increased work of breathing or respiratory muscle impairment — lung, chest wall, neuromuscular, obesity).

## Associated features
- Insomnia or sleepiness; orthopnea with diaphragm weakness; morning headache; shallow breathing during sleep; OSA or CSA may coexist.
- Consequences: pulmonary hypertension, cor pulmonale, polycythemia, **neurocognitive dysfunction**, depression; with progression, blood gas abnormalities extend into wakefulness. Arousals or bradytachycardia with episodes. May be absent while awake.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- Idiopathic: very uncommon in adults. Congenital: rare, prevalence unknown. Comorbid: more common.
- Obesity hypoventilation: ~**0.14%–0.6%** of general population; up to **42%** of sleep-clinic referrals with BMI >35.
- Idiopathic: slowly progressive. Comorbid: tracks severity of underlying condition. Complications (pulmonary HTN, cor pulmonale, dysrhythmias, polycythemia, neurocognitive dysfunction, respiratory failure) rise with blood gas severity.
- *Environmental:* CNS depressants — **benzodiazepines, opiates, alcohol** — reduce drive.
- *Genetic/physiological:* idiopathic = blunted CO2 response; comorbid with pulmonary, neuromuscular (ALS, spinal cord injury, diaphragmatic paralysis, myasthenia gravis, Lambert-Eaton, toxic/metabolic myopathies, post-polio, Charcot-Marie-Tooth), chest wall disorders, hypothyroidism; congenital = PHOX2B (autonomic nervous system/neural crest development), blunted hypercapnic response especially in NREM.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
Sex distribution mirrors the comorbid condition (e.g., COPD more in men and older age). Obesity hypoventilation now thought equal between genders, possibly slightly more in women.

## Diagnostic markers
- Gold standard: arterial pCO2 **>55 mmHg** during sleep, or a **≥10 mmHg rise** (to >50 mmHg) vs. awake supine — each lasting **>10 min**. Sleep ABGs impractical; noninvasive CO2 not well validated in adults.
- Surrogate (nonspecific): SpO2 **<90% for >5 min with nadir ≥85%**, or **<90% for ≥30%** of sleep time, without upper-airway obstruction.
- Congenital form: autonomic disorders, Hirschsprung's disease, neural crest tumors, box-shaped face.

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed.

## Functional consequences
Chronic hypercapnia/hypoxemia → pulmonary vasoconstriction → pulmonary hypertension → cor pulmonale; cognitive dysfunction, polycythemia, arrhythmias; hypercapnia further depresses drive → progressive respiratory failure.

## Differential diagnosis
- **Other medical conditions affecting ventilation:** idiopathic form requires excluding lung disease, skeletal malformation, neuromuscular disorders, other medical/neurological disorders, and ventilation-affecting medications. Distinguish from hypoxemia due to lung disease.
- **OSA / CSA:** hypoventilation shows **sustained** desaturation rather than periodic dips and may lack discrete airflow-reduction events; but apneas/hypopneas can coexist. Most with obesity hypoventilation also have OSA.

## Comorbidity
Pulmonary disease (ILD, COPD — smoking-related), neuromuscular/chest wall disorders, obesity, and — "most relevant to the clinician" — **medication use (benzodiazepines, opiates)**. Congenital form with autonomic dysfunction, Hirschsprung's. Coexisting OSA worsens hypoxemia/hypercapnia. Some idiopathic cases may be late-onset congenital.

## ICSD-3 relationship
ICSD-3 has six hypoventilation subtypes. Congenital and idiopathic match DSM-5; ICSD-3's obesity hypoventilation syndrome, medication/substance-related, and medical-disorder-related forms are lumped into DSM-5 "comorbid"; late-onset central hypoventilation with hypothalamic dysfunction is not in DSM-5.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **A:** cite PSG/blood gas data — sleep pCO2, change from awake, duration; or sustained SpO2 <90% data with absence of obstructive events.
- **Subtype evidence:** COPD, neuromuscular disease, BMI and awake pCO2 (obesity hypoventilation), current opioid/benzodiazepine/alcohol use and doses.
- **Symptoms:** morning headaches, daytime sleepiness, insomnia, orthopnea, cognitive slowing, depressive symptoms.
- **Severity:** awake blood-gas abnormalities, right heart failure signs.
- **Medication safety:** document review of CNS depressants and coordination with prescriber/pulmonology before adding sedatives.
- MSE phrasing: "psychomotor slowing and reduced concentration; reports daily morning headaches; on chronic opioid and benzodiazepine therapy."
