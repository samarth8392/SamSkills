# Sleep-Wake Disorders — Chapter Overview

> **Spark note:** Sleep quality, amount or timing produces clinically important distress/impairment; diagnosis requires more than an unwanted sleep pattern. Comorbid medical, psychiatric and sleep conditions are common, so assess several causes together and identify when objective sleep assessment is needed (pp. 587–589). The individual criteria govern exceptions: breathing disorders can sometimes be diagnosed on objective evidence without subjective symptoms (pp. 613, 622, 626).

## What unites the chapter and how disorders differ

| Pattern | Principal discriminator | Minimum threshold |
|---|---|---|
| Insomnia | Difficulty initiating/maintaining sleep or early waking despite opportunity | ≥3 nights/week, ≥3 months |
| Hypersomnolence | Sleepiness despite ≥7-hour main sleep plus lapses, long unrefreshing sleep or inertia | ≥3 times/week, ≥3 months |
| Narcolepsy | Irresistible sleep plus cataplexy, hypocretin deficiency or characteristic REM testing | ≥3 times/week, ≥3 months |
| OSA | Obstructive respiratory events on sleep testing | ≥5/hour plus symptoms, or ≥15/hour regardless; pediatric thresholds lower |
| Central apnea | Reduced/absent respiratory effort | ≥5 central apneas/hour |
| Hypoventilation | Sustained inadequate respiration with elevated CO2 | Objective sleep-related change; no fixed symptom duration |
| Circadian disorders | Sleep-wake timing mismatched to environment/required schedule | Persistent/recurrent pattern; no fixed duration in A–C |
| NREM arousals | Early-night incomplete arousal, poor recall/amnesia | Recurrent; impairment/distress |
| Nightmares | Usually later-night dysphoric story recall, clear alert awakening | Repeated; impairment/distress |
| REM behavior disorder | REM vocal/motor enactment plus requisite testing/history | Repeated; impairment/distress |
| RLS | Urge at rest, relieved by movement, evening/night predominance | ≥3 times/week, ≥3 months |
| Substance/medication-induced | Causal exposure/withdrawal chronology | Prominent, severe, independently important disturbance |

Thresholds above summarize the criteria in the linked notes; they do not replace exclusions (pp. 590–662).

## Sleep-stage and assessment essentials
- Healthy younger adults: approximately REM 20%–25%, N1 5%, N2 50%, N3 20%. N3 predominates early; REM cycles recur around 80–100 minutes and lengthen toward morning. Sleep continuity/depth change with age (pp. 588–589).
- PSG measures stages, respiration and other physiological parameters. Sleep latency, wake after sleep onset and efficiency describe continuity; architecture describes stage amounts/distribution. MSLT samples daytime sleep tendency across repeated nap opportunities (p. 589).
- Insomnia rests primarily on subjective/caregiver report; mismatch with objective data does not negate it. Breathing disorders and REM-behavior differentials commonly require PSG. Diary/actigraphy helps establish adequate opportunity and circadian patterns (pp. 592, 602, 613–614, 632, 654).

## Chapter-level pitfalls and differential logic
- First distinguish insufficient sleep opportunity from inability to sleep despite opportunity. Distinguish fatigue from propensity to fall asleep (pp. 597, 603).
- Normal sleep on a preferred schedule particularly supports **delayed/advanced phase** patterns; do not generalize that feature to every irregular/free-running circadian presentation (pp. 631–638).
- Snoring, gasping and witnessed pauses prompt respiratory assessment even in someone presenting with depression, insomnia or ADHD-like behavior (pp. 614–619).
- NREM events: early, incompletely awake and amnestic. Nightmares: detailed recall and full awakening. RBD: REM enactment, usually later, with alert orientation; medication effects, seizures and OSA can mimic it (pp. 640–655).
- RLS is a sensory urge with the characteristic rest/movement/time-of-day pattern; PLMS alone does not diagnose it (pp. 656–659).
- Comorbid insomnia/hypersomnolence/nightmares deserve an additional diagnosis when they independently warrant attention and are not adequately explained by the coexisting condition (pp. 591, 604, 651).
- RBD may precede synucleinopathy by years; cohort risks do not establish that a particular client has or will develop one (pp. 653–656).
- Insomnia and nightmares have suicide associations; assess the person directly instead of treating population association as client evidence. Teen sleep <8 hours is associated with self-harm and suicidality (pp. 589–590, 597, 650).

## Specifiers and coding conventions
- Insomnia/hypersomnolence/nightmares allow associated mental, medical or other sleep conditions; code relevant conditions immediately after the sleep diagnosis (pp. 590–591, 599–600, 647–648).
- Narcolepsy medical-condition subtypes: underlying condition first (p. 605). Opioid-comorbid CSA: opioid use disorder first when present, then G47.37 (p. 621).
- Substance-induced sleep codes incorporate same-class use-disorder severity; do not separately duplicate that use disorder. Caffeine/tobacco are exceptions to the standard three-column pattern (p. 661).
- Course/severity anchors vary by disorder; nightmare acute ≤1 month differs from hypersomnolence acute <1 month. Preserve exact boundaries in individual notes (pp. 600, 647).

## Notable edition content and source ambiguities
- The chapter explicitly contrasts DSM-IV classification with DSM-5's consolidated insomnia category and differentiated narcolepsy subtypes. This is historical context, not a claim that every change originated in TR (p. 587).
- TR source lists four current narcolepsy codes: G47.411, G47.419, G47.421 and G47.429 (p. 605).
- **Insomnia:** criterion D requires ≥3 months, yet episodic specifier says ≥1 to <3 months; the adjacent note directs shorter presentations to other specified insomnia. Preserve both statements (pp. 590–591).
- **Hypersomnolence:** criterion B requires ≥3 months, yet acute/subacute specifiers include shorter durations. The text does not reconcile this (pp. 599–600).
- **Nonrestorative-only sleep:** insomnia narrative points to other specified sleep-wake disorder (p. 592); formal residual-insomnia section explicitly lists it under other specified insomnia (p. 668). This internal difference is retained in both notes.
- **RBD:** criteria allow suggestive history plus established synucleinopathy, while diagnostic narrative calls that route probable/provisional without PSG (pp. 652–653).
- **Central apnea:** criteria subtype mentions frequent arousal, while narrative says arousal is not required; DSM narrative uses >50% central events versus ICSD comparison's at least 50% (pp. 620, 622, 625).

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
Record actual sleep opportunity/schedule, symptom frequency and course, functional effects, collateral source, relevant substances/medications and medical/psychiatric context. Quote objective findings only when available; make referral/testing needs explicit. Suggested cues throughout this chapter are prompts, never evidence that a client has the feature.
