# Substance/Medication-Induced Sleep Disorder

> **Spark note:** Severe, clinically important insomnia, sleepiness or parasomnia is linked to a substance/medication capable of causing it. Establish chronology and independent clinical importance; routine intoxication/withdrawal sleep symptoms alone are insufficient, and primary sleep problems may coexist (pp. 660–668).

## Criteria at a glance
- **A:** Prominent, severe disturbance of sleep.
- **B:** History/examination/laboratory evidence supports **both** onset during/soon after intoxication or withdrawal, or medication exposure/withdrawal, **and** the agent's capacity to cause it.
- **C:** An independent sleep disorder does not better explain it. Independence is suggested by preceding exposure, recurrence without exposure, or persistence approximately a month after acute withdrawal/severe intoxication.
- **D:** Does not occur only during delirium.
- **E:** Clinically significant distress or impairment.
- Diagnose rather than intoxication/withdrawal alone when sleep symptoms dominate and warrant independent clinical attention (p. 660). The approximately one-month clue is not an absolute cutoff: protracted withdrawal and onset up to four weeks after cessation require context (p. 663).

## Specifiers & severity
- **Insomnia type:** sleep-initiation/maintenance difficulty, repeated awakenings or unrefreshing sleep.
- **Daytime sleepiness type:** predominantly waking sleepiness/fatigue, sometimes prolonged sleep.
- **Parasomnia type:** abnormal behavioral events during sleep.
- **Mixed type:** multiple patterns without one predominating.
- Onset: **during intoxication**, **during withdrawal**, or **after medication use** (initiation, dose/use change or discontinuation). Use only onset states appropriate to the substance class. No formal severity scale (pp. 661–663).

- If use disorder present, name its severity first, then the induced disorder, onset and symptom type; the combined code incorporates use disorder, so do **not** add a separate same-class use-disorder diagnosis.

- Caffeine has one code because caffeine use disorder is not an official DSM-5 category. Tobacco code presumes moderate/severe tobacco use disorder.
- Multiple etiologically significant substances each receive their own entry (pp. 661–663).

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Distinguish primary sleep disturbance from self-medication and from medication side effects. Etiological attribution requires evidence, not merely co-occurrence.
- **Alcohol:** early sedation followed by later-night fragmentation; withdrawal causes REM rebound/vivid dreams. Disrupted sleep can persist months/years. Also worsens sleep-related breathing.
- **Caffeine:** timing/dose matter; late/high intake increases latency/awakenings and reduces sleep time; withdrawal may cause daytime sleepiness.
- **Cannabis:** acute effects vary; chronic tolerance occurs; withdrawal insomnia/unpleasant dreams can persist weeks.
- **Opioids:** acute sleepiness, later tolerance/insomnia; reduced restorative sleep; chronic long-acting agents can cause central apnea.
- **Sedatives/hypnotics:** residual sleepiness, tolerance/rebound and withdrawal may occur; shorter-acting drugs more often cause rebound insomnia. Some agents impair ventilation; receptor agonists can provoke complex parasomnias, especially at higher doses/with other sedatives. The source describes agent differences, so do not assume identical risks across all hypnotics.
- **Stimulants:** intoxication insomnia; withdrawal long sleep/daytime sleepiness. MDMA can cause disturbed sleep acutely and persistent symptoms in frequent users.
- **Tobacco:** insomnia, reduced sleep efficiency, sleepiness, craving-related awakenings and impaired sleep on withdrawal.
- Other implicated medications include autonomic, dopaminergic, cholinergic and serotonergic agents, antihistamines and corticosteroids (pp. 663–665).

## Associated features
Anxiety/depressed mood, irritability, fatigue, reduced concentration and cognitive problems can accompany the sleep disturbance; none independently proves the causal sleep diagnosis (p. 663).

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
No overall prevalence estimate is supplied. Developmental exposure patterns matter: recreational substances in younger people, polypharmacy in older adults and newly prescribed medications in children. Existing vulnerability to insomnia or other sleep disorders may increase risk (pp. 665–666).

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
Same exposure may produce different effects between sexes, partly through metabolic/hepatic differences; no separate diagnostic thresholds (p. 666).

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed in this section.

## Diagnostic markers
PSG/MSLT can quantify sleep disruption, sleepiness, respiration and movements, but substance-related EEG patterns are not specific. Two-week diary/actigraphy and appropriately interpreted drug testing can support assessment (p. 666).

## Functional consequences
Sleep disturbance during/after withdrawal may predict substance relapse, particularly alcohol; monitor sleep quality and daytime sleepiness in recovery (p. 667).

## Differential diagnosis
- **Intoxication/withdrawal alone:** choose induced sleep disorder only when predominant and independently clinically important.
- **Delirium:** no separate induced sleep diagnosis for symptoms confined to delirium.
- **Independent sleep disorder:** symptoms precede exposure or persist in abstinence; prescribed-drug effects usually remit days to weeks after stopping. Persistence >4 weeks prompts considering other causes, while chronic substance effects can last longer.
- **Medical-condition-associated sleep disorder:** timeline distinguishes illness effects from treatment effects. Independent/medical-associated disorder plus significant substance-related exacerbation can justify both diagnoses.
- **Breathing-related sleep disorders:** PSG-confirmed opioid central apnea/hypoventilation has its own chapter diagnosis and coding; do not substitute insomnia/sleepiness phenotype for documented ventilatory disease.
- When etiology cannot be established among substance, illness or independent disturbance, unspecified sleep-wake disorder is appropriate (pp. 664, 667–668).

## Comorbidity
Other sleep disorders may coexist, particularly insomnia, hypersomnolence, central apnea, hypoventilation and circadian shift-work disturbance (p. 668).

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Record agent, dose, formulation, timing, frequency, recent changes, prescribed versus nonprescribed use, intoxication/withdrawal evidence and onset relative to sleep symptoms.
- Map phenotype and independent distress/impairment; document pre-exposure sleep and periods of abstinence.
- State evidence for pharmacological capacity and causal relationship, including alternative medical/sleep disorders and delirium exclusion.
- Establish same-class use-disorder criteria/severity separately before selecting combined code; document its absence when appropriate.
- Record collateral/test source and limitations. Do not recommend abrupt cessation as a diagnostic maneuver; this source's chronology discussion is not an instruction to stop medication.
