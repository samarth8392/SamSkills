# Hypersomnolence Disorder

> **Spark note:** Excessive sleepiness even though the person gets **≥7 hours** of main sleep — shown by repeated daytime sleep lapses, a long (>9 h) unrefreshing main sleep, or marked sleep inertia — ≥3×/week for ≥3 months. The commonest mistake is diagnosing it in someone who is simply **not sleeping enough**; if nocturnal sleep adequacy is in doubt, don't diagnose (try 10–14 days of sleep extension first). Unlike narcolepsy, naps are long and unrefreshing and there is **never cataplexy**.

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** Self-reported excessive sleepiness despite a main sleep period of **≥7 hr**, with **≥1** of:
  1. Recurrent periods of sleep or lapses into sleep within the same day.
  2. Prolonged main sleep **>9 hr/day** that is nonrestorative.
  3. Difficulty being fully awake after abrupt awakening (sleep inertia).
- **B.** Occurs **≥3 times/week** for **≥3 months**.
- **C.** Significant distress or impairment (cognitive, social, occupational, other).
- **D.** Not better explained by / not exclusively during another sleep disorder (narcolepsy, breathing-related, circadian, parasomnia).
- **E.** Not attributable to a substance (drug of abuse, medication).
- **F.** Coexisting mental and medical disorders do not adequately explain the predominant hypersomnolence complaint.

## Specifiers & severity
- **Comorbidity:** with mental disorder (including SUD); with medical condition; with another sleep disorder.
- **Course:** **Acute** <1 month; **Subacute** 1–3 months; **Persistent** >3 months. (Note: B requires ≥3 months — see overview ambiguities.)
- **Severity** (days/week with difficulty maintaining daytime alertness, e.g., irresistible sleep attacks while sedentary, driving, visiting, working):
  - **Mild:** 1–2 days/week
  - **Moderate:** 3–4 days/week
  - **Severe:** 5–7 days/week

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Three symptom domains: **excess quantity** of sleep (long nights, long naps), **sleepiness**, and **sleep inertia** (impaired performance/vigilance after waking).
- Fall asleep quickly; good sleep efficiency (**>90%**). Sleepiness builds gradually rather than as sudden "attacks."
- Unintended sleep in sedentary settings (lectures, reading, TV, long drives); in severe cases in high-attention settings (work, meetings, social events).
- **Automatic behavior**: routine low-complexity actions with little/no recall (e.g., "found myself miles further down the road").
- **Sleep inertia ("sleep drunkenness")** in ~40% — helps distinguish from other causes of sleepiness. Hard to wake, may appear confused, combative, or ataxic; multiple alarms or others needed; lasts minutes to hours; also after naps.
- Two patterns: (a) main sleep **≥9 hr** (extreme up to 20 hr), nonrestorative, hard to wake; or (b) normal 7–9 hr nights plus **long naps (>1 hr)** that don't restore alertness. Most nap nearly daily. Weekend/holiday sleep up to **3 hr longer**.

## Associated features
- ~80% report nonrestorative sleep (nonspecific — also in OSA). Naps long (>1 hr) and unrefreshing; short naps (<30 min) also often unrefreshing.
- May fall asleep in the waiting room.
- Familial subset with autonomic dysfunction: vascular-type headaches, Raynaud's phenomenon, fainting.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- 5%–10% of U.S. sleep-clinic patients with daytime sleepiness complaints receive this diagnosis. ~1% of European/U.S. general population has sleep inertia episodes. M ≈ F.
- Onset late adolescence/early adulthood, mean **17–24 yr**, gradual over weeks to months. Usually persistent and stable without treatment; spontaneous remission **11%–25%** after 5–7 yr. Diagnosed on average **10–15 yr** after first symptoms. Pediatric cases rare. Other sleep disorders (e.g., OSA) can worsen sleepiness.
- *Environmental:* temporarily increased by stress and alcohol (not established precipitants); viral infection preceded/accompanied ~10%; common in months after **TBI**.
- *Genetic:* may be familial, autosomal-dominant.

## Diagnostic markers
- Nocturnal PSG: normal-to-long sleep, short sleep latency, normal-to-increased continuity, normal REM distribution, efficiency >90%.
- MSLT: mean sleep latency typically **<10 min, often ≤8 min**; SOREMPs (REM within 20 min of sleep onset) possible but infrequent. MSLT has poor test-retest reliability and doesn't separate this well from **narcolepsy type 2**.
- 2-week sleep diary; actigraphy more accurate. In a 32-hr ad lib bed-rest protocol, affected people slept >4 hr more than controls.

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed for this disorder (see chapter overview for sleep-related suicide risk).

## Functional consequences
Reduced efficiency, concentration, memory; work and relationship problems; trouble meeting morning obligations (lateness); embarrassing and dangerous unintended sleep (driving, machinery).

## Differential diagnosis
- **Normative long sleeper:** no excess sleepiness, inertia, or automatic behavior when they get their needed sleep; sleep is refreshing.
- **Insufficient nocturnal sleep / behaviorally induced insufficient sleep syndrome:** average <7 hr/night strongly suggests it (U.S. adult weeknight average 6.75 hr); catch-up sleep on free days/vacations. **Don't diagnose** if adequacy of sleep is questionable; a **10–14-day sleep extension trial** clarifies.
- **Narcolepsy:** sleeps 7–8 hr, wakes refreshed, short 15–20-min naps restore alertness; cataplexy, hypnagogic hallucinations, sleep paralysis, fragmented night sleep; MSLT typically **>2 SOREMPs**. Cataplexy **never** occurs in hypersomnolence disorder.
- **Fatigue** in another mental disorder (e.g., GAD) or medical condition (e.g., chronic fatigue syndrome): tiredness not relieved by more sleep and unrelated to sleep quantity/quality.
- **Breathing-related sleep disorders:** loud snoring, breathing pauses, obesity, small airway, large neck, hypertension, heart failure signs; PSG confirms apneas.
- **Circadian rhythm disorders:** time-of-day pattern (DSPT: morning inertia/sleepiness, alert evenings; ASPT: sleepy early evening, alert early morning).
- **Parasomnias:** rarely cause prolonged undisturbed sleep or daytime sleepiness; nightmare disorder that curtails sleep might cause sleepiness.
- **Hypersomnolence due to another mental disorder/medical condition** (e.g., MDE with atypical features; cancers, MS): if it adequately explains the sleepiness, no separate diagnosis; add hypersomnolence disorder only if severity/nature far exceeds what's expected.

## Comorbidity
- Depressive symptoms common, may meet criteria (partly psychosocial consequences).
- **>50%** have ADHD symptoms.
- Risk for substance-related disorders, especially self-medicating with **stimulants**.
- Neurodegenerative: Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, multiple system atrophy.

## ICSD-3 relationship
ICSD-3 lists nine "central disorders of hypersomnolence," including some not in DSM (Kleine-Levin syndrome, hypersomnia due to medical/neurological condition or substance, insufficient sleep syndrome).

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **A (main sleep ≥7 hr):** document usual bedtime, wake time, total sleep — ideally a 2-week diary; record weekday vs. weekend sleep.
- **A1–A3:** number/duration of naps and whether refreshing; daytime dozing settings (TV, meetings, driving); total sleep if >9 hr and still unrefreshed; difficulty waking ("needs multiple alarms," "confused or combative when woken").
- **B:** ≥3×/week, ≥3 months — give dates.
- **Severity:** days/week with difficulty staying alert.
- **Safety:** drowsy-driving incidents, near-misses, machinery use — note counseling given.
- **Rule-outs to document:** sleep restriction (and outcome of sleep-extension trial if done); snoring/apneas; cataplexy (emotion-triggered weakness), sleep paralysis, hypnagogic hallucinations; shift work/circadian pattern; sedating medications, alcohol, cannabis, stimulant withdrawal; depression with atypical features; medical causes (TBI, MS, hypothyroidism workup referenced via medical team).
- **Comorbidity specifier** with code of comorbid condition.
- MSE phrasing: "drowsy, yawning repeatedly, briefly dozed in waiting room"; "slowed responses; reports grogginess persisting hours after waking"; "denies emotion-triggered muscle weakness."
