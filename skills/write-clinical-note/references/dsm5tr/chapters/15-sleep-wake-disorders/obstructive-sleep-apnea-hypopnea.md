# Obstructive Sleep Apnea Hypopnea

> **Spark note:** Repeated upper-airway collapse during sleep, defined by **polysomnography (or out-of-center sleep testing)**: **≥5** obstructive apneas/hypopneas per hour **plus** nocturnal breathing symptoms or daytime sleepiness/fatigue, **or ≥15/hr regardless of symptoms**. It is the most common breathing-related sleep disorder and most cases are undiagnosed. For mental-health clinicians: it masquerades as depression, insomnia, fatigue, or (in kids) ADHD — screen for snoring, witnessed pauses, and obesity/large neck, and refer for a sleep study; you cannot diagnose it from history alone.

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** Either:
  1. PSG evidence of **≥5 obstructive apneas or hypopneas per hour of sleep** **and** ≥1 of:
     - (a) Nocturnal breathing disturbance: snoring, snorting/gasping, or breathing pauses.
     - (b) Daytime sleepiness, fatigue, or unrefreshing sleep despite sufficient sleep opportunity, not better explained by another mental disorder (including sleep disorder) and not due to another medical condition.
  2. PSG evidence of **≥15 obstructive apneas and/or hypopneas per hour**, **regardless of symptoms**.

## Specifiers & severity
Severity by **apnea hypopnea index (AHI)**:
- **Mild:** AHI <15
- **Moderate:** AHI 15–30
- **Severe:** AHI >30

Apnea = total absence of airflow; hypopnea = reduction in airflow. Severity is also informed by desaturation, sleep fragmentation, symptoms, and daytime impairment. Considered **more severe regardless of AHI** when: **>10% of sleep time at SpO2 <90%**, arousal index **>30/hr**, or **N3 <5%** of sleep. Thresholds vary with measurement technique.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Each event: ≥10 s breathing reduction in adults (or **two missed breaths in children**), typically with **≥3% O2 desaturation** and/or EEG arousal.
- **Cardinal symptoms: snoring and daytime sleepiness.**
- Adult diagnosis via PSG or **OCST** (out-of-center sleep testing) plus symptoms; ≥15/hr suffices without symptoms.
- **Children:** obstructive AHI **≥1/hr**, or obstructive hypoventilation with snoring or PSG airflow obstruction. Pediatric PSG may show labored breathing, partial obstructive hypoventilation with cyclic desaturation, hypercapnia, paradoxical breathing.
- Most cases are undiagnosed — attend to disturbed sleep + snoring/pauses and to risk signs: **central obesity, crowded pharyngeal airway, elevated BP**.

## Associated features
- Insomnia complaints (from frequent awakenings).
- Nonspecific: heartburn, **nocturia**, **morning headaches**, dry mouth, erectile dysfunction, reduced libido; difficulty breathing supine.
- Hypertension in **>60%**.
- Awake ABGs usually normal; waking hypoxemia/hypercapnia → think coexisting lung disease or hypoventilation. Imaging: narrowed airway. Cardiac: impaired ventricular function; nocturnal arrhythmias (sinus pauses, ectopy, atrial fibrillation). Severe desaturation → elevated hemoglobin/hematocrit.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
**Prevalence**
- Very common; M:F **2:1 to 4:1**; higher in older adults and certain racial/ethnic groups; rising with obesity.
- U.S.: **13% of men, 6% of women** have AHI ≥15; **14% of men, 5% of women** have AHI >5 plus daytime sleepiness.
- Sex gap narrows with age; postmenopausal females **2.6–3.5×** more likely than premenopausal.
- Children: **1%–4%**, no sex difference prepubertally; higher in obese children.
- Higher among African Americans than non-Latinx Whites; increased prevalence in African Americans, American Indians, Hispanics may relate to obesity linked to social determinants (food insecurity, food deserts, lack of safe places for activity).

**Development & course**
- Peaks: ages **3–8** (tonsils large relative to airway) → declines → second peak in adolescence (obesity) → rises in midlife/menopause; may plateau after 65 though some worsen.
- Insidious onset, gradual progression, persistent course; loud snoring often present for years, since childhood. Weight gain worsens symptoms. Most commonly manifests at **40–60 yr**.
- AHI rises ~2 events/hr over 4–5 yr in adults; greater with age, male sex, higher/increasing BMI. Can resolve with weight loss, especially after bariatric surgery. Children: seasonal variation; improvement with growth.
- **Children:** subtler; snoring is parent-reported; agitated arousals, unusual postures (sleeping on hands and knees); **secondary nocturnal enuresis** should raise suspicion; daytime sleepiness less prominent than adults; mouth breathing, swallowing difficulty, poor articulation. <5 yr: nighttime signs (observed apneas, labored breathing) dominate; >5 yr: daytime sleepiness, impulsivity/hyperactivity, ADHD, learning difficulty, morning headaches. Also delayed growth, failure to thrive, developmental delay.
- **Risk:** obesity and male sex (major); retrognathia/micrognathia; family history; genetic syndromes narrowing the airway (Down, Treacher Collins); adenotonsillar hypertrophy; menopause; endocrine syndromes (acromegaly). **Sedating psychiatric/medical medications can worsen** apnea if not managed carefully.
- Strong genetic basis: ~2× prevalence in first-degree relatives; ~1/3 of AHI variance from shared familial factors.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- Sleepiness/fatigue reported differently across cultures; snoring may be viewed as a sign of health → underdiagnosis.
- Menopause, pregnancy, and **polycystic ovary syndrome** raise risk in females; menopausal transition increases severity. **Women more often report fatigue, low energy, or insomnia rather than sleepiness and may underreport snoring.**

## Diagnostic markers
PSG quantifies respiratory events, desaturation, and continuity; MSLT or maintenance of wakefulness test may document sleepiness.

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed.

## Functional consequences
- >50% with moderate–severe OSA report daytime sleepiness.
- ~2× occupational accidents (snoring + sleepiness); motor vehicle crashes up to **7× higher** with elevated AHI. Be aware of **state reporting requirements**, especially for commercial drivers.
- Lower health-related quality of life (largest effect on "vitality"; severe OSA also affects general health, physical, and social functioning).

## Differential diagnosis
- **Primary snoring:** snoring without symptoms or PSG abnormalities.
- **Asthma / GERD:** may mimic nocturnal gasping/choking.
- **Hypersomnolence disorder, central sleep apnea, sleep-related hypoventilation:** definitive separation requires PSG.
- **Narcolepsy:** cataplexy, sleep-related hallucinations, sleep paralysis; shorter, refreshing, dream-associated naps; multiple SOREMPs on MSLT. **Can co-occur** — narcolepsy doesn't exclude OSA.
- **Insufficient sleep, circadian disorders:** other causes of sleepiness.
- **Central sleep apnea:** apneas from reduced/absent respiratory **effort**; snoring less prominent/absent; seen with CHF (Cheyne-Stokes), neurological disease, opioids.
- **Insomnia disorder:** no snoring or OSA history/signs; but they **coexist** and both may need treatment.
- **Nocturnal panic attacks:** fewer episodes, intense autonomic arousal, no excessive sleepiness, history of daytime panic; PSG lacks apnea/desaturation pattern.
- **Nocturnal asthma:** asthma history; PSG lacks apneas/desaturation; can coexist.
- **ADHD (children):** overlapping inattention, academic problems, hyperactivity, internalizing; snoring, labored breathing, enlarged tonsils point to OSA; they often co-occur, possibly causally.
- **Substance/medication-induced insomnia or hypersomnia:** history and improvement after discontinuation. Alcohol, barbiturates, benzodiazepines, opiates can **exacerbate** OSA — still diagnose OSA if its features are present.

## Comorbidity
- Hypertension, coronary artery disease, heart failure, stroke, diabetes, increased mortality; risk estimates **30%–300%** for moderate–severe OSA. Treating OSA reduces cardiovascular morbidity/mortality. Under-served ethnic/racialized groups may have undetected cardiovascular risk.
- Pulmonary hypertension/right heart failure (cor pulmonale, ankle edema, hepatic congestion) rare → very severe disease or associated hypoventilation/cardiopulmonary disease.
- More common with cerebrovascular disease, Parkinson's.
- **Depression:** up to 1/3 of referrals report depressive symptoms; ~10% moderate–severe scores; AHI correlates with depression severity (possibly stronger in men).

## ICSD-3 relationship
ICSD-3 has 11 sleep-related breathing disorder subtypes, including central sleep apneas, adult and pediatric OSA, and sleep-related hypoventilation disorders.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **Nocturnal symptoms (A1a):** bed-partner report of loud snoring, snorting/gasping, witnessed pauses; awakenings choking.
- **Daytime symptoms (A1b):** sleepiness (dozing while reading/TV/driving), fatigue, unrefreshing sleep despite adequate time in bed; morning headaches, nocturia, dry mouth.
- **Risk signs:** BMI/central obesity, large neck, hypertension, retrognathia, family history; in children enlarged tonsils, mouth breathing, secondary enuresis, unusual sleep postures.
- **Objective data:** cite PSG/OCST date, AHI, nadir SpO2 / % time <90%, arousal index → severity.
- **Referral:** document referral for sleep study when suspected (mental-health clinicians cannot diagnose without PSG/OCST).
- **Medication review:** sedative-hypnotics, benzodiazepines, opioids, alcohol use that may worsen apnea.
- **Safety:** drowsy driving/commercial driver status; reporting obligations considered.
- **Mental-health overlap:** note when depressive or insomnia symptoms may be partly explained by untreated OSA; in women, fatigue/insomnia may be the presentation.
- MSE phrasing: "appears fatigued, yawning; reports nonrestorative sleep and morning headaches"; "partner reports loud snoring with pauses."
