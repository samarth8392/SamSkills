# Restless Legs Syndrome

> **Spark note:** An urge to move the legs follows a rest–movement–evening pattern: rest provokes it, movement relieves it, and evening/night worsens it. Frequent symptoms must cause distress or impairment; periodic limb movements support but do not replace the clinical history (pp. 656–659).

## Criteria at a glance
- **A:** Urge to move legs, commonly with unpleasant sensations, with **all three**: begins/worsens during inactivity; partially/fully relieved by movement; worse in evening/night or restricted to that period.
- **B:** ≥3 times/week for ≥3 months.
- **C:** Clinically significant distress or impairment in important functioning.
- **D:** Not attributable to another mental/medical condition (e.g., cramps, arthritis, edema, ischemia), and not better explained by behavioral/positional discomfort or habitual tapping.
- **E:** Not attributable to drug/medication physiology, including drug-induced akathisia (p. 656).

## Specifiers & severity
No formal severity, remission or course specifiers here. Characterize impact and frequency in prose. Frequency/duration are DSM requirements; ICSD-3 criteria do not require them (pp. 656, 660).

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Sensations include crawling, tingling, burning or itching; arms may also be involved. Repetitive movement attempts to relieve symptoms.
- Evening worsening is independent of time spent inactive; compare similar daytime and evening rest periods.
- Severe cases may no longer show obvious movement relief; evaluate full history rather than treating one current observation as decisive.
- Primarily a history/self-report diagnosis; pediatric sensory description must come from the child, not only the parent. Children may describe a need to move without understanding the word urge (pp. 656–658).

## Associated features
Periodic leg movements in sleep occur in up to 90% when multiple nights are recorded; waking periodic movements, family history and initial dopaminergic response support the diagnosis. Sleep-onset/maintenance difficulty and sleepiness are common (p. 657).

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- With ≥3 weekly episodes and moderate/severe distress, prevalence estimated 1.6% in U.S./Europe; impairing RLS approximately 2%–3% in several studied regions.
- Often begins in second/third decade. Around 40% diagnosed as adults recall symptoms before 20; 20% before 10. Prevalence increases until about 60.
- Earlier/familial onset usually progresses slowly; late onset often progresses faster with aggravating factors.
- Risk: genetic susceptibility/family history, female sex, age and iron deficiency. MEIS1 and other variants implicated; brain iron and dopaminergic/opioidergic pathways are relevant. Serotonergic antidepressants can induce/aggravate symptoms (pp. 657–658).

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
Prevalence varies geographically and by acculturation; reported risk patterns differ between studied Mexican American and non-Latinx White samples. Women affected about twice as often; pregnancy prevalence 2–3 times background, peaks in third trimester and usually improves postpartum. Nulliparous women have similar risk to age-matched men (pp. 657–659).

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed.

## Diagnostic markers
PSG may show prolonged sleep latency, increased arousal and periodic movements; waking immobilization can also provoke symptoms. These are supportive rather than mandatory diagnostic tests (p. 659).

## Functional consequences
Sleep fragmentation and delayed onset reduce sleep time; fatigue/sleepiness, reduced energy, mood changes and occupational/social impairment occur. Children may show behavioral and educational problems (pp. 658–659).

## Differential diagnosis
- **Cramps:** painful muscle contraction without characteristic urge/frequent relief movements.
- **Positional discomfort:** resolves with one position change rather than repeated movement.
- **Arthritis/arthralgia:** joint-localized symptoms; **myalgia:** tenderness/soreness.
- **Ischemia, edema, neuropathy, radiculopathy:** neurological/vascular findings may explain symptoms; may also coexist with genuine RLS.
- **Akathisia:** medication relation, less characteristic evening worsening and periodic limb movements.
- **Habitual tapping/anxiety restlessness, venous disease, eczema, orthopedic disease/myelopathy:** examine sensory, timing and movement-relief pattern rather than assuming all restlessness is RLS.
- Family history, PLMS and response to dopaminergic therapy can clarify uncertain cases but do not alone establish diagnosis (p. 659).

## Comorbidity
Depression, GAD, panic and PTSD; cardiovascular disease is the principal medical association discussed. Other associations include diabetes, neuropathy, Parkinson's disease, MS, migraine, fibromyalgia, obesity, thyroid disease, cancer, OSA and narcolepsy. Iron deficiency, pregnancy and renal failure are common associations; symptoms can improve substantially when these resolve (pp. 659–660).

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Capture the person's own sensory description and map all three A features with comparable daytime/evening rest conditions.
- Record frequency, ≥3-month course and specific sleep/daytime effects.
- For children, distinguish their words from parent-observed movements/impairment.
- Document medical/medication mimics, iron/renal/pregnancy history and any specialist findings; do not label PLMS alone as RLS.
