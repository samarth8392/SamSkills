# Narcolepsy

> **Spark note:** Daily-ish irresistible sleep lapses (≥3×/week for 3 months) plus **one** objective/pathognomonic anchor: **cataplexy**, **low CSF hypocretin-1**, or **short REM latency/positive MSLT**. Type 1 (cataplexy or hypocretin deficiency) is an autoimmune loss of hypocretin neurons; type 2 has neither. Key pitfalls: confusing cataplexy with pseudocataplexy or "weak knees" from anxiety, misreading vivid sleep-onset hallucinations as psychosis, and missing narcolepsy behind OSA or ADHD-like behavior in children.

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** Recurrent irrepressible need to sleep, lapsing into sleep, or napping within the same day — **≥3 times/week over the past 3 months**.
- **B.** **≥1** of:
  1. **Cataplexy**, at least a few times per month, defined as either:
     - (a) long-standing disease: brief (seconds–minutes) sudden **bilateral** loss of muscle tone with **preserved consciousness**, triggered by laughter/joking; or
     - (b) children or within 6 months of onset: spontaneous grimaces or jaw-opening with tongue thrusting, or global hypotonia, **without** obvious emotional trigger.
  2. **Hypocretin deficiency:** CSF hypocretin-1 **≤1/3** of healthy-control values on the same assay, or **≤110 pg/mL** — not in the setting of acute brain injury, inflammation, or infection.
  3. **Sleep study:** nocturnal PSG **REM latency ≤15 min**, **or** MSLT **mean sleep latency ≤8 min with ≥2 SOREMPs**.

## Specifiers & severity
**Subtypes (each separately coded):**
- **Type 1 (NT1)** — B1 (cataplexy) **or** B2 (low hypocretin) met.
- **Type 2 (NT2)** — B3 met, but **no** cataplexy and hypocretin **not low or not measured**.
- **Due to a medical condition** — type 1-like or type 2-like, secondary to another condition (code the medical condition first).

**Severity:**
- **Mild:** naps needed only 1–2×/day; sleep disturbance mild if present; cataplexy (if present) <1×/week.
- **Moderate:** multiple naps daily; moderately disturbed sleep; cataplexy daily or every few days.
- **Severe:** nearly constant sleepiness; often highly disturbed nocturnal sleep (excess movement, vivid dreams); cataplexy drug-resistant with multiple daily attacks.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Typical sequence: sleepiness/increased sleep need first (90%), then cataplexy (within 1 yr in 50%, within 3 yr in 85%). Patients may start as NT2 and be **reclassified to NT1** when cataplexy appears; NT1 via low hypocretin can occur without clear cataplexy.
- Sleepiness worse when sedentary; relieved by **brief 10–20-min naps** (key contrast with hypersomnolence disorder).
- **Cataplexy:** seconds to ≤2 min; bilateral; triggered by positive emotion (laughter, anticipation, surprise) — less often anger/embarrassment; neck/jaw/limbs/whole body → head bobbing, jaw drop, falls; **fully awake and aware**. Not cataplexy: weakness in athletic exertion (physiological) or only after unusual triggers such as stress/anxiety (suggests psychopathology).
- **Static cataplexy:** children (rarely adults) with abrupt onset — continuous hypotonia, gait instability, ptosis, slack jaw, tongue protrusion, grimacing; most common within 6 months of rapid onset.
- NT1 = loss of hypothalamic hypocretin (orexin) neurons; CSF hypocretin <1/3 control (<110 pg/mL) in 85%–90% with cataplexy. Most NT2 have normal/intermediate levels. Hypocretin deficiency alone is sufficient for NT1.
- **PSG + MSLT protocol:** stop psychotropics (per half-life) and ensure adequate regular sleep, ideally **2 weeks** documented by diary or (preferably) actigraphy. Abrupt discontinuation of antidepressants, α-adrenergic agonists, or stimulants — or taking them during testing — alters REM physiology.
- Positive MSLT: mean latency ≤8 min + REM in **≥2 of 5** naps. A nocturnal SOREMP (REM ≤15 min) also meets B3: highly specific for NT1 (95%–97%) but only moderately sensitive (54%–57%).
- **False-positive SOREMPs:** shift work, circadian disorders, severe OSA, medication effects, insufficient sleep.
- MSLT reliability 85%–95% for NT1, poor for NT2 (test-retest may be <50%).
- Normal/intermediate CSF hypocretin in people with cataplexy can fall to undetectable over time.

**Secondary causes:** inherited disorders, tumors, and head trauma are most common; also hypothalamic surgery, MS and ADEM, stroke, encephalitis; ADCA-DN (DNMT1 mutation); cataplexy with sleepiness in Prader-Willi, Niemann-Pick type C, Möbius syndrome, Norrie disease; hypocretin deficiency reported in Parkinson's; NT2-like physiology in myotonic dystrophy and Prader-Willi. Head trauma or CNS infection can transiently lower hypocretin without cell loss.

## Associated features
- Automatic behavior when very sleepy (semi-automatic, hazelike, no memory).
- **Hypnagogic/hypnopompic hallucinations** in 20%–60% — vivid, visual/auditory, sometimes tactile; distinct from ordinary sleep-onset mentation.
- **Sleep paralysis** in 20%–60% (also occasional in normal sleepers, especially with stress or sleep loss).
- Nocturnal: fragmented sleep with brief awakenings, vivid dreams, periodic limb movements, REM sleep behavior disorder; nocturnal eating; obesity common.
- May fall asleep in the waiting room; during cataplexy may slump, slur, droop eyelids. **Reflexes abolished** in whole-body cataplexy (most attacks <10 s) — distinguishes from functional weakness.
- IQ normal; working memory and executive deficits reported.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- NT1: **0.02%–0.05%** of adults worldwide; U.S. incidence 0.74/100,000 person-years; lower in Israel, higher in Japan. NT2 prevalence unknown. Sex roughly equal.
- Peak onset **15–25 yr**; abrupt or progressive. Children with abrupt NT1 onset have highest severity that partly improves over first years; prepubertal abrupt onset may bring obesity and precocious puberty. ~50% diagnosed as adults recall onset in childhood/adolescence (diagnostic delay). Course persistent and lifelong.
- Early symptoms: sleepiness, hypnagogic hallucinations, vivid dreams, RBD. Sleepiness progresses to inability to stay awake by day and sleep well at night without increased 24-hr sleep total. Fairly stable; may fluctuate with pregnancy/stress. **Worsening suggests nonadherence or a new sleep disorder** — notably OSA (~25% of people with narcolepsy).
- Children/adolescents: aggression/behavior problems from sleepiness; school/college workload reduces sleep. Retirement allows napping, reducing stimulant need. Regular schedule helps at all ages.
- *Temperamental:* report needing more sleep than family members.
- *Environmental:* group A strep, influenza (esp. pandemic H1N1 2009), other winter infections, Pandemrix H1N1 vaccination may trigger autoimmunity → narcolepsy months later; head trauma; abrupt sleep-wake changes (job change, stress).
- *Genetic:* MZ concordance 25%–32%; 1%–2% of first-degree relatives (10–40× increase). Strong HLA DQB1\*06:02 association; DQB1\*03:01 raises and DQB1\*05:01, \*06:01, \*06:03 lower risk (small effects); T-cell receptor alpha polymorphisms.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- In one treatment-seeking sample of 1,097, African Americans more often had no/atypical cataplexy (despite low hypocretin) and earlier onset; obesity and OSA (linked to social determinants of health) complicate diagnosis.
- Sleep paralysis may be attributed to supernatural forces (e.g., a spirit sitting on the chest), affecting perceived danger and help-seeking.

## Diagnostic markers
- PSG + MSLT confirms, especially at initial diagnosis before treatment. With clear cataplexy, they confirm NT1; without cataplexy and without (measured) hypocretin deficiency, MSLT is diagnostic of NT2.
- Rule out before MSLT: REM-suppressing antidepressants, sedating drugs, stimulant withdrawal, prior sleep deprivation, shift work, severe depression; **chronic insufficient sleep** is common.
- nSOREMP: ~1% of controls, ~50% sensitive for NT1; only 10%–23% of NT2 with normal hypocretin.
- MSLT positive in 90%–95% NT1 vs 2%–4% of controls/other sleep disorders.
- Other PSG: frequent arousals, ↓ efficiency, ↑ N1; periodic limb movements (~40% of NT1); sleep apnea.
- CSF hypocretin-1: useful in suspected pseudocataplexy, atypical/no cataplexy, treatment-refractory cases; unaffected by meds, sleep deprivation, time of day; uninterpretable in severe concurrent infection, head trauma, coma. CSF cytology/protein/glucose normal.
- HLA DQB1\*06:02: positive in 85%–95% of NT1 (supports autoimmunity) but only 40%–50% of NT2; 12%–38% of general population carry it → helpful for NT1 screening, not for NT2. **No biomarkers for NT2.**

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed for this disorder.

## Functional consequences
Impaired school, driving, work, sustained-attention tasks; avoid jobs dangerous to self (machinery) or others (bus driver, pilot). Treated patients can usually drive, rarely long distances alone. Untreated: social isolation, accidental injury. Relationships suffer as people suppress emotions to avoid cataplexy.

## Differential diagnosis
- **Hypersomnolence disorder (idiopathic hypersomnia):** longer, less disrupted nights; more trouble waking; more continuous (not attack-like) sleepiness; longer unrefreshing naps with little dreaming; no cataplexy; no REM intrusions; MSLT lacks multiple SOREMPs.
- **Sleep deprivation / insufficient sleep:** common in adolescents and shift workers; MSLT can be falsely positive if sleep-deprived or phase-delayed.
- **Sleep apnea:** more common, may coexist (obesity) and hide cataplexy; consider narcolepsy if sleepiness persists after OSA treatment.
- **Insomnia disorder:** narcolepsy patients may blame sleepiness on broken nights, but they fall asleep and return to sleep easily; insomnia doesn't produce this degree of daytime sleepiness.
- **Major depressive disorder:** no cataplexy; MSLT usually normal; subjective–objective sleepiness dissociation. Psychiatric patients: 25% had MSLT latency <8 min but ≥2 SOREMPs were rare.
- **Functional neurological symptom disorder (pseudocataplexy):** long-lasting weakness, unusual triggers, frequent falls, generalized without partial attacks; may report dreaming in MSLT naps without SOREMPs; **reflexes intact** during prolonged attacks; home/lab video helps.
- **ADHD/behavior problems:** pediatric sleepiness → aggression, inattention → misdiagnosis.
- **Atonic seizures:** not usually emotion-triggered; abrupt falls rather than "melting"; other seizure types; EEG signatures.
- **Syncope:** presyncopal dizziness, tunnel vision, auditory change absent in cataplexy.
- **Chorea/PANDAS:** pediatric cataplexy misread, esp. after strep with high ASO; overlapping motor disorder may exist near onset.
- **Schizophrenia:** narcolepsy hallucinations are sleep-related, multisensory, "holistic"; schizophrenia predominantly verbal-auditory. High-dose stimulants can cause persecutory delusions. **With cataplexy present, attribute hallucinations/delusions to narcolepsy first** before diagnosing co-occurring schizophrenia.

## Comorbidity
Obesity (rapid weight gain in young children with sudden onset), bruxism, enuresis, precocious puberty, mood disorders, ADHD; parasomnias (sleepwalking, RBD), OSA, RLS, PLMs. Sudden worsening → consider comorbid sleep apnea. RBD present in ~30% of narcolepsy (see RBD file).

## ICSD-3 relationship
ICSD-3 has NT1 and NT2; secondary forms (G47.421, G47.429) reported as secondary narcolepsy subtypes.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **A:** frequency of irresistible sleep/naps (≥3×/week for 3 months), nap length, and that short naps are refreshing.
- **B1 cataplexy:** describe a typical episode in the client's words — trigger (laughing, joking, surprise), body parts (knees buckle, jaw drops, head nods), duration (seconds), **awareness preserved**, frequency per month. In children, note facial grimacing/tongue thrusting/hypotonia.
- **B2/B3:** cite results — CSF hypocretin value and assay, PSG REM latency, MSLT mean latency and number of SOREMPs; note medication washout and pre-test sleep documentation.
- **Subtype and severity** explicitly; for secondary forms list the medical condition first.
- **REM intrusion symptoms:** hypnagogic/hypnopompic hallucinations (content, modality, insight that they occur at sleep transitions), sleep paralysis.
- **Rule-outs:** insufficient sleep (diary/actigraphy), OSA, shift work, sedating meds/stimulant withdrawal, depression, seizure/syncope history, functional weakness features.
- **Safety:** driving and occupational risk counseling documented.
- **Psychosis caution:** if hallucinations reported, document sleep-wake timing, multisensory quality, retained insight, and presence of cataplexy before considering a psychotic disorder; note stimulant dose if delusions emerge.
- MSE phrasing: "drowsy, briefly dozed during session, alert after short rest"; "observed transient jaw slackening and head drop while laughing, remained responsive"; "describes vivid multisensory images at sleep onset, recognizes them as sleep-related."
