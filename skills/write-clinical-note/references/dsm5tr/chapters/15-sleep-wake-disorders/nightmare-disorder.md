# Nightmare Disorder

> **Spark note:** Repeated, vividly remembered dysphoric dreams produce distress or impairment, with rapid alertness and orientation on awakening. Late-night REM timing and coherent recall distinguish nightmares from confused, amnestic sleep terrors; independent clinical attention is needed when diagnosing alongside PTSD or another disorder (pp. 647–651).

## Criteria at a glance
- **A:** Repeated lengthy, highly dysphoric, well-recalled dreams, commonly involving threats and usually occurring in the second half of the main sleep period.
- **B:** Rapid alertness and orientation on waking.
- **C:** Clinically significant distress or functional impairment.
- **D:** Not physiologically attributable to a substance/medication.
- **E:** Coexisting medical/mental conditions do not sufficiently explain the predominant dream complaint (p. 647).

## Specifiers & severity
- **During sleep onset** when applicable.
- Record **with mental disorder, including substance use disorders**, **with medical condition**, and/or **with another sleep disorder** as relevant.
- **Acute:** ≤1 month; **subacute:** >1 but <6 months; **persistent:** ≥6 months.
- **Mild:** average <1 episode/week; **moderate:** ≥1/week but less than nightly; **severe:** nightly. No minimum frequency threshold is required by the diagnostic criteria (p. 647).

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Dreams are elaborate narratives evoking fear or other negative emotion; posttraumatic dreams need not reproduce the event exactly.
- Mostly REM-related; fragmentation, deprivation, jet lag or REM-altering drugs can move episodes earlier, including sleep onset.
- Dysphoria can persist after full awakening and impede returning to sleep. Some recalled bad dreams do not cause an immediate awakening.
- Sleep-onset nightmares can accompany sleep paralysis; paralysis may also occur independently (p. 648).

## Associated features
Mild autonomic activation may occur. Sustained movements or vocalizations are atypical because normal REM atonia remains; brief vocal/motor termination differs from sustained dream enactment (p. 648).

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- Childhood nightmares approximately 1%–5%; adults approximately 6% at least monthly and 2%–6% weekly. These estimates are symptoms, not necessarily full disorder, and often mix idiopathic/posttraumatic cases (p. 648).
- Typical onset 3–6 years; peak later adolescence/early adulthood; some cases persist lifelong.
- Associations include psychosocial adversity, low income, mood problems, insomnia, sleep-disordered breathing, antidepressants and heavy alcohol use.
- Fragmented/deprived sleep and irregular schedules increase risk; twin evidence suggests inherited susceptibility. Supportive parental responses may protect against chronicity (p. 649).

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
Spiritual/cultural interpretations affect meaning and disclosure. Refugee studies emphasize trauma and coexisting sleep problems; assess whether PTSD explains the complaint or nightmares need independent attention. Adult women report more nightmares than men; this difference was not found in children or elderly people (p. 649).

## Suicide risk
Frequent nightmares correlate with substantially elevated suicidal thoughts/behavior even accounting for mental illness and gender (p. 650).

## Functional consequences
Subjective distress can exceed measurable role impairment. Sleep avoidance/fragmentation may produce sleepiness, concentration problems, anxiety, low mood or irritability; children and parents can both be distressed (p. 650).

## Diagnostic markers
Abrupt REM awakenings, often later at night; autonomic changes can precede awakening. Trauma-related nightmares can arise from N2 sleep. Mild sleep disruption and periodic leg movements may occur; these findings alone do not diagnose nightmare disorder (p. 650).

## Differential diagnosis
- **Sleep terrors:** early N3, confusion, poor recall/amnesia, marked autonomic arousal; nightmares have detailed recall and complete awakening.
- **RBD:** sustained motor/vocal dream enactment and potential injury; assess REM atonia. Both can be diagnosed when nightmares independently warrant attention.
- **Bereavement:** dreams of loss may lead to reflection/insight rather than clinically significant distress.
- **PTSD/acute stress disorder:** trauma-linked dreams may be part of the syndrome; add nightmare disorder only when independently important.
- **Narcolepsy:** prominent excessive sleepiness, with or without cataplexy.
- **Nocturnal seizures:** stereotyped motor activity and repetitive aura-like dream content.
- **Breathing-related disorder:** arousals usually lack remembered nightmares.
- **Nocturnal panic:** wake-like panic without nightmare recall.
- **Sleep-related dissociation:** events occur during EEG-defined wakefulness and may be recalled as dreams.
- **Substance/medication effects:** consider dopaminergic agents, beta blockers/other antihypertensives, stimulants, antidepressants, smoking cessation agents and melatonin; REM rebound after antidepressant/alcohol withdrawal can produce nightmares. Causally induced, clinically significant cases belong under induced sleep disorder (pp. 650–651).

## Comorbidity
PTSD, acute stress, insomnia, RBD, psychotic/mood/anxiety/adjustment/personality disorders and grief; medical associations include cardiac disease, cancer, parkinsonism and pain. Independent clinical attention governs additional diagnosis (p. 651).

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Record episode frequency, duration/course, timing, remembered content and rapid orientation; use client description rather than assuming REM stage.
- Document sleep avoidance, delayed return to sleep, daytime effects and distress; match exact frequency/course to specifiers.
- Ask collateral about sustained enactment, injury, confusion and breathing symptoms.
- Map medication/substance timeline and trauma syndrome; state why nightmares warrant independent attention.
- Assess suicidality directly when indicated; the association is not individual evidence of suicidal intent.
