# Non–Rapid Eye Movement Sleep Arousal Disorders

> **Spark note:** Partial arousals out of deep NREM (slow-wave) sleep in the **first third of the night**: the person walks about blank-faced (sleepwalking) or bolts up screaming in terror (sleep terrors), is hard to wake or console, recalls little or no dream, and has **amnesia** next morning. Frequent in children and usually outgrown. Distinguish from **nightmares** (late night, REM, fully awake, vivid story recall) and **RBD** (REM, older adults, awake and oriented, dream enactment). New-onset adult sleepwalking warrants a search for OSA, seizures, or a **medication (e.g., zolpidem)**.

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** Recurrent episodes of **incomplete awakening**, usually in the **first third** of the major sleep episode, with either:
  1. **Sleepwalking:** repeatedly rising from bed and walking about; blank staring face; relatively unresponsive to others; awakened only with great difficulty.
  2. **Sleep terrors:** recurrent abrupt terror arousals, usually starting with a panicky scream; intense fear and autonomic arousal (mydriasis, tachycardia, rapid breathing, sweating); relatively unresponsive to comforting.
- **B.** No or little dream imagery recalled (e.g., a single visual scene).
- **C.** **Amnesia** for the episodes.
- **D.** Clinically significant distress or impairment.
- **E.** Not attributable to a substance (drug of abuse, medication).
- **F.** Coexisting mental/medical disorders don't explain the episodes.

## Specifiers & severity
- **Sleepwalking type (F51.3)** — specify if: *with sleep-related eating*; *with sleep-related sexual behavior (sexsomnia)*.
- **Sleep terror type (F51.4)**.
- No formal severity scale. "Disorder" and severity depend on **nature/consequences** (injury/violence potential, embarrassment, household distress), not merely frequency.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Episodes typically **1–10 min**, may last up to 1 hr; eyes usually **open**. Represent simultaneous wakefulness + NREM sleep.
- **Sleepwalking:** arises from any NREM stage, most often slow-wave sleep; reduced alertness, blank stare; limited recall if awakened or next morning; brief confusion then full recovery.
- **Sleep terrors:** abrupt sit-up screaming/crying, frightened face, autonomic storm; inconsolable; no or fragmentary single-image recall; may last longer in children. Usually one per night; rarely in daytime naps.

## Associated features
- Sleepwalking progresses from sitting up/picking at sheets to leaving the bed, room, or building; using bathroom, eating, talking; running or frantic escape. Mostly routine, low-complexity; unlocking doors and even **driving** reported; inappropriate urination (closet, wastebasket). Several minutes to half an hour. Sleep is relatively analgesic → injuries unnoticed until waking.
- **Sleep-related eating:** recurrent unwanted eating with variable amnesia (none to full awareness without control); inappropriate foods or nonfood items; evidence found next morning. More common in **women**.
- **Sexsomnia:** masturbation, fondling, groping, intercourse without conscious awareness; more common in **males**; relationship and **medicolegal** consequences.
- Sleep terrors: overwhelming dread, compulsion to escape; no storylike dream sequence; usually return to sleep without full awakening.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- Isolated episodes very common. 10%–30% of children have ≥1 sleepwalking episode; 12-month childhood sleepwalking ~5%. Episodes: 12%–14.5% of Canadian children; 1.0%–7.0% of UK adults; weekly–monthly in 0.5%–0.7% of adults. Lifetime 6.9%–29.2%; past-year adult 1.5%–3.6%.
- Sleep terror disorder prevalence unknown; episodes 34.4%–36.9% at 18 months, 19.7% at 30 months, 2.2% in adults.
- Diminish with age; **50%–65% remit** by adolescence; ages 10–18: sleepwalking 1.1%, sleep terrors 0.6%.
- Older children/adults recall more fearful imagery than young children.
- *Environmental:* **sedatives, sleep deprivation**, schedule disruption, fatigue, physical/emotional stress; **fever**.
- *Genetic:* family history in up to **80%** of sleepwalkers; up to **60%** of offspring if both parents affected; up to 10-fold increase in first-degree relatives for sleep terrors; MZ > DZ.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
Sleep-related eating more common in women; sleepwalking more often girls (childhood) and men (adulthood); sleep terrors more common in boys, equal in adults; sexsomnia more in males.

## Diagnostic markers
- Arise most from slow-wave sleep, first third of night; rare in naps. PSG often obscured by movement artifact; EEG may show rhythmic delta persisting into arousal, or theta/alpha/mixed activity; frequent slow/mixed-frequency arousals from SWS. **No spatiotemporal evolution** of EEG rhythms (vs. seizures).
- Video-PSG can document; no reliable marker unless an event is captured (sleep deprivation increases capture). SWS instability overlaps too much with controls.
- Sleep terrors begin **without anticipatory autonomic changes** (unlike REM nightmares), then heart rate doubles or triples.

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed.

## Functional consequences
Embarrassment, social isolation, occupational difficulty; uncommonly serious injury to self or to someone trying to console (only those nearby — **people are not "sought out"**). Sleep-related eating: poor diabetes control, weight gain, cuts/burns, toxic ingestions. Rare violent behavior with **forensic** implications.

## Differential diagnosis
- **Nightmare disorder:** easy, complete awakening; vivid storylike recall; later in night; REM. Parents may mislabel fragmentary terror imagery as nightmares.
- **Breathing-related sleep disorders:** confusional arousals with amnesia plus snoring, pauses, sleepiness; OSA may **trigger** sleepwalking.
- **REM sleep behavior disorder:** REM; easily awakened; detailed vivid dream recall; "acting out dreams."
- **Parasomnia overlap syndrome:** features of both sleepwalking and RBD.
- **Sleep-related seizures:** stereotyped, multiple per night, more likely from naps, can arise from wakefulness; may coexist; recurrent = epilepsy.
- **Alcohol-induced blackouts:** complex behavior with isolated memory loss during drinking; can be indistinguishable by history.
- **Dissociative amnesia with dissociative fugue:** arises from **wakefulness** during the sleep period; history of recurrent childhood physical/sexual abuse usually present.
- **Malingering/voluntary behavior:** occurs during wakefulness.
- **Panic disorder:** abrupt awakening from deep NREM with fear, but rapid complete awakening without confusion, amnesia, or motor activity.
- **Medication-induced complex behaviors:** benzodiazepines, nonbenzodiazepine sedative-hypnotics (Z-drugs), opiates, cocaine, nicotine, antipsychotics/dopamine blockers, TCAs, chloral hydrate (use or withdrawal) → diagnose **substance/medication-induced sleep disorder, parasomnia type**.
- **Night eating syndrome:** circadian meal-timing abnormality; awake overeating after normal sleep onset — not incomplete arousal.

## Comorbidity
Usually no significant mental disorder. Adults: association of sleepwalking with major depressive episodes and OCD. Sleep terrors: elevated depression/anxiety scores on inventories.

## ICSD-3 relationship
ICSD-3 NREM arousal disorders = confusional arousals, sleep terrors, sleepwalking.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **A:** collateral (parent/bed partner) description — time of night (first 1–3 hr after sleep onset), behaviors (walking, eating, leaving house, screaming), eyes open, blank stare, unresponsiveness, difficulty waking, duration.
- **B/C:** client recalls no dream or only a single image; amnesia next morning.
- **D:** injuries, safety incidents, embarrassment (sleepovers, travel, shared rooms), household distress.
- **Subtype specifier:** eating (what, nonfood items, cooking hazards), sexual behavior (partner report; note medicolegal sensitivity).
- **Precipitants:** sleep deprivation, stress, fever, alcohol, sedative-hypnotic use.
- **Rule-outs:** Z-drug/benzodiazepine or other medication temporally linked (→ parasomnia type); seizures (stereotypy, multiple nightly, daytime events); OSA symptoms; alcohol use the same night; dissociation/trauma history; nightmares (vivid recall) vs. terrors; panic.
- **Safety plan:** locked doors/windows, removing hazards, alarm — document counseling.
- MSE phrasing: "no recall of nocturnal episodes; parent reports child screaming, sweating and unconsolable ~1 hr after sleep onset."
