# Selective Mutism

> **Spark note:** The child **can** speak, and usually does at home with immediate family. They consistently **don't** speak where speech is expected (school, with peers, with relatives). It is an anxiety disorder that nearly always comes with social anxiety disorder. The key mistakes are diagnosing it during the normal first month of school or in a child who is still learning the language.

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** Consistent failure to speak in specific social situations where speaking is expected (e.g., school), even though the child speaks in other situations.
- **B.** Interferes with educational or occupational achievement, or with social communication.
- **C.** Lasts **≥1 month**, and the month is **not limited to the first month of school**.
- **D.** Not due to lack of knowledge of, or comfort with, the language required.
- **E.** Not better explained by a communication disorder (e.g., childhood-onset fluency disorder), and not occurring **exclusively** during ASD, schizophrenia or another psychotic disorder.

## Specifiers & severity
- No subtypes. "With panic attacks" can be added if applicable. Use the dimensional severity scale.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- The child does not initiate speech or respond reciprocally, with either children or adults.
- Speaks at home with immediate family, but often not even with close friends or second-degree relatives (grandparents, cousins).
- Most often marked by **high social anxiety**.
- Teachers have trouble assessing skills such as reading aloud.
- Nonverbal communication (grunting, pointing, writing) is often used. The child may happily take part when speech isn't required (e.g., a nonspeaking role in a school play).

## Associated features
- Excessive shyness, fear of social embarrassment, isolation and withdrawal, clinging, compulsive traits, negativism, temper tantrums, mild oppositionality.
- Language skills are usually normal. An occasional comorbid communication disorder is not tied to any particular type, and anxiety is still present.
- In clinical settings, an **additional anxiety disorder is almost always diagnosed**, most often social anxiety disorder.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- **Prevalence:**
  - Rare. Point prevalence **0.03%–1.9%** in clinic and school samples (US, Europe, Israel); not studied in epidemiological surveys.
  - Sex ratio roughly equal, with some evidence of more girls. No variation by race/ethnicity.
  - **Higher risk when the child must speak a non-native language** (e.g., immigrant families).
- **Course:**
  - Onset usually **before age 5**. Often first noticed at school entry, when social demands and reading aloud increase.
  - Persistence varies; the longitudinal course is largely unknown. Mutism often fades, but **social anxiety symptoms often remain**.
- **Risk factors:**
  - **Temperamental:** not well identified. Possibly negative affectivity or behavioral inhibition; parental shyness, isolation or social anxiety. Subtle receptive-language weaknesses (still within the normal range).
  - **Environmental:** parental social inhibition as a model. Parents described as overprotective or more controlling.
  - **Genetic/physiological:** likely shares genetic factors with social anxiety disorder. Abnormal auditory efferent activity during vocalization may make the child perceive their own voice oddly, leading to reluctance to speak.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- Children of immigrant families may refuse to speak the new language because they don't know it well. This is **explicitly excluded** (Criterion D).

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed.

## Functional consequences
- Social impairment and growing isolation with age. Teasing by peers.
- Academic impairment: the child doesn't tell teachers about needs (doesn't understand an assignment, needs the restroom).
- Severe school and social impairment is common.
- Mutism may serve as a compensatory strategy to lower anxious arousal.

## Differential diagnosis
- **Silent period (second-language learning):** a normal phase. Consider SM only if comprehension is adequate **and** refusal persists in **both** languages, across several unfamiliar settings, for a prolonged period.
- **Communication disorders** (language disorder, speech sound disorder, childhood-onset fluency disorder, social [pragmatic] communication disorder): the speech problem is **not restricted to particular social situations**.
- **ASD, schizophrenia or other psychosis, severe intellectual developmental disorder:** social communication is broadly impaired. SM requires an established capacity to speak in some settings (usually home).
- **Social anxiety disorder:** frequently co-occurs. **Both diagnoses may be given.**
- **ODD** (from the social anxiety differential): refusal to speak out of opposition to authority, rather than fear.

## Comorbidity
- Other anxiety disorders: **social anxiety disorder** most often, then separation anxiety and specific phobia.
- ASD often co-occurs in clinical settings.
- Oppositional behaviour in a substantial minority, sometimes only in situations that require speech.
- Communication delays or disorders in some.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **A:** list the settings where the child speaks ("speaks in full sentences with parents and siblings at home") and where they don't ("no verbal output to teacher or peers since school entry"). Include caregiver and teacher report.
- **B:** concrete impact, e.g., "unable to complete oral reading assessment", "does not request help or the restroom", no peer friendships.
- **C:** onset date and duration ≥1 month, beyond the first month of school.
- **D:** language history (languages at home, proficiency, comprehension). Document adequate comprehension of the school language.
- **E:** hearing, speech-language and developmental screening. No pervasive social-communication deficits and no psychotic symptoms.
- **MSE/phrases:** mute or whispering with the examiner; communicates by nodding, pointing or writing; freezes or avoids eye contact when prompted to speak; nonverbal play appropriate and engaged; no evidence of thought disorder.
- Screen for co-occurring social anxiety disorder and record it separately if criteria are met.
