# Specific Phobia

> **Spark note:** Marked fear of a circumscribed object or situation. The fear comes **almost every time and immediately** on contact, and the person avoids the trigger or endures it with intense fear. Unlike other anxiety disorders, **no particular cognition is required**. Multiple phobias are the norm (about 75% have more than one; the average is 3). Give **a separate diagnosis and code for each stimulus type**. Watch for situational phobia that is really agoraphobia (≥2 agoraphobic situations).

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** Marked (intense) fear or anxiety about a specific object or situation (flying, heights, animals, injections, blood).
  - *Children:* may show it by crying, tantrums, freezing or clinging.
- **B.** The object or situation **almost always** provokes **immediate** fear or anxiety.
- **C.** Actively avoided, or endured with intense fear or anxiety.
- **D.** Out of proportion to the actual danger **and** to the sociocultural context.
- **E.** Persistent, **typically ≥6 months**.
- **F.** Clinically significant distress or impairment.
- **G.** Not better explained by another mental disorder:
  - agoraphobia (situations tied to panic-like or incapacitating symptoms)
  - OCD (objects linked to obsessions)
  - PTSD (trauma reminders)
  - SAD (separation)
  - social anxiety disorder (social situations)

## Specifiers & severity
- Specify by phobic stimulus (each has its own code):
  - **Animal:** spiders, insects, dogs
  - **Natural environment:** heights, storms, water
  - **Blood-injection-injury:** needles, invasive procedures. Coded by subtype.
  - **Situational:** airplanes, elevators, enclosed places
  - **Other:** situations that may lead to choking or vomiting; in children, loud sounds or costumed characters
- **"With panic attacks"** may be added (attacks here are expected/cued).
- Severity: dimensional scale. Impairment rises with the **number** of feared stimuli.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- The fear is **circumscribed** to the phobic stimulus. It must differ from normal, transient fears and must be "marked".
- Fear varies with proximity. It can be anticipatory or happen in the moment, and can take the form of a full or limited-symptom (expected) panic attack.
- **Near-invariable:** someone who is anxious on only 1 of 5 flights does not qualify. Intensity can still vary with context (others present, length of exposure, turbulence).
- The fear is **immediate**, not delayed.
- **Active avoidance** is intentional: tunnels instead of bridges, not entering dark rooms, turning down a job in a place where the stimulus is common. It can be subtle (won't look at snake-like pictures).
- Long-standing avoiders may have rebuilt their lives (e.g., moved away from the animal) and have no current fear. Use **ongoing avoidance or refusal** (e.g., repeatedly declining work travel) to confirm the diagnosis.
- Patients often recognize their fear as excessive but still overestimate danger, so **the clinician judges proportionality**. Context matters: fear of the dark amid ongoing violence; insect fear where insects are eaten.
- The ≥6-month duration separates it from common transient fears, especially in children.

## Associated features
- Physiological arousal in anticipation of or during exposure.
- Situational, natural environment and animal types → **sympathetic** arousal.
- **BII** → **vasovagal** response: a brief rise in HR and BP, then a **drop** in HR and BP, causing fainting or near-fainting.
- Neural: abnormal activity in the amygdala, anterior cingulate, thalamus and insula during exposure.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- **Prevalence:**
  - US 12-month: **8–12%**. Europe about 6%. Asian, African and Latin American countries 2–4%.
  - Children about **5%** (range 3–9%). US adolescents 13–17 y about **16%**. Older adults **3–5%**.
  - Female:male about **2:1** (BII is about equal).
- **Development:**
  - Onset after a trauma (being attacked, getting stuck in an elevator), watching someone else's trauma, an unexpected panic attack in the situation, or information (media coverage of a crash). Many cannot recall the cause.
  - Mostly onset **before 10 y**; **median 7–11 y, mean about 10 y**. The situational type starts later than animal, natural environment and BII.
  - Childhood phobias wax and wane. Phobias that **persist into adulthood rarely remit**.
  - Can begin at any age after trauma. Choking phobia almost always follows a near-choking event.
- **Children:** fear shows as crying, tantrums, freezing or clinging. Young children may not grasp the idea of avoidance, so gather information from parents and teachers. Transient, mildly impairing fears are developmentally normal and should not be diagnosed. Assess impairment, duration and developmental fit.
- **Older adults:**
  - It is still among the common late-life disorders.
  - More natural environment phobias and **fear of falling**.
  - Often co-occurs with medical illness (CHD, COPD, Parkinson's), and elders may attribute anxiety to that illness.
  - Atypical mixed anxiety-depression presentations may warrant **unspecified anxiety disorder**.
  - Linked to lower quality of life and possibly to risk of **major neurocognitive disorder**.
- **Risk factors:**
  - **Temperamental:** negative affectivity, behavioral inhibition (nonspecific).
  - **Environmental:** parental overprotection, parental loss or separation, physical or sexual abuse. Traumatic encounters with the stimulus (sometimes).
  - **Genetic:** familial risk is *type-specific* (relatives with animal phobia → animal phobia). Heritability: animal about 32%, BII about 33%, situational about 25%.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- In the US, people of Asian and Latinx descent report lower prevalence than non-Latinx Whites and African Americans. Subtype rates vary across nations.
- Animal, natural environment and situational types are predominantly female. **BII is about equal across sexes.** Age at onset doesn't differ by sex.

## Suicide risk
- Associated with suicidal thoughts **and** attempts (US national data) and with the **transition from ideation to attempt**.
- In a German prospective study of 14–24-year-olds, **30% of first suicide attempts** were attributable to specific phobia.

## Functional consequences
- Impairment and reduced quality of life similar to other anxiety disorders and SUDs (occupational, interpersonal).
- Older adults: caregiving and volunteer roles affected; fear of falling reduces mobility and may lead to needing home support.
- More feared stimuli means more impairment.
- **BII:** avoids medical care even when needed.
- **Fear of vomiting or choking** can substantially reduce food intake.

## Differential diagnosis
- **Agoraphobia:**
  - Fears only one agoraphobic situation → specific phobia, situational. Fears ≥2 → agoraphobia (e.g., planes + elevators = public transport only → SP; planes + elevators + crowds → agoraphobia).
  - Cognition also separates them: fear of direct harm (crash, bite) → SP; fear of being unable to escape or get help with symptoms → agoraphobia.
- **Social anxiety disorder:** feared because of negative evaluation.
- **Separation anxiety disorder:** feared because of separation from an attachment figure.
- **Panic disorder:** if attacks occur *only* with the phobic stimulus → SP. If there are also unexpected attacks → panic disorder.
- **OCD:** fear driven by obsessions (blood → HIV contamination; driving → images of harming others) with OCD criteria met → OCD.
- **PTSD / acute stress disorder:** consider when onset follows trauma. Diagnose SP only if full PTSD criteria aren't met.
- **Eating disorders:** avoidance limited to food or food cues → consider AN or BN, not SP.
- **Psychotic disorders:** fear or avoidance from delusions → no SP.

## Comorbidity
- Rarely seen alone in medical settings. More often seen in mental health settings.
- Usually the **temporally primary** disorder because it starts early.
- Raises risk of later anxiety disorders, depressive and bipolar disorders, substance-related disorders, somatic symptom disorders, and personality disorders (especially **dependent PD**).

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **Name each stimulus and its type**, with a code for each (e.g., "fear of dogs → animal F40.218; fear of needles → BII injections F40.231").
- **A/B:** "reports intense fear every time a dog is within sight; fear onset is immediate". Note any expected panic attacks (record "with panic attacks").
- **C:** specific avoidance, e.g., "drives 40 min extra to avoid the bridge", "has declined 3 promotions requiring air travel", "has not had blood drawn in 6 years". For BII, document any fainting history.
- **D:** clinician's judgment of disproportionality, considering context (local dangers, cultural norms).
- **E/F:** duration ≥6 months; impairment, including medical-care avoidance and dietary restriction.
- **G rule-outs:** count the agoraphobic situations involved; cognition is about direct harm, not escape or help; no negative-evaluation focus; no separation focus; no obsessions; not limited to trauma reminders; not food-only avoidance; no delusions.
- **MSE/phrases:** anxious only when the stimulus is discussed or imagined; good insight that the fear is excessive; no generalized worry; autonomic signs (pallor, lightheadedness with blood talk in BII).
- Screen for suicidal ideation (an ideation-to-attempt risk factor). In older adults, note fear of falling and any medical comorbidity.
