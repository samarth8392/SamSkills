# Social Anxiety Disorder

> **Spark note:** Marked fear of social situations where the person could be **scrutinized**. The cognition is being **negatively evaluated**: humiliated, embarrassed, rejected, or offending others. That cognition, not the situation, is what separates it from its neighbours. Shyness alone is not a disorder (only about 12% of shy people qualify). In children the fear must show up **with peers**, not only with adults.

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** Marked fear or anxiety about ≥1 social situation with possible scrutiny: interactions (conversation, meeting strangers), being observed (eating, drinking), performing (giving a speech).
  - *Children:* must occur in **peer settings**, not only with adults.
- **B.** Fears acting in a way, or showing anxiety symptoms, that will be **negatively evaluated** (humiliating, embarrassing, leading to rejection or offending others).
- **C.** Social situations **almost always** provoke fear.
  - *Children:* may show crying, tantrums, freezing, clinging, shrinking, or failing to speak.
- **D.** Situations avoided, or endured with intense fear.
- **E.** Out of proportion to the actual threat and to the sociocultural context.
- **F.** Persistent, **typically ≥6 months**.
- **G.** Clinically significant distress or impairment.
- **H.** Not attributable to a substance or medication, or to another medical condition.
- **I.** Not better explained by another mental disorder (e.g., panic disorder, BDD, ASD).
- **J.** If a medical condition is present (Parkinson's, obesity, disfigurement from burns or injury), the fear is **clearly unrelated or excessive**.

## Specifiers & severity
- **Performance only:** fear limited to speaking or performing in public. Typical in musicians, dancers, athletes and people who present regularly. No fear or avoidance of nonperformance social situations.
- "With panic attacks" when applicable. These attacks are always socially cued.
- Severity: dimensional scale.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Feared judgments: seeming anxious, weak, crazy, stupid, boring, intimidating, dirty or unlikable.
- Feared visible signs: blushing, trembling, sweating, stumbling over words, staring.
- **Fear of offending others** (e.g., by one's gaze) may predominate in collectivistic cultures.
- Symptom-specific avoidance:
  - fear of hand tremor → avoids eating, drinking, writing or pointing in public
  - fear of sweating → avoids handshakes and spicy food
  - fear of blushing → avoids performing, bright lights, intimate topics
- **Paruresis** ("shy bladder"): avoids urinating in public restrooms when others are present.
- Must be near-invariable. Occasional anxiety doesn't count.
- Anticipatory anxiety can start weeks ahead (daily worry, rehearsing a speech for days).
- Avoidance can be extensive (no parties, school refusal) or **subtle** (over-preparing, diverting attention, limiting eye contact).
- When there is real threat (bullying), the anxiety may not be excessive. The clinician judges proportionality with culture in mind (reticence may signal respect).
- **Criterion G example:** fear of public speaking that is never required at work or school and causes no distress → no diagnosis. Passing up a wanted job or course because of it → G is met.

## Associated features
- Under-assertive or overly submissive (less often, controlling of the conversation). Rigid posture, poor eye contact, soft voice. Shy or withdrawn, discloses little.
- May choose jobs without social contact (not the performance-only type). May live at home longer. Men may marry and start families later; women may never work outside the home.
- **Self-medication** (drinking before a party).
- Older adults: medical symptoms such as tremor or tachycardia worsen.
- **Blushing is the hallmark physical response.**

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- **Prevalence:**
  - US 12-month about **7%**. Most of the world **0.5–2.0%**; Europe median 2.3%. Rising in the US and East Asia.
  - Adolescents (13–17) about half the adult rate. Lower after 65; older adults 2–5% (N. America, Europe, Australia).
  - Female > male in the community (OR 1.5–2.2), most marked in adolescents and young adults. Equal or slightly male-predominant in clinics, probably from help-seeking.
  - Lower US rates among Asian, Latinx, African American and Caribbean Black people than non-Hispanic Whites.
- **Development:**
  - **Median onset 13 y; 75% between 8 and 15 y.** May grow out of childhood inhibition or shyness, or begin in early childhood.
  - Onset can be insidious or follow a humiliating event (being bullied, vomiting during a speech). **Adult first onset is rare** and usually follows a stressor or a new role (promotion, marrying into a different social class).
  - Can ease after marriage (dating fears) and return after divorce. Persistent in people who seek care.
  - Adolescents fear a broader range of situations (including dating). Older adults show lower-level anxiety across more situations.
  - Late-life themes: sensory decline, tremor (Parkinson's), incontinence, forgetting names. Detection in elders is harder because of somatic focus, medical comorbidity, low insight, changed roles, and reticence.
  - Remission trajectories vary (short, fluctuating, chronic).
- **Risk factors:**
  - **Temperamental:** behavioral inhibition, fear of negative evaluation, harm avoidance, high negative affectivity, low extraversion.
  - **Environmental:** peer victimization, childhood maltreatment and adversity. Everyday ethnic discrimination and racism (among African Americans and Caribbean Blacks).
  - **Genetic:** behavioral inhibition is strongly genetic, with gene × environment interaction (inhibited children are more affected by anxious parental modeling). First-degree relatives have **2–6×** the risk. Heritability is higher in children than adults and higher for symptoms than for the diagnosis.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- Feared situations are similar across US ethnoracial groups.
- Non-Latinx Whites have earlier onset; Latinx people report more impairment.
- Immigrant status is associated with lower rates.
- **Taijin kyofusho** (Japan, Korea): fear of making others uncomfortable (e.g., "my gaze upsets people"), sometimes held with delusional intensity. Can meet criteria for SocAD, BDD or delusional disorder.
- Onset age doesn't differ by gender.
- Women report more social fears and more comorbid MDD and anxiety.
- Men more often fear dating, have ODD, CD or ASPD, and use alcohol or drugs for relief. **Paruresis is more common in men.**

## Suicide risk
- Among US adolescents, linked to higher risk of active suicidal thoughts and attempts in **Latinx** youth (not in non-Latinx Whites), independent of depression and income.

## Functional consequences
- School dropout. Lower well-being, employment, productivity, SES and quality of life.
- More often single, unmarried, divorced or childless (especially men); women more often unemployed.
- Friendships less close and less supportive. Leisure restricted. Older adults: caregiving and volunteering affected.
- **Only about half seek treatment**, typically after **15–20 years** of symptoms.
- **Unemployment predicts persistence.**

## Differential diagnosis
- **Normative shyness:** a trait, not pathology. Diagnose SocAD only with significant impairment and full criteria.
- **Agoraphobia:** avoids social settings out of fear of being trapped or without help. People with SocAD are **calm when entirely alone**; people with agoraphobia often aren't.
- **Panic disorder:** SocAD attacks are always socially cued, never "out of the blue". The patient fears the evaluation an attack would bring more than the attack itself.
- **GAD:** social worries are about ongoing relationships. In children, performance worries occur even when no one is evaluating. SocAD centres on others' evaluation.
- **Separation anxiety disorder:** comfortable socially when the attachment figure is present or when at home. People with SocAD can be uncomfortable even at home or with attachment figures.
- **Specific phobia:** may fear embarrassment (e.g., fainting at a blood draw) but not negative evaluation in other social settings.
- **Selective mutism:** no fear of evaluation when speech isn't required.
- **MDD:** fears evaluation because they feel bad or unworthy. SocAD fears evaluation of specific behaviours or symptoms.
- **BDD:** if social fear comes **only** from appearance beliefs → no separate SocAD.
- **Delusional disorder:** nonbizarre delusions about rejection or offending others. SocAD usually has good insight.
- **ASD:** core social-communication deficits. People with SocAD have adequate age-appropriate relationships and communication, though they may look impaired with strangers.
- **Avoidant PD:** broader avoidance, more impairment, and a pervasive negative self-concept (rejection = worthlessness, never fitting in since early childhood). Highest comorbidity pairing with SocAD.
- **Other mental disorders:** in schizophrenia, other psychotic features are usually present. In eating disorders, make sure fear of evaluation of ED behaviours isn't the only source. In OCD, diagnose SocAD only if the social fear is independent of the obsessions.
- **Medical conditions:** embarrassing symptoms (Parkinsonian tremor). Diagnose only if the fear of evaluation is excessive.
- **ODD:** refusing to speak out of opposition vs fear of evaluation.

## Comorbidity
- Other anxiety disorders, MDD and SUDs. SocAD usually comes **first**, except for specific phobia and SAD.
- Chronic isolation → MDD (also high in older adults).
- Substances: used as self-medication, and intoxication or withdrawal tremor can feed further social fear.
- Frequently BDD. Generalized SocAD with avoidant PD. In children: high-functioning ASD and selective mutism.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **A:** list the feared situations by type (interaction / observation / performance). In children, note peer-setting examples.
- **B:** state the feared evaluation in the patient's terms, e.g., "fears others will notice hand tremor and think he is incompetent".
- **C/D:** frequency ("anxious at every staff meeting"), avoidance ("has not eaten in the cafeteria in a year"), and safety behaviours (rehearsing, avoiding eye contact, holding a cup with both hands, pre-drinking).
- **E:** clinician's judgment that the fear exceeds the real risk; cultural context; any genuine bullying.
- **F/G:** duration ≥6 months; concrete impairment (declined promotion, dropped class, no dating).
- **H/I/J rule-outs:** substance use (including pre-event drinking) and medical conditions; for a visible medical condition, document that the fear is excessive; no appearance-only focus (BDD); no pervasive social-communication deficit (ASD); attacks are socially cued (not PD); calm when alone (not agoraphobia).
- **Specifier:** document "performance only" if nonperformance situations are comfortable.
- **MSE/phrases:** visible blushing or sweating; limited eye contact; soft or quiet voice; brief answers, little self-disclosure; rigid posture; good insight that the fear is excessive; anxious affect when describing evaluation.
- Screen alcohol use, depression and suicidality (especially in adolescents).
