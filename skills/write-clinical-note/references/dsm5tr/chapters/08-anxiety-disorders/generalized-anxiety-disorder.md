# Generalized Anxiety Disorder

> **Spark note:** Excessive, **hard-to-control** worry about **several** everyday domains (work, health, finances, family, minor matters), on **more days than not for ≥6 months**. It comes with **≥3 of 6** tension-type symptoms (only **1** needed in children). Autonomic hyperarousal is *less* prominent than in panic disorder. The common pitfall is missing that the worry is single-themed and therefore belongs to another disorder (illness, separation, social evaluation, obsessions, trauma).

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** Excessive anxiety and worry (apprehensive expectation) about a **number of** events or activities (e.g., work or school performance), occurring **more days than not for ≥6 months**.
- **B.** The worry is **difficult to control**.
- **C.** **≥3 of 6** symptoms, some present more days than not over the past 6 months (**children: 1 required**):
  1. Restlessness, feeling keyed up or on edge
  2. Easily fatigued
  3. Difficulty concentrating or mind going blank
  4. Irritability
  5. Muscle tension
  6. Sleep disturbance (trouble falling or staying asleep, or restless, unsatisfying sleep)
- **D.** Clinically significant distress or impairment.
- **E.** Not due to a substance or medication, or to a medical condition (e.g., hyperthyroidism).
- **F.** Not better explained by another mental disorder, where the worry focus would be:
  - panic attacks (PD)
  - negative evaluation (SocAD)
  - contamination or other obsessions (OCD)
  - separation (SAD)
  - trauma reminders (PTSD)
  - weight gain (AN)
  - physical complaints (SSD)
  - appearance flaws (BDD)
  - having a serious illness (IAD)
  - delusional content (schizophrenia, delusional disorder)

## Specifiers & severity
- No subtypes. "With panic attacks" if worry-triggered attacks occur.
- Severity: dimensional scale. Course fluctuates between syndromal and subsyndromal.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- The intensity, duration or frequency of worry is out of proportion to the real likelihood or impact. It intrudes on attention to the task at hand.
- **Adults** worry about routine matters: job responsibilities, health, finances, family health, misfortune befalling children, chores, being late. **Children** worry about **competence and performance quality**. The focus shifts from topic to topic.
- **How it differs from normal worry:**
  1. It is excessive and impairing, whereas normal worry feels manageable and can be postponed.
  2. It is more pervasive, pronounced, distressing and long-lasting, and often has **no precipitant**. The more domains involved, the more likely GAD.
  3. Normal worry seldom comes with physical symptoms.

## Associated features
- From muscle tension: trembling, twitching, shakiness, muscle aches.
- Somatic symptoms (sweating, nausea, diarrhea); **exaggerated startle**.
- Autonomic hyperarousal (tachycardia, dyspnea, dizziness) is **less** prominent than in PD.
- Stress-related conditions often co-occur (IBS, headaches).

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- **Prevalence:**
  - US 12-month: **0.9%** adolescents, **2.9%** adults. Worldwide mean 1.3% (0.2–4.3%). US lifetime morbid risk **9.0%**.
  - Females at least **2×** males.
  - Older adults (including ≥75): **2.8–3.1%** (US, Israel, Europe).
  - More common in people of European descent than Asian or African descent, and in high-income than low/middle-income countries.
- **Course:**
  - Many say they have been anxious all their lives; early-life worry shows as **anxious temperament**.
  - **Mean onset 35 y (N. America)**, later than other anxiety disorders. Rare before adolescence. Onset spans a wide range and is older in lower-income countries.
  - **Chronic, waxing and waning**, syndromal ↔ subsyndromal. **Full remission is very rare.** More persistent in lower-income countries; more impairing in high-income countries.
  - Earlier onset → more comorbidity and impairment. Younger adults have more severe symptoms than older adults.
  - Presentation is stable over the lifespan; **worry content is age-appropriate**.
  - **Children/adolescents:** school and sports performance (even when not being evaluated), punctuality, catastrophes (earthquakes, nuclear war). Overly conforming, perfectionistic, unsure of themselves; redo tasks; seek reassurance and approval excessively.
  - **Elderly:** chronic physical disease is a potent worry trigger. Frail elders' safety worries, especially **falling**, limit activity.
- **Risk factors:**
  - **Temperamental:** behavioral inhibition, negative affectivity, harm avoidance, reward dependence, attentional bias to threat.
  - **Environmental:** childhood adversity; overprotective, overcontrolling or avoidance-reinforcing parenting.
  - **Genetic:** **about ⅓ of the risk is genetic**, overlapping with negative affectivity and shared with other anxiety and mood disorders, especially **MDD**.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- Expression varies: **somatic** symptoms dominate in some cultures, **cognitive** in others (most visible at first presentation; more symptoms are reported over time).
- The worry topic can be culture-specific. Judge excessiveness in context.
- US: higher prevalence with **racism and ethnic discrimination**, and in some groups with being US-born.
- Clinical samples 55–60% women; epidemiological samples about ⅔ women.
- Symptoms are similar across sexes. Women's comorbidity is mostly anxiety and unipolar depression; men's extends to SUD.

## Suicide risk
- Associated with increased suicidal thoughts and behaviour after adjusting for comorbidity and stressors.
- **The most frequent anxiety disorder diagnosed in suicides** (psychological autopsy).
- Both subthreshold and threshold GAD in the past year are linked to ideation.
- One of the 3 anxiety disorders most linked to the move from ideation to attempt (chapter intro).

## Functional consequences
- Worry eats time and energy and slows efficiency at home and at work. The associated symptoms (tension, fatigue, poor concentration, poor sleep) add impairment.
- May undermine the ability to **encourage confidence in one's children**.
- Disability independent of comorbidity; most non-institutionalized adults are moderately to seriously disabled.
- **110 million disability days/year** in the US. Lower work performance, more medical use, and higher **coronary morbidity** risk.

## Differential diagnosis
- **Anxiety disorder due to AMC:** worry judged to be a physiological effect of a medical condition (pheochromocytoma, hyperthyroidism), based on history, labs or exam.
- **Substance/medication-induced:** e.g., severe anxiety only during heavy coffee use → **caffeine-induced anxiety disorder**.
- **Social anxiety disorder:** anticipatory anxiety about situations involving evaluation. GAD worry occurs **whether or not** the person is evaluated.
- **Separation anxiety disorder:** worry only about separation. GAD may include it but has other excessive worries too.
- **Panic disorder:** worry-triggered attacks don't count toward PD. Add PD only if there are unexpected attacks plus Criterion B.
- **Illness anxiety / somatic symptom disorder:** IAD if one's own illness is the only fear. SSD if worry centres on somatic symptoms. In GAD, health is one domain among several.
- **OCD:** GAD worry is about **future problems** and the abnormality is its excessiveness. Obsessions are **intrusive, unwanted, inappropriate** thoughts, urges or images.
- **PTSD:** anxiety is invariably present; don't diagnose GAD if PTSD explains it.
- **Adjustment disorder:** a residual category. Onset within **3 months** of a stressor, lasting **≤6 months** after the stressor ends. Use only when no other disorder (including GAD) is met.
- **Depressive, bipolar, psychotic disorders:** generalized worry is common in these. Diagnose GAD comorbidly if the worry is severe enough to need its own clinical attention.

## Comorbidity
- High lifetime and current overlap with other anxiety and unipolar depressive disorders, through shared negative affectivity and genetic/environmental risk (independent pathways also possible).
- Less common with substance use, conduct, psychotic, neurodevelopmental and neurocognitive disorders.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **A:** list ≥2–3 worry domains with examples ("worries daily about finances, children's safety, job performance and minor errands"). Document **more days than not** and **≥6 months**. Note that worry occurs without precipitants and is out of proportion.
- **B:** uncontrollability, e.g., "unable to stop worrying even when trying to focus on work"; "worry interferes with reading/TV".
- **C:** name each symptom present (≥3 in adults; ≥1 in children), e.g., "feels on edge", "fatigued by afternoon", "mind goes blank", "snaps at family", "neck/shoulder tension", "lies awake 1–2 h nightly".
- **D:** impairment and distress, with concrete examples.
- **E rule-outs:** caffeine, stimulants, decongestants, steroids, thyroid medication, alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal; TSH and other medical evaluation as indicated.
- **F rule-outs:** worry is not limited to one theme (illness, separation, evaluation, panic, trauma, weight, appearance); not intrusive or ego-dystonic (OCD); not tied to a recent stressor alone (adjustment); not purely a feature of a depressive episode.
- **MSE/phrases:** tense posture, fidgeting or restlessness; anxious, apprehensive affect; thought content dominated by "what-if" worries across multiple topics; reports difficulty concentrating; startles easily; insight usually fair to good.
- **Suicide screen**, given strong association with suicidal ideation and completed suicide.
- If worry occurs less than "more days than not" → document F41.8 other specified anxiety disorder with that reason.
