# Panic Attack Specifier

> **Spark note:** A panic attack is a **symptom event, not a mental disorder**, and it **cannot be coded**. Record it as a specifier on whatever disorder it occurs in (e.g., "PTSD with panic attacks", "MDD with panic attacks"). It is not used with panic disorder, because attacks are already in those criteria. Panic attacks are common (about 10% a year) and mark severity, comorbidity and poor prognosis across disorders. Most people who have them never develop panic disorder.

## Criteria at a glance
- An **abrupt surge** of intense fear or intense discomfort that **peaks within minutes**, with **≥4 of 13** symptoms. It may start from a calm or an anxious state.
  - 1 palpitations or racing heart · 2 sweating · 3 trembling or shaking · 4 shortness of breath or smothering · 5 choking feeling · 6 chest pain or discomfort · 7 nausea or abdominal distress · 8 dizzy, unsteady, light-headed or faint · 9 chills or heat sensations · 10 paresthesias · 11 derealization or depersonalization · 12 fear of losing control or "going crazy" · 13 fear of dying
- Culture-specific symptoms (tinnitus, neck soreness, headache, uncontrollable screaming or crying) may appear but **don't count** toward the 4.
- Usable with **any anxiety disorder** and with other mental disorders (depressive, PTSD, SUD, etc.) and some medical conditions (cardiac, respiratory, vestibular, GI).

## Specifiers & severity
- **Full-symptom** (≥4) vs **limited-symptom** (<4 symptoms, otherwise meets all features).
- **Expected** (obvious cue, such as a typical situation) vs **unexpected** (no obvious cue, e.g., while relaxing or on waking from sleep).
- Full attacks are linked to more morbidity than limited ones.

| Code | Use |
|---|---|
| — | Not codable. Write "with panic attacks" after the primary diagnosis. |

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- **11 of the 13 symptoms are physical; 2 are cognitive** (fear of losing control or going crazy; fear of dying). "Going crazy" is the patient's colloquial phrase, not a pejorative term.
- "Within minutes" means literally a few minutes to peak.
- **Time the onset from the abrupt rise**, not from when background anxiety began. The attack can return to an anxious or calm baseline, and can peak again.
- It is distinguished from ongoing anxiety by its rapid peak, discrete nature, and usually greater severity.
- The clinician judges expected vs unexpected from the sequence of events and the patient's view of whether there was any apparent reason.
- **Nocturnal panic** is waking *in* a state of panic, not panicking after fully waking. It is a type of unexpected attack.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- **Prevalence:**
  - Adult 12-month **9.5–11.2%** (US, Spain). No significant difference among African American, Asian American and Latinx people.
  - American Indians: lifetime about 8.5%. Cross-national lifetime: **13.2%**.
  - Female > male (the gap is larger for panic disorder).
  - Rare in children until puberty, then rises. Declines in older adults.
- **Course:**
  - Mean onset about **22–23 y** (US). Course follows the co-occurring disorders and life stress.
  - Unexpected attacks are rare before adolescence. Adolescents may under-disclose.
  - **Older adults:** weaker autonomic response; may call it "discomfort" rather than "fear"; hybrid limited attacks with generalized anxiety; retrospective situational explanations → unexpected attacks under-endorsed.
- **Risk factors:**
  - **Temperamental:** negative affectivity, anxiety sensitivity, behavioral inhibition, harm avoidance, prior "fearful spells".
  - **Environmental:** smoking; stressors in the months before the first attack; separation from parents, overprotective parenting, parental rejection.
  - **Physiological:** in COPD, low perceived control and catastrophic beliefs about breathless episodes increase panic symptoms.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- Culture affects the expected/unexpected call.
- Symptom frequencies vary: more paresthesias in African Americans, dizziness in several Asian groups, trembling in non-Latinx Whites.
- **Khyâl attacks** (Cambodian: dizziness, tinnitus, neck soreness; fear of khyâl circulation → catastrophic cognitions).
- **Trúng gió** (Vietnamese; headache).
- **Ataque de nervios** (Latin American; may last longer than minutes; some presentations instead fit **other specified dissociative disorder**).
- Women more often report shortness of breath and nausea, and less often sweating.

## Diagnostic markers
- Recordings of natural attacks show an abrupt rise in arousal (usually HR) that peaks and subsides within minutes, sometimes preceded by cardiorespiratory instability.
- Increased HR and tidal volume; **pCO2 drops**.

## Suicide risk
- Panic attacks are associated with higher rates of suicide attempts and suicidal thoughts, independent of comorbidity and other risk factors.

## Functional consequences
- In co-occurring disorders (anxiety, depressive, bipolar, SUD, psychotic, personality), panic attacks mean **greater severity, more comorbidity and poorer treatment response**.
- Recurrent and more severe attacks raise the odds of developing panic disorder and other conditions and of a persistent, impairing course.
- Full attacks bring more health care use, disability and lower quality of life than limited ones.

## Differential diagnosis
- **Other paroxysmal episodes** (e.g., "anger attacks"): the core emotion is not fear or discomfort (anger, grief) → not a panic attack.
- **Anxiety disorder due to AMC:** hyperthyroidism, hyperparathyroidism, pheochromocytoma, vestibular dysfunction, seizures, cardiopulmonary disease (arrhythmia, SVT, asthma, COPD). Workup: calcium, Holter, exam.
- **Substance/medication-induced:** stimulant, caffeine or cannabis intoxication; alcohol or barbiturate withdrawal. Check whether attacks predated the substance use. **Red flags:** onset after 45; vertigo, loss of consciousness, incontinence, slurred speech, amnesia.
- **Panic disorder:** repeated unexpected attacks are **necessary but not sufficient**; full PD criteria must be met.

## Comorbidity
- Associated with anxiety, depressive, bipolar, impulse-control and substance use disorders. Predicts later anxiety, depressive and bipolar disorders, alcohol use disorder, and possibly others.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Record the format: "[Primary diagnosis], with panic attacks" (e.g., "F43.10 posttraumatic stress disorder, with panic attacks"). There is no separate code.
- Describe one representative attack: trigger or context, whether expected or unexpected, time to peak, duration, and symptoms counted (≥4 of 13; exclude culture-specific ones).
- Note the frequency, any nocturnal attacks, and whether attacks are full or limited.
- Document medical and substance rule-outs when warranted: age at onset, atypical features, caffeine, stimulants, cannabis, alcohol or sedative withdrawal.
- Screen for criteria for panic disorder (any unexpected attacks plus ≥1 month of worry or behaviour change) and for agoraphobia.
- Suicide screen (attacks are an independent risk marker).
- **MSE/phrases:** describes a sudden-onset surge with autonomic symptoms; fear of dying during the episode; returns to baseline within minutes to an hour; no alteration of consciousness (if there is, think medical).
