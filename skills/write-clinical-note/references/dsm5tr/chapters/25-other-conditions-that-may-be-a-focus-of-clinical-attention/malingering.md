# Malingering

> **Spark note:** Malingering requires intentional false or grossly exaggerated symptoms for an external benefit. Inconsistency, a legal case, or poor cooperation alone does not demonstrate intentional deception. (p. 1102)

## Essential features and clinical pearls
External incentives can include avoiding duties/work/prosecution, obtaining compensation, or obtaining drugs. The source notes that feigning can be adaptive in extraordinary circumstances such as captivity during war. This is not a mental-disorder diagnosis. (p. 1102)

## Factors prompting closer assessment
The source highlights combinations of medicolegal context, substantial discrepancy between claims and objective findings, poor evaluation/treatment cooperation, and antisocial personality disorder. These are reasons to investigate, not a scored diagnostic test or proof of intent. (p. 1102)

## Differential diagnosis
- **Factitious disorder:** intentional symptom falsification without the external incentive characteristic of malingering; apparent sick-role motivation favors factitious disorder.
- **Functional neurological symptom disorder / other somatic symptom-related conditions:** symptoms are not established as intentionally manufactured for an external incentive; do not equate unexplained symptoms with feigning.
- Evidence of feigned impairment still requires clarifying motivation to distinguish malingering from factitious disorder. (p. 1102)

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Describe specific claims, observations, discrepancies, sources, and actual evidence of intentional production and external incentive.
- Clearly distinguish suspicion from substantiated findings and consider alternative explanations for inconsistent function or noncooperation.
- Avoid pejorative labels and do not fabricate a motive or infer deception solely from benefit eligibility or litigation.
