# Major or Mild Neurocognitive Disorder Due to Prion Disease

> **Spark note:** The combination of rapidly advancing cognitive loss and cerebellar/movement findings raises this etiology. Motor features or biomarker evidence are required; rapid progression is common rather than an absolute duration criterion. (pp. 965–968)

## Criteria at a glance
**A:** Major/mild NCD. **B:** Gradual emergence, commonly followed by rapid worsening. **C:** Prion-associated motor signs (such as myoclonus or ataxia) **or** biomarker evidence. **D:** No better medical or mental explanation. (pp. 965–966)
## Specifiers & severity
Establish the [major](major-neurocognitive-disorder.md) or [mild](mild-neurocognitive-disorder.md) syndrome first: acquired decline, concern plus objective assessment, functional threshold, and exclusion of delirium-only or another mental disorder. Major NCD severity describes cognitive effects on function: mild = instrumental tasks affected; moderate = basic self-care affected; severe = complete dependence. Record clinically significant behavioral symptoms individually with the with/without behavioral disturbance specifier. These are separate from the severity of the underlying disease. (pp. 916–920)

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
The group includes sporadic, genetic, iatrogenic, and variant Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease and rarer prion conditions. Most are recognized at major NCD severity because progression may occur over roughly six months. Variant CJD may initially feature mood/anxiety or social withdrawal. Definitive confirmation in the source requires brain tissue examination; supportive tests differ by prion subtype. (pp. 966–968)

## Diagnostic markers
CSF 14-3-3 and tau indicate neuronal injury but have variable specificity. RT-QuIC has very high specificity. Diffusion-weighted MRI can show cortical/subcortical gray-matter abnormalities; some patients show characteristic periodic EEG discharges. No single generic abnormal test substitutes for attribution. (p. 967)

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
Sporadic CJD incidence is approximately 1–2 per million annually; prevalence is poorly known because survival is short. Typical peak onset is around 67, but a broad age range occurs. Rare slower cases unfold over about two years. Up to 15% of prion disease involves dominant PRNP mutations. Documented acquired transmission includes particular tissue products/procedures and, for variant CJD, transfusion; routine health care employment was not shown to increase sporadic CJD risk. (pp. 966–967)

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed.

## Differential diagnosis
Other NCDs can resemble the occasional slower prion course. Rapid decline with striking cerebellar/motor findings favors prion disease but competing medical etiologies remain excluded by Criterion D. (p. 968)

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Describe dated rate of decline and affected domains, objective testing and independence.
- Record specific motor findings and/or exact biomarker evidence, its source and diagnostic limitations.
- Distinguish a suspected clinical etiology from tissue-confirmed disease; document competing-cause assessment and major/mild level.
