# Major or Mild Neurocognitive Disorder Due to Traumatic Brain Injury

> **Spark note:** A remote head injury plus a cognitive complaint does not establish causation. The cognitive syndrome must start at injury or on regaining consciousness, continue beyond the acute period, and satisfy the general major/mild NCD requirements. Injury severity and NCD severity are different judgments. (pp. 948–950)

## Criteria at a glance
- **A:** Major or mild NCD criteria are met.
- **B:** A head impact or rapid brain displacement occurred, supported by ≥1: unconsciousness, amnesia around the injury, confusion/disorientation, or neurological evidence (examination or imaging).
- **C:** NCD begins immediately at injury or recovery of consciousness and continues after the acute injury phase. No universal minimum number of days is specified. (pp. 948–950)
- Do not misattribute intoxication, treatment effects, systemic injury, psychological trauma, language barriers, or another illness to the acute brain injury signs. (p. 949)
## Specifiers & severity
Establish the [major](major-neurocognitive-disorder.md) or [mild](mild-neurocognitive-disorder.md) syndrome first: acquired decline, concern plus objective assessment, functional threshold, and exclusion of delirium-only or another mental disorder. Major NCD severity describes cognitive effects on function: mild = instrumental tasks affected; moderate = basic self-care affected; severe = complete dependence. Record clinically significant behavioral symptoms individually with the with/without behavioral disturbance specifier. These are separate from the severity of the underlying disease. (pp. 916–920)

Add an appropriate psychiatric diagnosis/code when clinically significant psychiatric symptoms are attributed to the same medical condition; see the relevant phenomenological chapter.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
Attention, processing speed, memory/learning, executive skills, and social cognition are commonly affected. Focal injury can add aphasia, apraxia, or perceptual-motor impairment. Domain testing interpreted against premorbid function, plus functional assessment, establishes NCD; imaging alone cannot. No other established diagnostic biomarker is specified. (pp. 950, 952–953)

## Associated features
Seizures, eye-movement abnormalities, primitive reflexes, headache, dizziness, sensory sensitivity, fatigue, sleep changes, irritability, aggression, and apathy may support the clinical picture but are not substitutes for cognitive criteria. (pp. 950–954)

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- Source estimates >2.87 million U.S. TBIs annually; that is injury incidence, not NCD prevalence. NCD frequency varies with injury severity and elapsed time. (pp. 950–951)
- Mild injury generally improves over days/weeks, with cognitive resolution typically within 3–12 months. Persistent or later worsening symptoms call for reassessment of alternative contributors. Improvement after more severe injuries can continue for years. (pp. 951–952)
- Poorer outcome is associated with older age (including >40), lower baseline cognitive resources, earlier depression, longer posttraumatic amnesia, intracranial abnormalities, and pre-injury substance disorders. (p. 952)
- Injury grading in the source: mild unconsciousness ≤30 min, amnesia/altered consciousness ≤1 day, Glasgow score 13–15; moderate unconsciousness >30 min–<24 h, amnesia/alteration >1–<7 days, Glasgow 9–12; severe unconsciousness ≥24 h, amnesia/alteration ≥7 days, Glasgow 3–8. Complicated mild injury has the mild clinical profile with abnormal acute imaging. These do not determine NCD severity. (pp. 949–950)

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
Sex differences in injury circumstances and exposure are described; prognosis findings are mixed. Language barriers can confound acute injury assessment. (pp. 949, 951)

## Suicide risk
The source describes elevated long-term risk, including after concussion and in civilian/veteran populations. Depression contributes but does not explain all risk. Assess current thoughts, past behavior, and protective/contextual factors without inferring suicidality from a TBI history. (p. 953)

## Functional consequences
Separate cognitive limitations in medication management, employment, self-care, and community activity from motor disability, pain, psychiatric symptoms, and available support. (pp. 953–954)

## Differential diagnosis
- **Depression, PTSD, anxiety, sleep disorders, substance effects:** can independently account for the same cognitive profile or amplify TBI impairment.
- **Medication effects:** assess anticholinergic drugs, benzodiazepines, antipsychotics, and antiepileptics.
- **Invalid assessment, factitious disorder, malingering:** investigate alternative explanations when performance cannot be interpreted or expected recovery and presentation diverge; inconsistency alone does not prove deliberate deception. (p. 954)

## Comorbidity
PTSD, depressive/anxiety syndromes, substance disorders, seizures, sleep problems, and physical injuries are common. More than one condition may explain cognitive deficits. (p. 954)

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Record injury date/mechanism, contemporaneous evidence of ≥1 injury sign, source of records/collateral, and timing of cognitive onset relative to consciousness.
- Document baseline versus present performance by domain and quantified findings; identify which cognitive deficits alter independent function.
- Track recovery and persistence beyond the acute period; distinguish injury grade from major/mild NCD and major severity.
- Record psychiatric, medication, sleep, pain, substance, sensory/motor contributors and suicide assessment actually performed.
