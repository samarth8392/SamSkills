# Delirium

> **Spark note:** Delirium is an acute, fluctuating disturbance of **attention and awareness** (an altered state of consciousness) plus another cognitive deficit, caused by a medical condition, a substance or medication, a toxin, or several of these. Hypoactive delirium is the form that gets missed: a quiet, sleepy older patient is often labelled "depressed" or "demented". Never diagnose it in coma. When delirium sits on top of an existing NCD, **manage the delirium first**. (pp. 907–915)

## Criteria at a glance (p. 907)
- **A. Attention + awareness:** reduced ability to **direct, focus, sustain and shift** attention, **plus** reduced awareness of the environment.
- **B. Time course:** develops over a short period (**usually hours to a few days**), is a **change from baseline**, and tends to **fluctuate in severity across the day**.
- **C. Another cognitive disturbance:** e.g., memory deficit, disorientation, language, visuospatial ability or perception.
- **D. Exclusions:** A and C are **not better explained** by a preexisting, established or evolving NCD, and they do **not** occur in a severely reduced level of arousal such as **coma**.
- **E. Etiology:** evidence from history, physical exam or labs that the disturbance is a **direct physiological consequence** of another medical condition, substance intoxication or withdrawal (drug of abuse or medication), toxin exposure, or **multiple etiologies**.

## Specifiers & severity (pp. 908, 911)
**Course:**
- **Acute:** lasts a few hours or days.
- **Persistent:** lasts weeks or months.
- In hospital, delirium typically lasts about **1 week**, but some symptoms often persist after discharge.

**Level of activity:**
- **Hyperactive:** increased psychomotor activity, ± mood lability, agitation, refusal to cooperate with care. More common or more often recognized. Often linked to medication side effects and drug withdrawal.
- **Hypoactive:** reduced psychomotor activity, sluggishness, lethargy approaching stupor. May be more frequent in older adults and is often **unrecognized** in EDs and hospitals.
- **Mixed level of activity:** normal psychomotor activity despite disturbed attention and awareness, **or** an activity level that fluctuates rapidly. Patients can switch quickly between hyper- and hypoactive states.

**Etiological subtypes (specify whether):**
- **Substance intoxication delirium:** diagnose **instead of** intoxication when A and C dominate the picture and are severe enough to need clinical attention.
- **Substance withdrawal delirium:** diagnose instead of withdrawal under the same conditions.
- **Medication-induced delirium:** A and C arise as a side effect of a medication taken **as prescribed**.
- **Delirium due to another medical condition (F05).**
- **Delirium due to multiple etiologies (F05):** e.g., more than one medical condition, or a medical condition plus intoxication or medication side effect.

### Recording order (pp. 910–911)
- **Substance intoxication/withdrawal delirium:** [severity] [substance] use disorder **with** [substance] intoxication/withdrawal delirium, [acute/persistent], [hyperactive/hypoactive/mixed].
  - e.g., *F14.221 severe cocaine use disorder with cocaine intoxication delirium, acute, hyperactive*
  - e.g., *F10.231 severe alcohol use disorder with alcohol withdrawal delirium, acute, hyperactive*
  - Do **not** give a separate use disorder diagnosis.
- **No use disorder:** name only the delirium, e.g., *F16.921 phencyclidine intoxication delirium, acute, hypoactive*.
- **Medication-induced:** [medication]-induced delirium, course, activity, e.g., *F19.921 dexamethasone-induced delirium, acute, hyperactive*.
- **Substance with no class:** use "other substance". **Etiology suspected but class unknown:** use "unknown substance".

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls (pp. 911–912)
- The core is an **acute impairment of consciousness**: attention plus awareness. Questions must be repeated, the person **perseverates** on an earlier answer, and irrelevant stimuli distract easily. Impaired awareness affects internal thinking, insight, and making sense of the surroundings.
- Symptoms often **worsen in the evening and at night** as orienting cues fade ("sundowning" pattern).
- Criterion C commonly involves **recent memory**, **disorientation to time/place**, language (especially **semantic comprehension**), and perceptual disturbance. Perceptual disturbances are misinterpretations, illusions or hallucinations. They are usually **visual** but can be in any modality, and range from simple to highly complex.
- **The arousal continuum:** normal → delirium → stupor → coma. Delirium needs an **aroused cortex** that can respond to verbal stimulation.
  - If there is only a minimal response to verbal or physical stimuli, classify it as a disorder of arousal (coma/stupor), **not** delirium. This includes iatrogenic deep sedation in the ICU and anesthesia.
  - Delirium can be a **stage after emergence** from coma or stupor.
  - A patient in a sleep phase of the disrupted circadian cycle should not be mistaken for an arousal disorder.
- Delirium often arises **on top of** an existing NCD, because mild or major NCD makes the brain more vulnerable.
- Code the etiological subtype that fits: intoxication, withdrawal, medication, medical condition or multiple.

## Associated features (pp. 912–913)
- **Sleep-wake disturbance** is very common and has been proposed as a core criterion. It can include daytime sleepiness, nighttime agitation, trouble falling asleep, all-day sleepiness, all-night wakefulness, or full **day–night reversal**.
- **Emotional disturbance:** anxiety, fear, depression, irritability, anger, euphoria, apathy. Shifts can be rapid and unpredictable. Patients may call out, scream, curse, mutter or moan, especially **at night** and when cues are lacking.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors (pp. 913–914)
**Prevalence by setting:**
- Community: 1–2%.
- Older adults in North American EDs: 8–17%, often signalling a medical illness.
- On hospital admission: 18–35%. Arising during hospitalization: 29–64%.
- Postoperative older adults: 11–51%. ICU: up to 81%.
- Nursing home / post-acute care: 20–22%.
- End of life: up to 88%.
- In a US ICU case series, younger African Americans had lower delirium rates than White patients of similar age, despite more risk factors.

**Course:**
- Most recover fully with or without treatment, especially non-elderly patients.
- Can progress to stupor, coma, seizures or death if undetected and the cause goes untreated.
- In older adults it is linked to later cognitive decline and major NCD, especially when cognitive impairment was already present.
- 1-year mortality after diagnosis is **38–41%** in hospitalized patients, highest with malignancy or serious medical illness.

**Risk factors:**
- Functional impairment, preexisting cognitive impairment, sensory impairment (vision/hearing), older age, illness severity/comorbidity, infection, depression, history of stroke, history of alcohol use.
- Mild and major NCD raise risk and complicate the course.
- Falls are an **outcome**, not a risk factor.
- In a 1990–2016 meta-analysis, **anticholinergic use was not a validated predictor** of delirium.
- Older adults are especially susceptible. Infants and children are also susceptible, with significant morbidity and mortality. Early and mid adulthood carry lower susceptibility and lower mortality.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues (p. 914)
- **Men** more often show motor agitation and affective lability. **Women** more often show **hypoactive** delirium.
- **Male sex is a risk factor.** Sex/gender factors may interact with other risks.
- Culture: see the race finding under prevalence.

## Diagnostic markers (p. 914)
- Labs reflect the underlying condition or the intoxication/withdrawal state.
- EEG often shows **generalized irregular theta slowing**. Fast activity occasionally appears (e.g., some alcohol withdrawal delirium).
- Without a premorbid alpha baseline, EEG cannot detect slowing unless it falls in the abnormal theta or delta range.

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed.

## Functional consequences (p. 914)
- More functional decline and a higher risk of institutional placement.
- Hospitalized patients aged ≥65 with delirium have worse post-discharge outcomes: mortality, institutionalization and dementia.

## Differential diagnosis (pp. 914–915)
- **Psychotic disorders** (brief psychotic, schizophrenia, schizophreniform) and **mood episodes with psychotic features:** delirium can present with vivid hallucinations, delusions, language disturbance and agitation. Delirium shows impaired attention/awareness, fluctuation and a medical/substance etiology.
- **Acute stress disorder:** fear, anxiety or dissociation (depersonalization) can occur in delirium, but ASD follows a traumatic event, and attention and awareness are not globally impaired.
- **Malingering / factitious disorder:** the presentation is often atypical, and no medical condition or substance accounts for the apparent cognitive disturbance.
- **Other NCDs (the most common dilemma in older adults):** decide between delirium alone, **delirium superimposed on an NCD** (e.g., Alzheimer's), or NCD without delirium. Onset and course are hard to use when a prior NCD went unrecognized or when cognitive impairment persists after a delirium episode. **When delirium and major NCD co-occur, prioritize managing the delirium.**
- **Substance intoxication/withdrawal without delirium:** diagnose delirium only when attention/awareness and cognitive symptoms dominate and need clinical attention.
- **Coma / stupor:** excluded by Criterion D.

## Comorbidity
Often comorbid with, and superimposed on, major or mild NCD.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **Criterion A:** describe attention objectively: cannot do digit span, serial 7s or months backward; questions must be repeated; perseverates; distracted by hallway noise. Describe awareness: misperceives the setting, unaware of the IV, etc. Note the level of consciousness explicitly (alert / drowsy but rousable to voice / stuporous), because coma excludes the diagnosis.
- **Criterion B:** record **baseline** (from family or staff), the onset date/time, and **fluctuation** (e.g., "lucid this morning, disoriented and pulling at lines by evening"). Note any validated screen used (e.g., CAM/CAM-ICU or 4AT; these are clinician tools and are not named in the DSM).
- **Criterion C:** orientation to time/place/person; recent memory; language (disorganized or tangential speech, poor comprehension); perceptual disturbance (visual hallucinations, illusions).
- **Criterion E:** link to the etiology with specifics: labs (Na, glucose, infection markers, LFTs/ammonia, tox screen), medication changes (sedatives, opioids, steroids, anticholinergics), last drink/last dose with withdrawal signs, recent surgery/ICU stay.
- **Specifiers:** document course (acute vs persistent) and activity type. Flag **hypoactive** presentations explicitly so they are not mistaken for depression.
- **Rule-outs to document:** preexisting NCD (record baseline cognition), coma/stupor, primary psychotic or mood disorder, ASD, intoxication/withdrawal without delirium, malingering/factitious.
- **Recording:** follow the name order for substance-related deliriums. For medical etiology, code the medical condition first, then F05.
- **Associated observations:** sleep-wake reversal, nocturnal calling out or agitation, emotional lability, fall risk, need for safety sitter.
