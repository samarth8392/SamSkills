# Major or Mild Neurocognitive Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition

> **Spark note:** This is an attributed NCD with a known medical cause outside the chapter's named etiologies. Coexisting medical illness alone is insufficient; show the physiological and temporal connection. (pp. 975–977)

## Criteria at a glance
**A:** Major/mild NCD. **B:** History, examination or laboratory evidence connects the cognitive syndrome physiologically to a medical condition. **C:** Another mental disorder or a more specific NCD does not better account for the deficits. (p. 975)
## Specifiers & severity
Establish the [major](major-neurocognitive-disorder.md) or [mild](mild-neurocognitive-disorder.md) syndrome first: acquired decline, concern plus objective assessment, functional threshold, and exclusion of delirium-only or another mental disorder. Major NCD severity describes cognitive effects on function: mild = instrumental tasks affected; moderate = basic self-care affected; severe = complete dependence. Record clinically significant behavioral symptoms individually with the with/without behavioral disturbance specifier. These are separate from the severity of the underlying disease. (pp. 916–920)
## Coding
Codes below reproduce this DSM-5-TR edition, not a verification of current billing rules. (pp. 975–976)

| Presentation | Recording order/code |
|---|---|
| Major, without behavioral disturbance | [medical-condition code] specific causative condition, then F02.80 |
| Major, with behavioral disturbance | [medical-condition code] specific causative condition, then F02.81 |
| Mild | G31.84; do not add the etiological medical code |

Record major severity in words; the mild syndrome's behavioral specifier is also recorded without changing its code. Add an appropriate psychiatric diagnosis/code when clinically significant psychiatric symptoms are attributed to the same medical condition; see the relevant phenomenological chapter.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
Potential causes include structural lesions/hydrocephalus, hypoxia, endocrine or metabolic disease, nutritional deficiency, infection, immune disease, hepatic/renal failure, multiple sclerosis, epilepsy, radiation and electrical injury. The causal assessment is strengthened by onset/exacerbation tracking the disease and by stabilization or improvement when the disease is treated. Name the actual condition in the diagnosis. (p. 976)

## Development & course
Course generally tracks the underlying condition: improvement is possible for treatable etiologies; progressive illness may produce advancing deficits. Markers depend on the specific medical condition. No generic minimum duration is provided. (p. 976)

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed.

## Differential diagnosis
Persistent deficits after successful treatment should prompt reconsideration of another or additional etiology. An attributable condition does not exclude simultaneous neurodegeneration. Apply the general delirium and psychiatric exclusions as well. (pp. 916–919, 976–977)

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Document baseline/decline, concern plus quantified cognitive evidence, and degree of independent functioning.
- Name the medical condition and specific history/examination/laboratory evidence supporting a physiological link.
- Describe chronology, treatment response, competing etiologies and follow-up of residual impairment.
