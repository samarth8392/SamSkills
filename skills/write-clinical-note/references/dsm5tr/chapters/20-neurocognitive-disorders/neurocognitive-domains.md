# Neurocognitive Domains (Table 1)

> **Spark note:** All NCD criteria are built on six cognitive domains. Criterion A of major and mild NCD asks for decline in **one or more** of these domains, and the etiological subtypes are defined by **which** domains are hit first and hardest (e.g., memory in Alzheimer's, executive/social cognition in frontotemporal, complex attention/executive in vascular). Use the domain names verbatim in notes. The "major" and "mild" examples below are everyday anchors for the two severity levels. They are not cut-offs. (p. 905)

The table gives each domain a working definition (its subdomains), examples of what people report or what you see at the major and mild levels, and example assessments. Below is a structured paraphrase.

## 1. Complex attention (pp. 905)
**Subdomains:** sustained attention, divided attention, selective attention, processing speed.

| Level | Everyday picture (paraphrased) |
|---|---|
| Major | Overwhelmed when there are many stimuli (TV, radio, talk) and easily pulled off-task. Can attend only if input is cut down and simplified. Cannot hold information just given (a phone number or address, what was just said). Cannot do mental arithmetic. All thinking is slow, and tasks must be reduced to one or a few parts. |
| Mild | Ordinary tasks take longer. Starts to make errors on routine work and needs more double-checking. Thinks better without competing input (radio, TV, other conversations, phone, driving). |

**Assessment examples:**
- *Sustained:* keep responding over time, e.g., press a button for every tone.
- *Selective:* attend despite distractors, e.g., hear a mixed string of letters and numbers and count only the letters.
- *Divided:* do two tasks at once, e.g., tap rapidly while learning a story.
- *Processing speed:* time any task, e.g., block design, symbol–digit matching, counting speed, serial 3s.

## 2. Executive function (pp. 905–906)
**Subdomains:** planning, decision-making, working memory, responding to feedback/error correction, overriding habits/inhibition, mental flexibility.

| Level | Everyday picture |
|---|---|
| Major | Gives up on complex projects. Can manage only one task at a time. Relies on others to plan IADLs and to make decisions. |
| Mild | Multistep projects take more effort. Multitasking is harder, and so is resuming after an interruption (visitor, phone call). May report fatigue from the extra effort of organizing, planning and deciding. Large gatherings feel more taxing or less fun because shifting conversations are hard to follow. |

**Assessment examples:**
- *Planning:* find the way out of a maze; interpret a sequence of pictures or an arrangement of objects.
- *Decision-making:* choose between competing alternatives, e.g., simulated gambling.
- *Working memory:* hold and manipulate information briefly, e.g., add up a list or repeat digits/words backward.
- *Feedback/error utilization:* use feedback to work out the rule for a problem.
- *Inhibition/overriding habits:* choose the harder correct response, e.g., look away from where an arrow points, or name the ink colour instead of reading the word (Stroop-type).
- *Mental flexibility:* shift between concepts or rules (number↔letter, verbal↔key-press, adding↔ordering, sort by size↔by colour).

## 3. Learning and memory (p. 906)
**Subdomains:** immediate memory; recent memory (free recall, cued recall, recognition); very-long-term memory (semantic, autobiographical); implicit learning.

| Level | Everyday picture |
|---|---|
| Major | Repeats self, often within the same conversation. Cannot keep a short shopping list or the day's plans in mind. Needs frequent reminders to stay oriented to the task at hand. |
| Mild | Has trouble recalling recent events and leans more on lists and calendars. Needs occasional reminders or re-reading to follow characters in a film or novel. May repeat self to the same person over a few weeks. Loses track of whether bills have been paid. |

**Key note:** semantic, autobiographical and implicit learning stay relatively intact compared with recent memory, except in severe major NCD.

**Assessment examples:**
- *Immediate memory span:* repeat a list of words or digits. This is sometimes counted as working memory (executive).
- *Recent memory:* encoding of new material (word lists, a short story, diagrams), tested three ways: (1) free recall, (2) cued recall with semantic prompts (e.g., "all the food items"), (3) recognition (e.g., "Was 'apple' on the list?").
- Also assess semantic memory (facts), autobiographical memory (personal events and people) and implicit/procedural learning (skills learned without awareness).
- *Pearl:* if cues and recognition do not help, the encoding deficit is typical of Alzheimer's disease. If cues and recognition do help, suspect a retrieval problem (subcortical/vascular/frontal; see etiology files).

## 4. Language (p. 906)
**Subdomains:** expressive language (naming, word finding, fluency, grammar and syntax) and receptive language.

| Level | Everyday picture |
|---|---|
| Major | Marked expressive or receptive difficulty. Relies on vague fillers ("that thing", "you know what I mean") and on pronouns in place of names. In severe cases, cannot recall the names of close friends or family. Shows idiosyncratic word use, grammatical errors, and reduced spontaneous output and economy of speech. Stereotyped speech appears. Echolalia and automatic speech usually come before mutism. |
| Mild | Noticeable word-finding pauses. Substitutes general words for specific ones. May avoid using acquaintances' names. Makes subtle grammatical slips (omitted or misused articles, prepositions, auxiliary verbs). |

**Assessment examples:**
- *Expressive:* confrontation naming of objects or pictures. Fluency means generating as many words as possible in 1 minute, either by category (semantic, e.g., animals) or by letter (phonemic, e.g., "F").
- *Grammar/syntax:* compare the rate of errors on naming and fluency tasks with norms and with normal slips of the tongue.
- *Receptive:* comprehension (word definitions, pointing to animate and inanimate objects) and carrying out verbal commands.

## 5. Perceptual-motor (pp. 906–907)
**Subdomains:** abilities covered by the terms visual perception, visuoconstructional, perceptual-motor, praxis and gnosis.

| Level | Everyday picture |
|---|---|
| Major | Marked difficulty with once-familiar activities (using tools, driving) and with getting around familiar places. Often more confused at dusk, when shadows and dimmer light change perception. |
| Mild | Relies more on maps or on others for directions. Uses notes or follows others to reach a new place. Gets lost or turned around when not concentrating. Parks less precisely. Spatial tasks (carpentry, assembly, sewing, knitting) take more effort. |

**Assessment examples:**
- *Visual perception:* line bisection detects a basic visual defect or attentional neglect. Motor-free perceptual tasks include face recognition and matching figures. These work best when the figures cannot be named verbally, and some ask whether a figure could be "real" based on its dimensions.
- *Visuoconstructional:* hand–eye assembly tasks such as drawing, copying and block assembly.
- *Perceptual-motor:* combine perception with purposeful movement, e.g., a form board without visual cues or a rapid pegboard.
- *Praxis:* learned movements, e.g., imitate a wave goodbye or pantomime using a hammer on command.
- *Gnosis:* recognition of faces and colours.

## 6. Social cognition (p. 907)
**Subdomains:** recognition of emotions; theory of mind.

| Level | Everyday picture |
|---|---|
| Major | Behaviour is clearly outside social norms: immodest dress, or no sensitivity to norms around political, religious or sexual talk. Fixates on a topic despite the group's disinterest or direct feedback. Acts on intentions without regard for family or friends. Makes unsafe decisions (e.g., clothing wrong for the weather or setting). Usually has little insight into these changes. |
| Mild | Subtle change in behaviour or attitude, often described as a "personality change". Reads social cues or facial expressions less well. Less empathy. More extraverted or more introverted. Less inhibited. Subtle or episodic apathy or restlessness. |

**Assessment examples:**
- *Emotion recognition:* identify positive and negative emotions in pictures of faces.
- *Theory of mind:* infer another person's thoughts, desires or intentions, e.g., story cards asking where a character will look for a lost item or why a character is sad.

## Quick map: which domains each etiology hits first (from the subtype criteria)
| Etiology | Characteristic early domain profile |
|---|---|
| Alzheimer's | Learning and memory (encoding) ± one more domain; nonamnestic variants are visuospatial or logopenic language |
| Frontotemporal, behavioral variant | Social cognition and/or executive; learning, memory and perceptual-motor relatively spared |
| Frontotemporal, language variant | Language (speech production, word finding, naming, grammar, comprehension) |
| Lewy bodies | Attention/executive and visuoperceptual; fluctuating |
| Vascular | Complex attention (incl. processing speed) and frontal-executive |
| Parkinson's / Huntington's / HIV (subcortical) | Processing speed, executive, retrieval memory |
| Prion | Rapid global decline, often with myoclonus/ataxia |
| TBI | Variable; commonly attention, processing speed, executive, memory |

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Name the **domain** affected and give a functional example at the matching level (e.g., "executive function: now needs spouse to manage bills (IADL), consistent with major-level impairment").
- Give **both** sources Criterion A needs: (1) concern from the individual, an informant or a clinician, and (2) objective test results with the instrument named. Where possible, state performance against norms (SD or percentile; see the major/mild NCD files).
- Record whether recall improves with cues or recognition, because this separates encoding from retrieval deficits.
- For social cognition, record collateral report, since insight is often poor at the major level.
- MSE elements by domain:
  - *Attention:* digit span, serial 7s, months backward, distractibility.
  - *Executive:* clock draw, trails, fluency, abstraction, perseveration.
  - *Memory:* 3- to 5-word recall with and without cues.
  - *Language:* naming, fluency, paraphasias, comprehension of multistep commands.
  - *Perceptual-motor:* copying a figure, praxis.
  - *Social cognition:* disinhibited or tactless remarks, empathy, insight.
