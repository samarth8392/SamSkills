# Major or Mild Neurocognitive Disorder Due to HIV Infection

> **Spark note:** HIV infection must be documented, and the cognitive syndrome must be attributable to it. A new abrupt decline demands investigation of delirium, opportunistic infection, or another cause; HIV status alone does not establish HIV-related NCD. (pp. 961–965)

## Criteria at a glance
**A:** Major/mild NCD syndrome. **B:** Established HIV infection. **C:** No better non-HIV explanation, including secondary CNS disease such as progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy or cryptococcal meningitis. **D:** Another medical or mental disorder does not better account for the NCD. (p. 961)
## Specifiers & severity
Establish the [major](major-neurocognitive-disorder.md) or [mild](mild-neurocognitive-disorder.md) syndrome first: acquired decline, concern plus objective assessment, functional threshold, and exclusion of delirium-only or another mental disorder. Major NCD severity describes cognitive effects on function: mild = instrumental tasks affected; moderate = basic self-care affected; severe = complete dependence. Record clinically significant behavioral symptoms individually with the with/without behavioral disturbance specifier. These are separate from the severity of the underlying disease. (pp. 916–920)

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
Often a subcortical pattern: slowed processing, executive/complex-attention difficulty, and impaired acquisition of new material, with relatively better recognition and retention of learned information. Aphasia is less typical, although fluency may decline. Other patterns are possible. HIV tests establish infection, not cognitive causality; MRI/CSF evaluation can help investigate competing causes. (pp. 962–964)

## Associated features
Ataxia, incoordination, and slowing may become prominent at the major level. Older age, educational disadvantage, depression, other substance use, vascular/metabolic comorbidity, earlier immunosuppression, low CD4 nadir, and CNS viral/inflammatory activity are associated with greater impairment. (pp. 962–964)

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- Many reported cognitive abnormalities are asymptomatic neurocognitive impairment; low test performance alone should not automatically be called mild NCD. Samples and diagnostic approaches vary. A cited treated German clinic sample had 20% asymptomatic impairment, 17% mild NCD, and 6% dementia. (pp. 962–963)
- Course may improve, stabilize, fluctuate, or deteriorate. Rapid progression is less common with effective combination antiretroviral treatment; sudden changes require a different-cause workup. (p. 963)
- Childhood perinatal infection may produce developmental delay. Aging and HIV-related disease can combine with other etiologies. Chronic inflammation, comorbid illness, inadequate CNS viral control, and some treatment effects complicate attribution. (pp. 963–964)

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
Educational quality and inequitable access to testing/treatment affect observed differences. The text reports more frequent cognitive impairment among women despite higher U.S. HIV incidence among men; this is not an individual diagnostic rule. (pp. 962–963, 965)

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed in this etiological section; chapter-wide NCD suicide findings are discussed on p. 927.

## Functional consequences
Assess treatment adherence and everyday executive demands directly. Separate cognitive contributions from physical illness and other comorbid disability. (p. 964)

## Differential diagnosis
- **Opportunistic CNS infection/neoplasm, delirium:** particularly important with sudden onset or worsening.
- **Other infections, TBI, substance disorders, neurodevelopmental conditions:** HIV may worsen a preexisting deficit, but show the additional contribution.
- **Alzheimer/vascular NCD:** especially relevant with advancing age and progressive decline. (pp. 964–965)

## Comorbidity
Systemic/CNS inflammation, other infections, substance use and other psychiatric disorders, and vascular/metabolic illnesses can compound impairment. (p. 965)

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Identify confirming HIV records, cognitive baseline, objective domains affected, and degree of independent function.
- Record onset/course, immune/viral history available, treatment context, and differential evaluation of sudden changes.
- State the evidence supporting HIV's contribution rather than assuming all impairment in a person with HIV is caused by HIV.
