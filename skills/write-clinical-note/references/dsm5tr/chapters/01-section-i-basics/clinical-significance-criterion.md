# Clinical Significance Criterion

> **Spark note:** Most criteria sets contain a generic criterion requiring **clinically significant distress or impairment** in social, occupational or other important areas of functioning. It exists because, without biomarkers, many symptoms (especially mild ones) are not pathological in themselves; this criterion is what sets the disorder threshold. It is a hard clinical judgment, especially for role function, and often needs collateral information (pp. 115–116).

## What the text says (paraphrase) (pp. 115–116)
- **Why it exists:** there are no clear biological markers or useful severity measurements for many disorders, so normal and pathological symptom expressions cannot be fully separated. This gap matters most when a symptom (especially a mild one) is not pathological in itself and occurs in people who should not get a diagnosis.
- **Solution:** a generic **distress or disability** criterion sets the threshold. Usual wording: the disturbance causes clinically significant distress **or** impairment in social, occupational or other important areas of functioning.
- Deciding whether it is met, **especially for role function**, is an inherently difficult clinical judgment.
- The criterion is also especially helpful for judging **need for treatment** (linked to the definition of mental disorder, p. 104).
- Information from the **individual plus family/third parties** is often needed, gathered by interview or by self- or informant-report assessments of the person's performance.

## Structure to remember
- **Either/or:** distress **or** impairment is enough. Both are not required.
- **Domains:** social · occupational (includes school/academic) · "other important areas" (e.g., self-care, parenting, household, health management).
- It is used again in the **other specified/unspecified** rule: a subthreshold presentation qualifies for OS/US only if it causes clinically significant distress or impairment (p. 114).

## Pitfalls
- Diagnosing from a symptom count alone without showing distress or impairment.
- Accepting the person's self-report alone when insight is limited; the text endorses collateral and informant reports.
- Mistaking normative distress (e.g., culturally expected grief) for clinically significant distress; see [cultural-considerations.md](cultural-considerations.md).

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **Name the domain and give a concrete example.** "Occupational: missed 6 shifts in past month due to panic attacks; placed on warning." "Social: has stopped attending weekly family dinners." "Other: unable to manage child's school drop-off."
- **Separate distress from impairment.** Record subjective distress (client's words, a 0–10 rating, a standardized measure) and functional change (compared with baseline) as distinct items.
- **Give the baseline comparison.** "Previously worked full-time and socialized weekly; now..."
- **Cite your sources.** Note collateral (spouse, parent, teacher, records) and any standardized measure (e.g., WHODAS 2.0, a disorder-specific severity measure) used to judge role function.
- **If a required distress/impairment criterion is NOT met**, state so explicitly and do not assign that diagnosis. This instruction applies where the particular disorder requires the criterion; it does not add one to every diagnosis. Consider a Z code or "no diagnosis" ("Symptoms present but no clinically significant distress or impairment; criterion not met").
- Use functional language that payers and other clinicians recognize: frequency, duration, specific tasks affected, and safety implications.
