# Cautionary Statement for Forensic Use of DSM-5

> **Spark note:** DSM-5's definition of mental disorder was written for **clinicians, public health and researchers**, not for courts. A DSM diagnosis (even intellectual developmental disorder, schizophrenia, major NCD, gambling disorder or pedophilic disorder) generally does **not** establish a legal "mental illness" or meet a legal standard (competence, criminal responsibility, disability). That requires information about **functional impairments and how they affect the specific abilities in question**. A diagnosis implies **no particular level of risk, impairment or disability**, says nothing necessary about **cause**, and does not show that the person **could not control** their behaviour at a given time (pp. 124–125; see also p. 104).

## Key points (paraphrase)
- **Purpose mismatch:** the definition serves clinical, public health and research needs, not the technical needs of courts and lawyers. DSM-5 also gives **no treatment guidelines** (p. 124).
- **Legitimate forensic value** (p. 124):
  - When a mental disorder is a **predicate** for a legal decision (e.g., **involuntary civil commitment**), an established system improves **reliability**.
  - Helps legal decision-makers understand disorder characteristics.
  - Acts as a **check on ungrounded speculation** about disorders or a person's functioning.
  - **Longitudinal course** information helps when the legal question concerns **past or future** mental functioning.
- **Risks:** misuse or misunderstanding, because of the **imperfect fit** between legal questions and clinical diagnosis (p. 124).
- **Diagnosis ≠ legal standard.** Legal standards usually need **additional information**: functional impairments and how they affect the **particular abilities** in question. Impairment varies widely **within** each diagnosis, so a diagnosis implies **no specific level** of risk, impairment or disability (p. 124).
- **Untrained users:** use by nonclinical, nonmedical or insufficiently trained people to judge whether a disorder is present is **not advised** (p. 124).
- **No necessary implications** about **etiology** or the person's **degree of control** over behaviour. Even when reduced control is a feature of the disorder, the diagnosis alone does **not** show the person was unable to control behaviour **at a particular time** (p. 125).
- Intro restatement: criminal responsibility, disability compensation and competency judgments need information beyond the criteria (p. 104).

## Pitfalls
- Writing that a diagnosis "renders the client incompetent" or "caused the offense".
- Implying a risk level (e.g., violence risk) from the diagnosis alone.
- Letting a records request for court or disability purposes drive diagnostic choices.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **Keep diagnosis and function separate.** Document the diagnosis per DSM-5-TR, then describe **specific functional abilities** with examples (e.g., understanding information, managing finances, sustaining work tasks, appreciating consequences). Do not let the label stand in for function.
- **Avoid legal conclusions** in clinical notes unless you are doing a formal forensic evaluation in that role. Use clinical language ("impaired reality testing during the episode, per MSE and collateral"), not legal terms ("insane", "incompetent", "not responsible").
- **Time-specific statements:** if the legal question is about a past or future time, document what is known about the **course** and the mental state at that time, with sources. Note the limits.
- **Control and etiology:** do not assert inability to control behaviour, or a cause, from the diagnosis. Describe the observed or reported evidence and note the uncertainty.
- **Risk:** use a structured, individualized risk formulation, not a diagnosis-based inference.
- When releasing records for legal or disability use, consider adding a brief note that **DSM diagnoses do not by themselves establish legal standards** and that functional findings are documented separately.
