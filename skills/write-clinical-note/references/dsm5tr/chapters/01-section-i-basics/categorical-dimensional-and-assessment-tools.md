# Categorical vs Dimensional Diagnosis, Assessment Tools, and Suicide Text

> **Spark note:** DSM-5-TR is **primarily categorical with dimensional elements**. It does **not** assume disorders are discrete entities, or that everyone with a diagnosis is alike. Use the **Level 1 Cross-Cutting Symptom Measure** as a "review of mental systems", the **WHODAS** instead of the GAF, and record diagnoses **nonaxially**. The TR added an **"Association With Suicidal Thoughts or Behavior"** section where relevant evidence exists, but risk must be assessed **individually**, not inferred from the diagnosis (pp. 105–106, 111).

## Categorical vs dimensional (p. 105)
- **Problems with pure categories:** high comorbidity, symptom heterogeneity within disorders, heavy use of other specified/unspecified, and genetic/environmental risk data that cut across categories. Boundaries are **more fluid over the life course** than once thought.
- **Dimensional pros:** more reliable; conveys subthreshold information. **Cons:** less familiar and vivid; no agreed optimal dimensions; less useful in practice so far.
- **DSM-5 stance:** primarily **categorical** (for clinical utility and ICD coding compatibility) with **dimensional elements**. No assumption of absolute boundaries between disorders, or between disorder and no disorder. People sharing a diagnosis are **heterogeneous**. **Boundary cases** can only be diagnosed **probabilistically**, so capture clinical information **beyond the diagnosis**.

## Organizational structure (pp. 101–103)
- Chapters were regrouped using **11 validators**: neural substrates, family traits, genetic risk, specific environmental risk, biomarkers, temperamental antecedents, emotional/cognitive processing abnormalities, symptom similarity, course, high comorbidity, shared treatment response.
- **Order:** neurodevelopmental first, then **internalizing** (anxiety, depressive, somatic), **externalizing** (impulsive, disruptive conduct, substance use), **neurocognitive**, and others.
- **Life span ordering:** early-life disorders first (neurodevelopmental, schizophrenia spectrum), then adolescent/young adult (bipolar, depressive, anxiety), then later life (neurocognitive). Within-chapter order follows the same idea where possible.
- Example judgment call: **ADHD** placed in Neurodevelopmental rather than Disruptive/Impulse-Control on the weight of evidence.

## Cross-Cutting Symptom Measures (pp. 94, 106)
- The **DSM-5 Level 1 Cross-Cutting Symptom Measure** (Section III) covers the major domains (mood, psychosis, cognition, personality, sleep, etc.). It works as a **review of mental systems**, like the medical ROS, to uncover latent disorders, atypical/subsyndromal presentations and comorbidity.
- The **APA Practice Guidelines for the Psychiatric Evaluation of Adults** endorse it as a first step.
- Versions: **self**, **parent/guardian** and **child (ages 11–17)**. Free at psychiatry.org/dsm5.
- Cross-cutting and diagnosis-specific severity measures are given at **intake as a baseline**, then repeated to **monitor change** and inform treatment (p. 112).

## Removal of the multiaxial system (p. 106)
| DSM-IV | DSM-5 / DSM-5-TR |
|---|---|
| Axis I, II, III | Listed together, **no axis labels**, typically in **order of clinical importance** |
| Axis IV (psychosocial/environmental) | **Z codes** ("Other Conditions That May Be a Focus of Clinical Attention") listed with diagnoses |
| Axis V (GAF) | **WHODAS** (WHO Disability Assessment Schedule; based on the ICF), in Section III "Assessment Measures" |

## Association With Suicidal Thoughts or Behavior (TR addition) (p. 111)
- **New text section** in each disorder where the literature supports it. It gives disorder-specific prevalence of suicidal thoughts/behaviour and associated risk factors (p. 120).
- Within one diagnosis, suicide-relevant psychopathology ranges from **none to severe**. **Do not rely on the diagnosis alone.** Use clinical judgment informed by known risk factors.
- Treat the section as an **alert to inquire further**. Real risk assessment is individualized and goes **well beyond** DSM diagnosis.

## Section III resources (pp. 111–112)
- **Conditions for Further Study:** proposed conditions that lack the evidence for routine clinical use. They are included to spur research and can serve as **other specified** examples.
- Section III assessment tools, the **CFI** and the proposed criteria need **more evidence** before widespread clinical use. Section II criteria, by contrast, are well established.

## Pitfalls
- Recording a GAF score or axes in new notes.
- Assuming two patients with the same diagnosis need the same plan; record dimensional severity and features.
- Writing "low risk: diagnosis not associated with suicide", or the reverse. Risk is individual.
- Treating Section III conditions (e.g., attenuated psychosis syndrome) as official diagnoses. Check each condition's research status and the established category, if any, that can describe the actual clinical presentation; research criteria do not automatically establish a diagnosis or code.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **Intake:** record Level 1 CCSM results (domains above threshold) and the **Level 2** or disorder-specific measures used to follow up. Give **baseline scores** for later comparison.
- **Function:** record WHODAS 2.0 scores (or domain-level descriptions) in place of GAF.
- **Severity and heterogeneity:** add dimensional ratings (e.g., severity measure scores, specifiers) so the diagnosis is not the only descriptor.
- **Suicide risk:** document **individual** risk and protective factors, current ideation/intent/plan, history and access to means. You may note that the diagnosis carries an elevated base-rate association, as context only.
- **Progress notes:** repeat the measures at intervals and record the change from baseline to justify continued care or a change in level of care.
