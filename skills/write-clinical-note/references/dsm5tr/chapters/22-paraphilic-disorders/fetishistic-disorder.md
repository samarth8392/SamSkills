# Fetishistic Disorder

> **Spark note:** A persistent sexual focus on objects or a very specific nongenital body part is diagnostic only with significant distress/impairment. An accommodated sexual preference, use of a purpose-built sex toy, or clothing used exclusively for arousing cross-dressing does not by itself establish this disorder. (pp. 1057–1059)

## Criteria at a glance
- **A:** ≥6 months of repeated, intense arousal involving nonliving objects or a highly specific nongenital body-part focus; evidenced by fantasy, urges, or behavior.
- **B:** Clinically significant distress or impairment in an important area.
- **C:** Objects are not solely clothing used for cross-dressing and are not devices designed for direct tactile genital stimulation. (p. 1057)

## Specifiers & severity
- Specify **Body part(s)**, **Nonliving object(s)**, **Other**; combinations can apply, such as both feet and footwear.
- **In a controlled environment:** opportunities are limited by the setting.
- **In full remission:** ≥5 years free of significant distress/impairment in an uncontrolled setting; no requirement that the interest disappear. No graded severity anchors are given. (p. 1057)

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Focus may involve clothing/materials, shoes, or selected body parts. Partialism falls within this category; it was classified under paraphilia NOS in DSM-IV-TR.
- Many people with fetishistic interests have no significant impairment and enjoy sex without the object. The object may enhance satisfaction; only a minority require it for arousal/satisfaction.
- Neither collecting objects nor unconventional sexual interests establishes B. (pp. 1057–1058)

## Associated features
The object can be involved through multiple sensory modalities or through a partner's use of it. Extensive collections may occur. (p. 1058)

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
No population prevalence estimate is supplied. Interests can begin before adolescence; paraphilias often emerge around puberty. Established disorder tends to persist with variation in urge/behavior intensity and frequency. (p. 1058)

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
Assess cultural sexual norms and individual context. Clinical disorder samples are almost entirely male; behavior also occurs in women, and fantasy shows a smaller sex difference. (p. 1058)

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed.

## Functional consequences
Absent preferred objects/body-part involvement may interfere with partnered arousal or sexual functioning. Some prefer solitary fetish-associated sex despite an affectionate reciprocal relationship; preference alone is insufficient without B. (pp. 1058–1059)

## Differential diagnosis
- **Transvestic disorder:** clothing exclusively used for arousing cross-dressing belongs there rather than automatically here; establish whether arousal is to the material/object or to dressing.
- **Purpose-built genital-stimulation devices:** excluded by C.
- **Sexual masochism disorder:** if forced-dressing fantasy chiefly arouses through domination/humiliation and causes distress/impairment, assess masochism.
- **Fetishism without disorder:** no significant distress/functional impairment means B is absent even with frequent consensual or solitary behavior. (p. 1059)

## Comorbidity
Other paraphilic disorders and hypersexuality can co-occur; neurological conditions are rarely associated. (p. 1059)

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Record specific arousal focus, fantasy/urge/behavior pattern, and ≥6-month duration (A).
- Establish impairment or distress in concrete terms (B), including whether preferred stimuli are obligatory or merely preferred.
- Document C exclusions, consent/context, culturally informed interpretation, and the predominant motivation in overlapping dressing/humiliation presentations.
- Specify combined foci, actual setting restrictions, and remission interval only when assessed.
