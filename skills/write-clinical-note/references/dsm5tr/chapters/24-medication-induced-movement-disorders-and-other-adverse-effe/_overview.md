# Medication-Induced Movement Disorders & Other Adverse Effects — Overview

> **Spark note:** These are **not mental disorders**. They are in Section II because they come up constantly when mental disorders are treated with medication and because they mimic psychiatric symptoms: akathisia vs. anxiety/agitation, NMS vs. malignant catatonia, MIP vs. depression or negative symptoms, TD vs. chorea. The most useful discriminator is **timing relative to starting, raising, lowering or stopping a medication**. Causality is often hard to prove because all of these also occur without drug exposure (p. 1069).

## What unites the chapter
- Usual culprits are **antipsychotics and other dopamine receptor blocking agents (DRBAs)**. The TR prefers this term to "neuroleptic," which is becoming outdated (p. 1069).
  - First-generation: chlorpromazine, haloperidol, fluphenazine
  - Second-generation: clozapine, risperidone, olanzapine, quetiapine
  - Antiemetic/GI DRBAs: prochlorperazine, promethazine, trimethobenzamide, thiethylperazine, metoclopramide
  - Amoxapine (an antidepressant)
- Newer agents lower the risk but don't remove it.

## Timing ladder (the fastest discriminator)
| Condition | Typical onset after start/increase | Key feature |
|---|---|---|
| Acute dystonia | ≥50% within 24-48 h; ~90% within 5 days | Sustained contractions/postures |
| NMS | Exposure within 72 h before sx; most in wk 1; nearly all ≤30 days | Fever + rigidity + AMS + autonomic instability + CK |
| Acute akathisia | Within 4 wk (often rapid) | Subjective restlessness + observed movement |
| Parkinsonism (MIP) | 2-4 wk, usually by 3 mo (CCBs: 2nd peak ~1 yr) | Bradykinesia, rigidity, 3-6 Hz rest tremor |
| Postural tremor | Varies with drug (lithium most studied) | Fine 8-12 Hz tremor with sustained posture |
| Tardive dyskinesia | ≥3 mo exposure (≥1 mo if age ≥60); can appear ≤4 wk after stopping oral (≤8 wk after stopping LAI) | Choreiform/athetoid/semirhythmic, ≥4 wk |
| Tardive dystonia/akathisia | Late in treatment; may persist months-years | Persists despite stopping or lowering the drug |
| Antidepressant discontinuation | 2-4 days after last dose | Dizziness, "electric shocks," insomnia, anxiety; ≤2-3 wk |

## Notable points / TR changes in the text
- Terminology moves from "neuroleptic" to **"antipsychotic medications and other dopamine receptor blocking agents."** The code title "Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome" is kept.
- Parkinsonism is split by agent: **G21.11** (antipsychotic/DRBA) vs. **G21.19** (other medication).
- Tardive dystonia (G24.09) and tardive akathisia are listed together. **Tardive akathisia uses G25.71, the same code as acute akathisia.** The written name tells them apart.
- Malignant catatonia is named as an NMS look-alike; some regard NMS as a drug-induced form of it.
- T codes take a 7th character: **A** initial, **D** subsequent, **S** sequelae.

## Chapter-level pitfalls & differential logic
- **Akathisia vs. anxiety/agitation/mania/ADHD:** new restlessness soon after a dose increase that worsens with more drug and improves with dose reduction or anti-akathisia treatment points to akathisia. Mistaking it for psychosis or agitation and raising the dose makes it worse. It is linked to suicide attempts.
- **MIP vs. depression/negative symptoms/catatonia:** look for rigidity on exam (lead-pipe/cogwheel), rest tremor and timing. MDD shows vegetative sx and hopelessness.
- **MIP vs. TD:** this matters for treatment. Anticholinergics (for MIP) can worsen TD; VMAT2 inhibitors (for TD) can worsen MIP. Raising the antipsychotic worsens akathisia and MIP but may temporarily mask TD.
- **NMS vs. serotonin syndrome, malignant catatonia, malignant hyperthermia, withdrawal, anticholinergic toxicity, infection:** NMS = DRBA exposure + fever + lead-pipe rigidity + AMS + autonomic instability + high CK.
- **Acute dystonia vs. catatonia:** people with acute dystonia commonly express distress and ask for help; those with retarded catatonia are typically mute and withdrawn and may not express subjective distress. This concerns expression, not proof of an absence of distress.
- **Discontinuation vs. relapse:** discontinuation begins within days of stopping, is somatic (dizziness, shocks) and resolves in ≤2-3 wk. Relapse is gradual and resembles the original episode.

## Coding conventions
- G codes need no encounter character. T43.205_ and T50.905_ need A/D/S.
- Record the condition alongside the mental disorder being treated. These codes don't replace that dx.
- "Other adverse effect of medication" covers **non-movement** side effects that become a main clinical focus.

## Documentation cues (chapter-wide)
- Medication timeline: agent, dose, route (oral vs. LAI), date started or changed, date of symptom onset.
- Observed exam findings, not just "EPS" (tremor frequency/at rest vs. posture, rigidity type, gait, orofacial movements). Name the rating scale if one was used (e.g., AIMS, BARS, SAS).
- Response to dose change or corrective agent.
- Rule-outs considered (primary neuro disease, psychiatric agitation, catatonia, withdrawal).
- Effect on adherence and safety (falls, aspiration, suicidality with akathisia).
