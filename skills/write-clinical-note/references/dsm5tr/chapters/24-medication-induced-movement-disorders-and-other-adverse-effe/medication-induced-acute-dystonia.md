# Medication-Induced Acute Dystonia

> **Spark note:** These are sustained, often frightening muscle contractions (neck twisting, eyes locked upward, tongue protrusion, jaw clenching) beginning **within hours to days** of starting or raising a D2 blocker, or after cutting an anticholinergic. **Laryngeal dystonia can obstruct the airway.** The mistake is to read it as psychotic posturing or catatonia and **raise** the offending drug (p. 1074-1076).

## Criteria at a glance (structured from text)
- Sustained abnormal contractions (↑ tone) and postures linked to a medication known to cause acute dystonia.
- Any **D2-like blocker** can cause it. Most often antipsychotics and antiemetic/promotility agents. Also reported with SSRIs, cholinesterase inhibitors, opioids, methylphenidate.
- **Timing:** ≥50% within **24-48 h** of starting or rapidly raising a DRBA, or of **reducing** an EPS-preventing drug (e.g., anticholinergic). ~**90% within 5 days**.
- Not better explained by a mental disorder (e.g., catatonia), a primary neuro/medical condition, or a tardive movement disorder.

## Presentations
- Focal, segmental or generalized. Head/neck most common, but can extend to limbs or trunk.
- **Oromandibular:** tongue protrusion, gaping, grimacing → dysarthria, dysphagia, trismus.
- **Oculogyric crisis:** forced conjugate eye deviation (up/down/sideways) lasting minutes to hours. **Blepharospasm.**
- **Cervical:** antecollis, retrocollis, laterocollis, torticollis.
- **Limb** (distal > proximal); **Pisa syndrome** (lateral trunk lean); back arching → **opisthotonos**.
- **Laryngeal (life-threatening):** throat clutching, stridor, dysphonia, dysphagia, dyspnea.

## Associated features · Risk
- Fear and anxiety are common, plus muscle pain/cramps. Unwarned patients are especially distressed, which raises the risk of **later nonadherence**.
- In psychosis, the dystonia may be mistaken for psychiatric sx → dose increase.
- Risk: **children and adults <40 with psychosis**, **males**, prior ADR, **high-potency typical antipsychotics**.

## Differential diagnosis
- **Primary neuro dystonia:** precedes the drug or progresses without a med change; focal signs; family hx. Idiopathic focal/segmental dystonias persist for days to weeks regardless of medication.
- **Tardive dystonia:** not acute; may emerge when the antipsychotic dose is **lowered**.
- Seizures, infections, trauma, CNS/PNS lesions, **hypoparathyroidism (tetany)**, **anaphylaxis**, tardive laryngeal dystonia, respiratory dyskinesia.
- **NMS:** dystonia plus **fever and generalized rigidity**.
- **Catatonia:** symptoms preceding antipsychotic exposure and lack of improvement with dose reduction or anticholinergic support an alternative explanation. People with retarded catatonia are typically mute and withdrawn and may **not express subjective distress**, whereas those with acute dystonia commonly express distress and seek help. Lack of expressed distress does not establish absence of internal distress.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Exact posture and body region (e.g., "sustained upward eye deviation ×40 min"; "neck rotated L, unable to correct voluntarily").
- Airway status: voice, stridor, swallowing, breathing. Escalate immediately if laryngeal signs appear.
- Med timeline: DRBA/antiemetic start or dose increase within hours to days; any anticholinergic taper.
- Subjective distress and help-seeking (distinguishes from catatonia).
- Response to intervention as given by the prescriber, and psychoeducation provided (lowers future nonadherence).
