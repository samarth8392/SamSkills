# Medication-Induced Parkinsonism

> **Spark note:** After Parkinson's disease, MIP is the most common cause of parkinsonism. It causes disability, falls and nonadherence. **Bilateral parkinsonism appearing subacutely within weeks of starting or raising a D2 blocker strongly suggests MIP.** Clinically it can't be reliably told apart from PD, and it is commonly misread as depression or negative symptoms (p. 1069-1070).

## Criteria at a glance (structured from text)
- Parkinsonian signs/sx: tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia/akinesia. Rigidity and bradykinesia are more common, and tremor may be absent.
- **Temporal link** to medication: weeks after starting or raising a causative agent, or after **reducing** an anticholinergic/antiparkinsonian drug used to treat or prevent EPS.
  - Antipsychotics/DRBAs: typically **2-4 wk**, usually **by 3 mo**. Onset can be rapid, or insidious after months.
  - Calcium channel blockers: second onset peak around **1 yr**.
- Not better accounted for by: psychiatric phenomena (catatonia, negative sx, MDD psychomotor retardation), another non-parkinsonian medication-induced movement disorder, another neuro/medical condition (PD, Wilson's), or **antipsychotic-exacerbated PD**.
- Signs are often symmetric. Asymmetry does **not** exclude MIP.

**Causative agents:** high-potency D2 antipsychotics carry the highest rates (haloperidol, fluphenazine, risperidone), with no difference in clinical features between FGA and SGA. Other agents: CCBs (flunarizine, cinnarizine), dopamine depleters (reserpine, tetrabenazine), antiepileptics (phenytoin, valproate, levetiracetam), antidepressants (SSRIs, MAOIs), lithium, chemotherapy (cytosine arabinoside, cyclophosphamide, vincristine, doxorubicin, paclitaxel, etoposide), immunosuppressants (cyclosporine, tacrolimus). Toxins: MPTP, organophosphates, manganese, methanol, cyanide, CO, carbon disulfide.

## Clinical features & pearls
- **Tremor:** "pill-rolling," 3-6 Hz, steady, present **at rest** and suppressed by purposeful use of the limb. It may be intermittent, unilateral or bilateral, or positional. Can involve the jaw, lips ("**rabbit syndrome**") or tongue. Worse with anxiety, stress or fatigue.
- **Rigidity:** lead-pipe = constant through the range of motion. Cogwheel = ratchet-like (tremor superimposed), most often at wrist/elbow. Patients report stiffness, aching, muscle/joint pain or poor coordination.
- **Bradykinesia/akinesia:** global slowing, trouble initiating movement, reduced grooming. Patients describe listlessness, low drive or fatigue, which **mimics depression or negative symptoms**.
- **Gait/posture:** short stride, reduced arm swing, stooped posture, shuffling, staring face.
- Other signs: micrographia, hypophonia, reduced dexterity, dysphagia, decreased gag reflex, postural instability, reduced blinking, seborrhea, drooling (less common with antipsychotics because of their anticholinergic effects).
- Severe cases: contractures, bedsores, PE, incontinence, aspiration pneumonia, weight loss, hip fracture.

## Prevalence · Course · Risk
- At least **50%** of long-term outpatients on typical antipsychotics develop parkinsonian signs at some point. The rate is underrecognized and often misattributed to PD or psychiatric illness.
- Linked to gait dysfunction, falls and nursing home placement, making it a serious iatrogenic problem in older adults.
- Risk factors: female sex, older age, cognitive impairment, other neuro conditions, HIV, family hx PD, severe psychiatric illness. **Anticholinergics reduce risk.**
- Associated behavioral sx: depression, worsened negative symptoms.

## Differential diagnosis
- **PD / Parkinson-plus (MSA, PSP, Wilson's):** ≥3 cardinal features, hyposmia, REM sleep behavior disorder, autonomic/urinary sx, unilateral asymmetric onset. Primary neuro patients also worsen on MIP-causing drugs.
- **Non-parkinsonian tremor:** finer and faster (~10 Hz), worse on intention.
- **Substance withdrawal:** hyperreflexia, autonomic signs.
- **Cerebellar disease:** intention tremor, nystagmus, ataxia, scanning speech.
- **TD:** choreiform, not steadily rhythmic.
- **Stroke/CNS lesion:** focal signs, weakness, clasp-knife spasticity. MIP has lead-pipe rigidity with **normal strength**.
- Red flags for an alternative cause: family hx of inherited neuro disease, rapid progression without med change, focal signs (frontal release, cranial nerve findings, Babinski).
- **NMS:** severe rigidity/akinesia **plus** fever and high CK.
- **MDD:** vegetative signs (early-morning awakening), hopelessness, despair.
- **Negative sx / catatonia:** hard to separate. In psychiatric rigidity, resistance is **inconsistent** across range of motion, whereas parkinsonian rigidity is constant. Rigidity can also occur in psychosis, delirium, major NCD, anxiety, and FND.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Med timeline: agent (DRBA vs. other → G21.11 vs. G21.19), dose, date started/increased, any anticholinergic taper; date of sx onset.
- Observed: rest tremor (body part, laterality), rigidity type, bradykinesia (slow finger taps, delayed initiation), gait (shuffling, decreased arm swing), masked facies, hypophonia, micrographia, drooling. Note the scale used (e.g., Simpson-Angus).
- Separate it from depression/negative sx: "psychomotor slowing with cogwheel rigidity R>L wrist; denies hopelessness, sleep/appetite intact."
- Functional/safety impact: falls, dysphagia/aspiration risk, ADL decline, adherence concerns.
- Plan: prescriber notified, dose change or corrective agent, reassessment date.
