# Gender Dysphoria in Children

> **Spark note:** For prepubertal children: marked incongruence between experienced/expressed gender and assigned gender for ≥6 months, shown by ≥6 of 8 concrete, behavioral indicators. **A1, a strong desire to be, or insistence on being, another gender, is required.** Distress or impairment must be present. Gender-nonconforming play or dress alone (e.g., "tomboy" behavior) is not gender dysphoria. In supportive settings distress may be absent, and it often appears only when others interfere.

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** Marked incongruence between experienced/expressed and assigned gender, **≥6 months**, with **≥6 of 8** (must include **A1**):
  1. Strong desire to be another gender, or insistence that one is another gender (or an alternative gender different from assigned gender). **[Required]**
  2. Strong preference for clothing typical of the experienced gender: for children assigned male, cross-dressing or simulating female attire; for children assigned female, wearing only typically masculine clothing with strong resistance to typically feminine clothing.
  3. Strong preference for cross-gender roles in make-believe/fantasy play.
  4. Strong preference for toys, games or activities stereotypically used by another gender.
  5. Strong preference for playmates of another gender.
  6. Strong rejection of toys/games/activities typical of assigned gender (for children assigned male, also strong avoidance of rough-and-tumble play).
  7. Strong dislike of one's sexual anatomy.
  8. Strong desire for primary and/or secondary sex characteristics matching experienced gender.
- **B.** Clinically significant distress or impairment in social, **school** or other important functioning.

## Specifiers & severity
- **With a disorder/difference of sex development** (e.g., E25.0 congenital adrenal hyperplasia, E34.50 androgen insensitivity syndrome) — use only with a specific, codable DSD documented in the medical record.
- No severity or posttransition specifier for the child criteria.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Core: incongruence between assigned and experienced gender, plus distress. Experienced gender may be binary, intermediate or alternative.
- Examples may be less prominent in children raised with fewer gender stereotypes.
- **Children assigned female at birth:** persistent conviction of being a boy, aversion to being a girl, asserting they'll grow up to be a man; prefer boys' clothes/hairstyles, may be seen as boys by strangers, may ask for a boy's name; intense reactions to dresses; may refuse school/events requiring them; prefer contact sports, rough-and-tumble play and boy playmates; little interest in dolls; occasionally refuse to urinate sitting; may wish for or claim to have/grow a penis; may not want breasts or menstruation.
- **Children assigned male at birth:** persistent conviction of being a girl or growing up to be a woman, aversion to being a boy; prefer girls'/women's clothing or improvise (towels, aprons, scarves for long hair or skirts); role-play female figures (e.g., "mother"), interest in female fantasy characters; prefer stereotypically feminine games, dolls, girl playmates; avoid rough-and-tumble play; may find penis/testes disgusting, wish them removed, or wish for a vagina.
- Increasingly, families present after the child has **already socially transitioned**.
- Puberty onset: AFAB ~9–13, AMAB ~11–14, so concerns may emerge in the transition to adolescence (see adolescent/adult file for younger adolescents).

## Associated features
- Increasing age in prepubertal children is associated with more behavioral/emotional problems, related to growing **nonacceptance by others**.
- Children who feel supported and accepted may show few or no psychological problems.
- Autism spectrum traits overrepresented; autistic individuals more likely to show gender diversity.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- No large population studies; treatment-seeking-based prevalence <0.1% (see adolescent/adult file for survey data).
- **Onset:** gender-nonconforming behavior usually begins at **2–4 years** (clinic samples in Canada and the Netherlands), when most children begin gendered behaviors; for some, onset is at school entry.
- **Anatomic dysphoria** increases as puberty approaches.
- Criteria are more concrete/behavioral than adult criteria; young children are less likely to express extreme persistent anatomic dysphoria.
- **Distress** may appear only when parents/others say the child is "really" not another gender (e.g., intense crying), and may be absent in supportive environments.
- **Persistence into adolescence/adulthood:** the source reports AMAB 2–39% and AFAB 12–50% in studies from North America/the Netherlands, while explicitly noting that **general-population outcome studies of childhood gender variance do not exist** (p. 719). These heterogeneous study figures are not individual forecasts or general-population probabilities. Persistence is modestly correlated with baseline severity; early social transition is described as a possible factor, not an established causal effect.
- Some children whose dysphoria remits do not seek gender-affirming treatment at puberty; they frequently report nonheterosexual orientation and may stay gender nonconforming without transgender identity. Some remitted cases recur in adulthood.
- Sexual orientation: high rate of attraction to people of the child's birth-assigned gender regardless of trajectory. If dysphoria persists, most identify as heterosexual (relative to experienced gender); if it desists, most identify as gay, lesbian or bisexual.
- Prepubertal-onset dysphoria can persist, desist or be denied (sometimes identifying as gay/lesbian or as heterosexual cisgender), with possible later recurrence. Onset of puberty, or anticipating it, can intensify dysphoria.
- Early-onset group often presents for care in childhood, adolescence or young adulthood, possibly reflecting more intense dysphoria.
- **With DSD:** often come to attention early (gender assignment decisions, gonadectomy, induced puberty, infertility, early genital surgery). Gender-nonconforming behavior may follow syndrome-specific patterns; thresholds for supporting social/medical transition in minors have traditionally been lower with DSDs. Many experience gender **uncertainty** rather than firm conviction; rates vary by syndrome and assignment.
- **Temperamental:** greater intensity of nonconformity and **older age at presentation** → more likely persistence.
- **Environmental:** AMAB children (without DSD) more often have older brothers.
- **Genetic/physiological:** weak familiality, higher MZ than DZ twin concordance, some heritability; polygenic basis suggested. DSD-related risk rises with grossly atypical prenatal androgen exposure relative to assigned gender (e.g., 46,XY cloacal exstrophy or penile agenesis assigned female; 5-α-reductase-2 or 17-β-HSD-3 deficiency raised female; 46,XX CAH with poor glucocorticoid adherence). Prenatal androgens relate more to gendered **behavior** than to identity, so **nonconforming behavior alone should not be read as current or future dysphoria**.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- Reported worldwide, including cultures with institutionalized gender categories beyond man/woman (India, Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Oman, Samoa, Thailand, Indigenous Peoples of North America); unclear whether criteria are met there.
- Co-occurring problems vary with cultural attitudes; anxiety relatively common even in accepting cultures.
- Child referral sex ratio AMAB:AFAB **1.25:1 to 4.3:1**. Rising referrals, younger presentation, more early social transition.

## Suicide risk
- Canadian study of **572** gender-referred children vs comparison groups: **8.6×** more likely to self-harm or attempt suicide, even after adjusting for behavior and peer problems, particularly in the second half of childhood.
- Transgender individuals overall: suicidality/attempt rates of 30–80%; risk factors include past maltreatment, gender-based victimization, depression, substance use, younger age.

## Functional consequences
- Nonconformity may appear after the first 2–3 years and interfere with daily activities.
- Older children: peer problems, isolation, distress; teasing, harassment, pressure to wear clothing of birth-assigned gender, especially in nonsupportive environments.
- Impairment may include school refusal, depression, anxiety, peer and behavioral problems.

## Differential diagnosis
- **Nonconformity to gender roles:** distinguished by the **strong desire to be another gender** and the extent/pervasiveness of gender-variant interests; not meant to label "tomboyism" or "girly-boy" behavior.
- **Autism spectrum disorder:** concrete, rigid thinking about gender roles can be hard to distinguish from co-occurring dysphoria.
- **Body dysmorphic disorder:** focus on a body part perceived as defective, not as a marker of assigned gender.
- **Psychotic disorders:** rare in children; gender beliefs without psychosis are not delusions.

## Comorbidity
- Clinically referred children: elevated anxiety, disruptive/impulse-control and depressive disorders.
- ASD traits overrepresented. PTSD after harassment/violence.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **A1 (required):** record the child's own statements (e.g., "says repeatedly 'I am a girl,' asks to be called [name]") and their persistence/consistency over ≥6 months.
- **A2–A8:** record observed/reported clothing preferences, pretend-play roles, toy/activity and playmate choices, rejection of activities, statements about anatomy or wished-for body characteristics; count ≥6 total.
- **B:** distress (crying, anger when misgendered or made to wear certain clothing) or impairment (school refusal, peer isolation); note whether distress emerges mainly with interference or rejection.
- **Context:** family acceptance, social transition status, school climate, bullying, siblings (birth order), DSD history from medical record.
- **Rule-outs / co-occurring:** distinguish from gender-nonconforming play alone; screen ASD traits, anxiety, depression, disruptive behavior; trauma/victimization.
- **Safety:** screen self-harm and suicidal thoughts, especially in older children and those experiencing rejection.
- Use the child's name and pronouns as they and the family indicate; record assigned gender as AFAB/AMAB.
