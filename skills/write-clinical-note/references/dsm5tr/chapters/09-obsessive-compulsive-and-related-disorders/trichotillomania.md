# Trichotillomania (Hair-Pulling Disorder)

> **Spark note:** Recurrent pulling of one's own hair causing hair loss, with repeated failed
> attempts to cut down, and distress or impairment. Pulling is **not driven by obsessions** and is
> not cosmetic. Anxiety, boredom or tension may come first, and relief or gratification may follow.
> Hair loss may not be obvious because people pull diffusely or camouflage it. Most patients admit
> to pulling, but a patient who denies it needs a dermatological work-up. (pp. 430–432)

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** Recurrent pulling out of one's own hair, **resulting in hair loss**.
- **B.** **Repeated attempts to decrease or stop** pulling.
- **C.** Clinically significant distress or impairment.
- **D.** Pulling or hair loss not attributable to another medical condition, e.g., a
  dermatological condition.
- **E.** Not better explained by another mental disorder, e.g., trying to fix a perceived
  appearance flaw in BDD.

## Specifiers & severity
No specifiers or severity ratings. The text describes **focused** vs. **automatic** pulling styles
descriptively, and many people use a mix.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- **Sites:**
  - most common: **scalp, eyebrows, eyelids/eyelashes**
  - less common: axillary, facial, pubic, perirectal
  - sites may change over time
- **Pattern:** brief episodes scattered through the day, or longer sessions lasting hours. It may
  persist for months or years.
- **Hair loss** may not be clearly visible when single hairs are pulled across a whole site.
  Concealment is common: makeup, scarves, wigs.
- **"Distress"** includes feeling a loss of control, embarrassment and shame.
- Impairment comes partly from avoiding work, school and public places.

## Associated features
- **Hair rituals:**
  - choosing hairs by texture or color; trying to get the root out intact
  - examining the hair, rolling it, pulling it through the teeth, biting it
  - **swallowing it (trichophagia)**
- **Triggers and affect:** anxiety or boredom; rising tension before pulling or while resisting;
  gratification, pleasure or relief afterward.
- **Focused pulling** comes with tension and relief. **Automatic pulling** happens without full
  awareness.
- Some feel an itch-like or tingling scalp sensation that pulling relieves. **Pain is usually
  absent.**
- **Hair-loss patterns:**
  - patchy alopecia or thinning
  - crown or parietal predilection on the scalp
  - **"tonsure trichotillomania"**: near-baldness with a spared outer rim, especially at the nape
  - eyebrows or eyelashes may be completely absent
- **Concealment:**
  - Pulling usually happens alone or only in front of immediate family.
  - Some urge to pull others' hair and do it surreptitiously.
  - Some pull from pets, dolls or fibers (sweaters, carpets).
  - Some deny pulling.
- **Most have other BFRBs:** skin picking, nail biting, lip chewing.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
**Prevalence**
- 12-month estimates of **1–2%** in adults and adolescents (nonrepresentative US samples).
- **Female:male ratio ≥10:1** in clinical samples but **~2:1 in the community**. More equal in
  children.
- Representative US online survey (n>10,000): **1.7%** current, with no sex difference (men 1.8%,
  women 1.7%).

**Development and course**
- Hair pulling in infants usually resolves.
- **Onset typically coincides with or follows puberty.**
- Untreated course is chronic and waxing-waning; it may come and go over weeks to years.
- May worsen **premenstrually**, but not consistently in pregnancy.
- A minority remit without relapse within a few years of onset.

**Genetic risk:** there is evidence of genetic vulnerability. TTM is more common in people with OCD
and their first-degree relatives.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- Similar presentation across cultures, though non-Western data are scarce.
- Sex ratios are given under Prevalence.

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed.

## Functional consequences
- Distress and social and occupational impairment.
- Possible **irreversible damage** to hair growth and quality.
- **Infrequent medical consequences:**
  - digit purpura; musculoskeletal injury (carpal tunnel; back, shoulder or neck pain)
  - blepharitis; dental damage from biting hair
- **Trichophagia → trichobezoar**, which can cause anemia, abdominal pain, hematemesis,
  nausea/vomiting, **bowel obstruction or perforation**.

## Diagnostic markers
- Biopsy is rarely needed because most patients admit to pulling.
- **Skin biopsy and dermoscopy/trichoscopy** can tell TTM from other alopecias. Dermoscopy shows
  reduced density, short vellus hairs, and broken hairs of varying shaft lengths.

## Differential diagnosis
- **Normative hair removal or manipulation:**
  - Purely cosmetic removal does not count.
  - Twisting or playing with hair usually does not count.
  - **Biting hair rather than pulling it does not qualify.**
- **OCD:** pulling as part of symmetry rituals → OCD, not TTM.
- **BDD:** removing hair seen as ugly, asymmetric or abnormal → BDD, not TTM.
- **Stereotypic movement disorder:** pulling as a stereotypy, e.g., in intellectual disability or
  ASD when frustrated or excited, alongside head banging or biting → stereotypic movement disorder.
- **Psychotic disorder:** pulling in response to a delusion or hallucination → not TTM.
- **Another medical condition:**
  - Rule out skin inflammation and other dermatological causes.
  - **Nonscarring alopecia:** alopecia areata, androgenic alopecia, telogen effluvium.
  - **Scarring alopecia:** discoid lupus, lichen planopilaris, central centrifugal cicatricial
    alopecia, pseudopelade, folliculitis decalvans, dissecting folliculitis, acne keloidalis nuchae.
  - Consider these especially when the patient **denies pulling**. Use biopsy or dermoscopy.
- **Substance-related:** stimulants can worsen pulling but are unlikely to be the primary cause of
  persistent pulling.

## Comorbidity
- Most commonly **MDD** and **excoriation disorder**.
- Other BFRBs such as nail biting occur in the **majority**. They may warrant an extra diagnosis of
  **other specified OCRD (other body-focused repetitive behavior disorder)**.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)

Use examples only when supported by the actual assessment. Attribute client/collateral reports, distinguish observations from interpretations, and leave unassessed findings unknown.
- **Criterion A:**
  - Record sites, frequency and duration of episodes.
  - Note visible hair loss on exam (patches, thinning, missing lashes or brows, tonsure pattern) or
    pulling the patient reports.
  - Note any camouflage (wig, scarf, makeup).
- **Criterion B:** document specific attempts to stop (barriers, gloves, prior treatment) and that
  they failed.
- **Criterion C:** shame, loss of control, avoidance (swimming, haircuts, intimacy, school or
  work).
- **Pulling style:** focused (urge, tension, relief) vs. automatic (reading, TV, driving), plus
  triggers (boredom, anxiety, stress).
- **Rituals and trichophagia:** ask directly. If the patient swallows hair, document GI symptoms and
  flag for medical evaluation (trichobezoar).
- **Rule-outs:**
  - pulling is not cosmetic (BDD), not a symmetry ritual (OCD), not psychotic
  - not a developmental stereotypy
  - a dermatology work-up if pulling is denied; stimulant use
- **Screen for other BFRBs** (picking, nail or lip biting, cheek chewing) and for depression.
- **Phrasing example:** "Reports 30–60 min nightly of automatic pulling from crown while studying;
  notes tension before and relief after; multiple failed attempts to stop; wears hat to conceal
  patchy thinning."
