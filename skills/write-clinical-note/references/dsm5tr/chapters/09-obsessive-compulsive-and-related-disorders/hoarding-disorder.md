# Hoarding Disorder

> **Spark note:** Hoarding disorder is long-standing difficulty discarding possessions, whatever
> their value. It stems from a perceived need to save them and distress at parting with them, and
> the resulting clutter makes **active living areas** unusable. The saving is **intentional**, which
> separates it from passive accumulation in dementia, depression or psychosis. A tidy home does not
> rule it out if others have cleared it. Many patients have poor insight and report no distress, so
> impairment may be visible only to others. (pp. 425–427)

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** Persistent difficulty discarding or parting with possessions, regardless of actual value.
- **B.** Due to a **perceived need to save** the items **and distress** at discarding them.
- **C.** Accumulation **congests and clutters active living areas** and substantially compromises
  their intended use. If the areas are uncluttered, it is only because third parties intervened
  (family, cleaners, authorities).
- **D.** Clinically significant distress or impairment, including in keeping a **safe
  environment** for self and others.
- **E.** Not attributable to another medical condition, e.g., brain injury, cerebrovascular
  disease, Prader-Willi syndrome.
- **F.** Not better explained by another mental disorder:
  - OCD obsessions
  - decreased energy in MDD
  - psychotic delusions
  - cognitive deficits in major NCD
  - restricted interests in ASD

## Specifiers & severity
- **With excessive acquisition:** difficulty discarding *plus* excessive acquisition of items that
  are not needed or have no space.
  - Present in **80–90%**.
  - Most often **buying**, then **free items** (leaflets, discards). **Stealing** is less common.
  - It may be denied at first and surface later in treatment.
  - Distress occurs when the person is unable or not allowed to acquire.
- **Insight** about hoarding beliefs and behaviors (discarding, clutter, acquisition):
  - **With good or fair insight:** recognizes them as problematic.
  - **With poor insight:** *mostly* convinced they are not problematic despite contrary evidence.
  - **With absent insight/delusional beliefs:** *completely* convinced they are not problematic
    despite contrary evidence.
- **Severity:** no formal DSM severity specifier.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- **"Persistent"** means long-standing, not transient circumstances such as inheriting property.
- **Discarding** covers throwing away, selling, giving away and recycling.
- **Reasons for saving:**
  - perceived usefulness or aesthetic value
  - strong sentimental attachment
  - feeling responsible for the items' fate; avoiding waste
  - fear of losing important information
- **Commonly saved:** newspapers, magazines, clothing, bags, books, mail, papers. Valuables are often
  mixed into the piles.
- **Criterion B** separates hoarding from passive accumulation and from cases with no distress when
  items are removed. Distress at discarding may show as anxiety, frustration, regret, sadness or
  guilt.
- **Clutter:** large groups of usually unrelated objects piled in a disorganized way in spaces meant
  for other uses (tabletops, floors, hallways).
  - Examples: can't cook in the kitchen, sleep in the bed or sit in a chair.
  - Criterion C concerns **active** areas, not garages, attics or basements.
  - Clutter often spills into vehicles, yards, the workplace and relatives' homes.
- **Cleared homes:** a person forced to clear the home still meets criteria.
- **Normative collecting** is organized and selective. It does not cause the clutter, distress or
  impairment, even when the volume is similar.
- **With poor insight**, the patient may report no distress, and impairment may be seen only by
  others. Third-party attempts to discard still cause high distress.

## Associated features
- Indecisiveness, perfectionism, avoidance, procrastination, poor planning and organizing,
  distractibility.
- **Unsanitary conditions** can result from the clutter or from organizing deficits.
- **Animal hoarding:**
  - Many animals kept with failure to provide minimal nutrition, sanitation or veterinary care.
  - Failure to act on the animals' decline (disease, starvation, death) or on the environment
    (overcrowding, squalor).
  - May be a special form of hoarding disorder. Many also hoard objects.
  - Compared with object hoarding: **worse sanitation and poorer insight**.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
**Prevalence**
- No nationally representative studies.
- Community point prevalence in the US and Europe: **1.5–6%**.
- Meta-analysis of 12 high-income-country studies: **2.5%, with no sex difference**. Clinical
  samples are mostly women.
- Netherlands: symptoms **~3× more prevalent at >65 y** than at 30–40 y.

**Development and course**
- Symptoms emerge around **15–19 y**, interfere by the **mid-20s**, and are clinically significant
  by the **mid-30s**. Research participants are typically in their 50s.
- **Severity rises with each decade, especially after 30.**
- Course is usually **chronic**; few report waxing and waning.
- In children, pathological hoarding is easy to tell from normal saving and collecting.
- Children don't control their environment, so consider whether **parents are keeping spaces usable**.

**Risk factors**
- **Temperamental:** indecisiveness, in patients and their first-degree relatives.
- **Environmental:** stressful or traumatic events before onset or before worsening, by
  retrospective report.
- **Genetic:** familial. **>50%** have a relative who hoards. Twin studies: **~50%** of variance is
  additive genetic; the rest is nonshared environment.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- **Culture:** features are consistent across cultures, including in low- and middle-income
  countries. Where thrift and storage are valued, **base the diagnosis on distress and impairment**.
- **Sex/gender:** core features are similar. **Women show more excessive acquisition, especially
  buying.**

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed.

## Functional consequences
- Clutter impairs moving through the home, cooking, cleaning, hygiene and sleep.
- Appliances go unrepaired and utilities may be shut off because repair workers can't get access.
- **Safety:** fire, falls (especially in older adults), poor sanitation, other health risks.
- Occupational impairment, poor physical health, high use of social services.
- Family strain; conflict with neighbors and authorities.
- **Eviction proceedings** are common in severe cases.

## Differential diagnosis
- **Other medical conditions (Criterion E):**
  - TBI, tumor or seizure surgery, cerebrovascular disease, CNS infection (HSV encephalitis),
    Prader-Willi syndrome.
  - Damage to the **anterior ventromedial prefrontal and cingulate cortices** is especially
    implicated.
  - Hoarding starts *after* the injury.
  - Some patients don't care about the items and can discard them easily; others are reluctant.
- **Neurodevelopmental disorders:** accumulation that directly results from ASD or intellectual
  disability → not hoarding disorder.
- **Psychotic disorders:** accumulation from delusions or negative symptoms → not hoarding disorder.
- **Major depressive episode:** accumulation from psychomotor retardation, fatigue or low energy →
  not hoarding disorder.
- **OCD:** hoarding driven by contamination, harm or incompleteness obsessions, or by avoiding
  onerous rituals → OCD.
  - **Incompleteness** is the most common driver, e.g., fear of losing one's identity or a need to
    document every experience.
  - In OCD the behavior is unwanted, distressing and not rewarding.
  - Acquisition, if any, is obsession-driven (e.g., buying items accidentally touched).
  - **Bizarre items** point to OCD: trash, feces, urine, nail clippings, hair, diapers, rotten
    food.
  - Diagnose **both** if severe hoarding is independent of the OCD symptoms.
- **Neurocognitive disorders:** frontotemporal or Alzheimer's.
  - Onset is gradual and *follows* the NCD.
  - Often comes with self-neglect, domestic squalor, disinhibition, gambling, rituals, stereotypies,
    tics or self-injury.

## Comorbidity
- **~75%** have a mood or anxiety disorder. **MDD 30–50%**, social anxiety disorder, GAD.
- **~20%** also meet OCD criteria.
- Comorbidity is often the reason for presenting. Patients rarely volunteer hoarding, and routine
  interviews rarely ask about it, so **screen actively**.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)

Use examples only when supported by the actual assessment. Attribute client/collateral reports, distinguish observations from interpretations, and leave unassessed findings unknown.
- **Criterion A/B:**
  - Note the patient's stated reasons for keeping items (usefulness, sentiment, responsibility, fear
    of losing information).
  - Record observed or reported distress when discarding is proposed.
  - Note that the difficulty is long-standing.
- **Criterion C:** describe which **active rooms** are compromised and how (bed unusable, kitchen
  inaccessible, pathways only). Use home visit or photo observation if available. Note third-party
  clean-outs.
- **Criterion D:** document safety hazards (fire load, fall risk, blocked exits, sanitation,
  pests), utility shut-offs, code or eviction actions, family conflict.
- **Specifiers:**
  - Excessive acquisition: buying, free items, stealing. Ask directly; it is often denied at first.
  - Insight: how the patient views the problem vs. collateral reports.
- **Animal hoarding:** number of animals, their condition, sanitation.
- **Rule-outs to document:**
  - onset after brain injury, stroke or CNS infection
  - cognitive screen (NCD), especially in older adults
  - ASD or intellectual disability
  - psychotic content; depressive anergia
  - OCD-driven saving and bizarre items
- **Collateral:** family or agency reports are often essential when insight is poor.
- **Phrasing examples:**
  - "Reports inability to use bed for 2 years due to accumulated papers; becomes tearful and
    agitated when discarding is discussed."
  - "Minimizes clutter; sister reports fire-department citation (poor insight)."
