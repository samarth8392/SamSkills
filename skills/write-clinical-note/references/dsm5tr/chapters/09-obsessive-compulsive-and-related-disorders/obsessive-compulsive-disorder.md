# Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder

> **Spark note:** OCD means obsessions and/or compulsions that take up time (e.g., >1 h/day) or
> cause distress or impairment. Obsessions are intrusive, unwanted and distressing. Compulsions are
> driven acts meant to neutralize them or to follow rigid rules, and they are unrealistic or
> clearly excessive. Two common errors: equating OCD with OCPD (which has no intrusive obsessions),
> and diagnosing a psychotic disorder when the patient is delusionally convinced of OCD beliefs.
> That presentation is OCD **with absent insight/delusional beliefs**. Most patients have
> both obsessions and compulsions, often across several symptom dimensions. (pp. 410–413)

## Criteria at a glance
- **A. Obsessions, compulsions, or both.**
  - **Obsessions:** both (1) and (2) are required.
    1. Recurrent, persistent thoughts, urges or images, felt *at some point* as intrusive and
       unwanted. In most people they cause marked anxiety or distress.
    2. The person tries to ignore or suppress them, or to neutralize them with another thought or
       action (i.e., a compulsion).
  - **Compulsions:** both (1) and (2) are required.
    1. Repetitive behaviors (washing, ordering, checking) or mental acts (praying, counting,
       silently repeating words). The person feels driven to do them in response to an obsession
       or by rigidly applied rules.
    2. They aim to prevent or reduce distress or to prevent a dreaded event. They are either not
       realistically connected to what they are meant to prevent, or clearly excessive.
  - *Note:* young children may not be able to say what the behaviors are for.
- **B.** Time-consuming (e.g., **>1 hour/day**) **or** clinically significant distress or impairment.
- **C.** Not attributable to a substance/medication or another medical condition.
- **D.** Not better explained by another mental disorder. Examples the text lists:
  - GAD worries; BDD appearance preoccupation; hoarding difficulty discarding.
  - Trichotillomania hair pulling; excoriation skin picking.
  - Stereotypies; ritualized eating in eating disorders.
  - Preoccupation with substances or gambling; illness preoccupation in illness anxiety disorder.
  - Sexual urges or fantasies in paraphilias; impulses in disruptive/impulse-control disorders.
  - Guilty ruminations in MDD; thought insertion or delusions in psychosis.
  - Repetitive behavior patterns in ASD.

## Specifiers & severity
**Insight**, about the beliefs underlying the symptoms:
- **With good or fair insight:** OCD beliefs are definitely or probably not true, or may or may
  not be true. Example: the house definitely won't, probably won't, or may or may not burn down if
  the stove isn't checked 30 times.
- **With poor insight:** the beliefs are probably true.
- **With absent insight/delusional beliefs:** completely convinced the beliefs are true. Seen in
  **≤4%** of patients.
- Insight can change over the course of the illness. **Poorer insight is linked to worse long-term
  outcome** (pp. 411–412).

**Tic-related:**
- Use for a current *or past* tic disorder.
- Up to **30%** of OCD patients have a lifetime tic disorder. It is most common in males with
  childhood onset.
- This group differs in symptom themes, comorbidity, course and familial transmission.

**Severity:** DSM gives no formal severity specifier. The text describes a range from 1–3 h/day of
mild-moderate symptoms to near-constant, incapacitating symptoms.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- **Obsessions** are not pleasurable and not felt as voluntary. Examples: contamination thoughts,
  violent images, an urge to stab someone.
- **Suppression** includes avoiding triggers and thought suppression.
- **Compulsions** may bring temporary relief but are not done for pleasure.
- **Obsessions and compulsions usually match in theme:** contamination → washing; harm → checking.
- **Symptom dimensions:**
  - **cleaning:** contamination obsessions, cleaning compulsions
  - **symmetry:** symmetry obsessions; repeating, ordering and counting compulsions
  - **forbidden/taboo thoughts:** aggressive, sexual, religious
  - **harm:** fear of harming self or others, checking
- The dimensions are consistent across cultures and over time in adults. Most patients have
  symptoms in more than one.
- Difficulty discarding that is *secondary to* typical obsessions (e.g., fear of harming others)
  must be told apart from primary hoarding disorder.
- **Criterion B** separates OCD from common occasional intrusions and double-checking.
- **Children:** compulsions are easier to spot because they are observable. Most children have both
  obsessions and compulsions. Symptoms are less stable in children than in adults.
  - Adolescents have more sexual and religious obsessions than children.
  - Children and adolescents have more harm obsessions (death or illness of self or loved ones)
    than adults.

## Associated features
- **Sensory phenomena** precede compulsions in up to **60%**: physical sensations, "just-right"
  sensations, feelings of incompleteness.
- Affective responses include marked anxiety (possibly recurrent panic attacks), disgust, and a
  distressing sense of incompleteness until things feel "just right."
- **Avoidance** of trigger people, places and things, e.g., restaurants and public restrooms in
  contamination OCD; social contact in harm OCD.
- **Dysfunctional beliefs** are common but **not specific to OCD:**
  - inflated responsibility and overestimation of threat
  - perfectionism and intolerance of uncertainty
  - overimportance of thoughts (a forbidden thought is as bad as the act) and the need to control them
- **Family accommodation**, where family join in rituals, maintains symptoms. It is a key treatment
  target, especially in children.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
**Prevalence**
- 12-month prevalence: **1.2%** in the US; **1.1–1.8%** internationally.
- Slightly more common in females in adulthood; more common in males in childhood.

**Development and course**
- Mean onset **19.5 y**; **25% by age 14**. Onset after 35 is unusual.
- Males start earlier: **~25% of males** have onset **before age 10**.
- Onset is usually gradual but can be acute.
- Untreated course is usually **chronic and waxing-waning**. Some have an episodic course; a
  minority deteriorate.
- Untreated adult remission is low: **~20% at 40-year follow-up**.
- **40%** of childhood or adolescent onsets may remit by early adulthood.

**Temperamental risk:** greater internalizing symptoms, higher negative emotionality, and
behavioral inhibition in childhood.

**Environmental risk**
- Adverse perinatal events, prematurity, maternal tobacco use in pregnancy.
- Childhood physical or sexual abuse; other stress or trauma.
- Infectious agents and a postinfectious autoimmune syndrome are linked to **sudden-onset
  childhood OCD** (see due-to-AMC file, PANS/PANDAS).

**Genetic/physiological risk**
- First-degree relatives: **2×** the rate; **10×** if the proband had childhood/adolescent onset.
- Twin concordance: MZ **0.57** vs. DZ **0.22**. Additive genetic effects explain **~40%** of
  variance.
- Implicated regions: **orbitofrontal cortex, anterior cingulate and striatum**. Changes in
  frontolimbic, frontoparietal and cerebellar networks are also reported.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- **Culture:** sex distribution, age at onset, comorbidity and symptom structure are similar
  worldwide (cleaning, symmetry, hoarding, taboo thoughts, harm). Content can vary:
  - sexual obsessions are reported less in some religious groups
  - aggressive obsessions are more common where urban violence is high
  - causes may be attributed to physical, social, spiritual or supernatural factors, which shape
    compulsions and help-seeking
- **Sex/gender:**
  - Males: earlier onset, often in childhood, and more comorbid tics.
  - Females: onset usually in adolescence.
  - Females have more cleaning symptoms; males more forbidden-thought and symmetry symptoms.
  - **Peripartum** onset or worsening can occur. Intrusive thoughts of harming the infant can lead
    to avoiding the infant.
  - Some women report **premenstrual** worsening.

## Suicide risk
- Systematic review of clinical samples (lifetime rates): attempts **14.2%**; suicidal ideation
  (SI) **44.1%**; current SI **25.9%**.
- **Predictors:** OCD severity, the unacceptable-thoughts dimension, comorbid depression or
  anxiety severity, and past suicidality.
- Brazil, n=582 outpatients: lifetime SI 36%, plans 20%, attempts 11%, current SI 10%.
  - Sexual/religious dimension and comorbid SUD → linked to SI and plans.
  - Impulse-control disorders → linked to current SI, plans and attempts.
  - Lifetime MDD or PTSD → linked to every aspect of suicidality.
- **Swedish registry** (n=36,788): suicide death **OR 9.8**; attempts **OR 5.5**. Risk stays high
  after adjusting for comorbidity.
  - Raises risk: comorbid personality disorder or SUD.
  - Protective: female sex, higher parental education, comorbid anxiety disorder.

## Functional consequences
- Reduced quality of life and high social and occupational impairment, scaling with severity.
- Impairment comes from time spent on symptoms, from avoidance, and from symptom-specific problems:
  - harm obsessions → avoiding relationships
  - symmetry or "just right" → failure to finish work, school failure, job loss
  - contamination → avoiding doctors or hospitals; dermatological lesions from washing
  - medications seen as contaminated → interferes with treatment
- **Childhood onset:** developmental problems, few relationships outside the family, lack of
  autonomy and financial dependence on family.
- Imposing rules on the family (e.g., no visitors) → family dysfunction.

## Differential diagnosis
- **GAD:** worries are about real-life concerns. Obsessions are often odd, irrational or magical,
  with linked compulsions.
- **Specific phobia:** the feared object is more circumscribed, and there are no rituals.
- **Social anxiety disorder:** fear is limited to social or performance situations; reassurance is
  sought to reduce embarrassment.
- **MDD:** rumination is mood-congruent, not necessarily felt as intrusive, and not linked to
  compulsions.
- **BDD:** concerns limited to appearance.
- **Trichotillomania:** hair pulling without obsessions.
- **Hoarding disorder:** primary difficulty discarding. If accumulation is *driven by* OCD
  obsessions (completeness, harm prevention), diagnose OCD instead.
- **Anorexia nervosa:** OCD content is not limited to food and weight.
- **Tics and stereotypies:**
  - They are simpler than compulsions and do not neutralize obsessions.
  - Tics are preceded by premonitory sensory *urges*; compulsions by obsessions.
  - Complex tics can be hard to tell apart. Give both diagnoses if both are present.
- **Psychotic disorders:** OCD has obsessions and compulsions (unlike delusional disorder) and no
  hallucinations or disorganization. Delusional OCD beliefs → absent-insight specifier, not a
  psychotic disorder.
- **"Compulsive" sex, gambling or substance use:** these are pleasurable and resisted only because
  of consequences.
- **OCPD:** no intrusive thoughts or responsive rituals; instead a pervasive pattern of
  perfectionism and rigid control. Both can be diagnosed.

## Comorbidity
- Lifetime (US adults):
  - **any anxiety disorder 76%** (panic, social anxiety, GAD, specific phobia)
  - **depressive or bipolar 63%**, including MDD 41%
  - **impulse-control disorder 56%**
  - **SUD 39%**
- **Order of onset:** OCD usually starts after most comorbid anxiety disorders (except separation
  anxiety) and after PTSD, but often before depression.
- **OCPD 23–32%** in a longitudinal treatment-seeking sample.
- **Tic disorder:** up to 30%. In children, look for the **OCD + tic + ADHD triad**.
- BDD, trichotillomania and excoriation are more frequent in OCD.
- **OCD is elevated in other disorders, so assess for it there:** schizophrenia/schizoaffective
  (~**12%**), bipolar disorder, anorexia and bulimia, BDD, Tourette's.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)

Use examples only when supported by the actual assessment. Attribute client/collateral reports, distinguish observations from interpretations, and leave unassessed findings unknown.
- **Criterion A, obsessions:**
  - Record content and theme (contamination, harm, taboo, symmetry).
  - Note that the thoughts are experienced as intrusive and unwanted ("ego-dystonic").
  - Record efforts to suppress or neutralize them.
- **Criterion A, compulsions:**
  - Describe the overt rituals (washing, checking, ordering) and covert ones (counting, praying,
    mental reviewing, reassurance seeking).
  - Note their link to the obsession or to rigid rules, and the feared consequence they are meant
    to prevent.
  - Note the excess or lack of realistic connection.
- **Criterion B:** quantify hours/day spent on obsessions and rituals, plus avoidance and specific
  functional impacts (late to work, unable to finish tasks, skin damage from washing).
- **Insight:** document belief conviction in the patient's own framing (definitely, probably, or
  completely true) and select the specifier.
- **Tic history:** note any current or past motor or vocal tics → tic-related specifier.
- **Accommodation:** record family participation in rituals or reassurance.
- **Rule-outs to document:**
  - substances or medications (stimulants), medical causes (acute onset in a child → infection
    history)
  - psychosis (hallucinations, disorganization)
  - GAD worry vs. obsession; depressive rumination; OCPD traits
  - BDD, hoarding and BFRB focus; tics
- **Suicide screen:** document SI/SB, especially with taboo or unacceptable thoughts, comorbid
  depression or SUD, or prior attempts.
- **MSE phrasing examples:**
  - "Thought content notable for intrusive, ego-dystonic contamination concerns; denies
    hallucinations."
  - "Patient acknowledges fears are 'probably not realistic' (good/fair insight)."
  - "Observed repeated hand-sanitizing during session."
  - "Reports 3–4 h/day checking stove and locks."
