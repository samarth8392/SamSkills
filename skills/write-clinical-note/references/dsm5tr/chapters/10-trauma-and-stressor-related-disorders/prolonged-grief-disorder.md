# Prolonged Grief Disorder

> **Spark note:** **New in DSM-5-TR.** It was promoted from "persistent complex bereavement disorder" in DSM-5 Section III. PGD is grief that is still intense and impairing **≥12 months** after the death of someone close (**≥6 months** in children and adolescents). It is defined by **yearning or preoccupation** plus **≥3 of 8** grief symptoms, nearly every day for at least the last month, beyond cultural norms. The key discriminator is that the distress centers on **loss of and separation from the deceased**, not generalized low mood (MDD) and not fear of a traumatic event (PTSD). A grief spike on an anniversary does not count by itself.

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** Death of a person close to the bereaved **≥12 months ago** (**≥6 months** for children and adolescents).
- **B.** Since the death, a persistent grief response with **≥1 of 2**, present **most days** to a clinically significant degree and **nearly every day for at least the last month**:
  1. Intense yearning or longing for the deceased.
  2. Preoccupation with thoughts or memories of the deceased. In youth, this may focus on the **circumstances of the death**.
- **C.** Since the death, **≥3 of 8**, with the same frequency rule (most days, nearly daily for the last month):
  1. Identity disruption (e.g., feeling part of oneself has died).
  2. Marked disbelief about the death.
  3. Avoidance of reminders that the person is dead. In youth: efforts to avoid reminders.
  4. Intense emotional pain related to the death (anger, bitterness, sorrow).
  5. Difficulty reintegrating into relationships and activities (friends, interests, planning the future).
  6. Emotional numbness due to the death.
  7. Feeling life is meaningless due to the death.
  8. Intense loneliness due to the death.
- **D.** Clinically significant distress or impairment.
- **E.** Duration and severity **clearly exceed** the expected social, cultural or religious norms for the person's culture and context.
- **F.** Not better explained by another mental disorder (e.g., **MDD, PTSD**). Not due to a substance or medical condition.

**Minimum:** 1 B + 3 C = 4 symptoms, nearly daily for ≥1 month, at ≥12 months (≥6 months in youth) after the death.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- The 12-month threshold generally separates normal grief from grief that stays severe and impairing, but the length of adaptive grief varies between individuals and cultures.
- Yearning often comes with intense sorrow and frequent crying.
- The narrative lists **guilt** as an example of emotional pain (C4), alongside anger and bitterness.
- Occurs across genders and in diverse social and cultural groups.
- **Frequency rule:** "most days" since the death **and** "nearly every day" for the past month. Spikes around anniversaries, birthdays and holidays **without** persistent grief at other times do **not** qualify.

## Associated features
- Maladaptive cognitions about the self, guilt about the death, and lowered expectations about one's future and goals.
- **Somatic complaints**, sometimes mirroring the deceased's symptoms (e.g., appetite change), linked to comorbid depression or anxiety and more health-care visits.
- Harmful health behaviors from reduced self-care.
- **Hallucinations of the deceased** (e.g., hearing their voice) occur in normal grief but may be more common in PGD. They are linked to disrupted identity and purpose.
- Bitterness, anger, restlessness, blaming others for the death, and reduced sleep quantity and quality.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- **Prevalence:** DSM-5-TR PGD in adults is unknown. A meta-analysis using a different definition (≥6 months postloss) found a pooled **9.8%**, with high heterogeneity. Higher in trauma-exposed populations. Possibly higher in high-income Western than Asian countries, though recent Chinese studies show high and variable rates. Persistent complex bereavement disorder affected about **18%** of bereaved US youth in the community.
- **Course:** data are limited. Symptoms usually begin within the first months, though the full syndrome may be delayed. Course may be especially prolonged in **parents after a child's death**. Comorbid **PTSD** complicates the course, more often after violent death (murder, suicide), especially if the person faced a threat to their own life or witnessed a gruesome death. **Older age** may raise risk. Older adults with PGD symptoms may be at risk of **progressive cognitive decline**.
- **Children:**
  - Distress shows in **play and behavior**, developmental regression, and anxious or protest behavior at separation and reunion.
  - Losing a primary caregiver is especially disorganizing.
  - They may protest when daily care is done differently from how the deceased did it (cooking, bedtime routines).
  - Intense insecurity: worries about the safety of caregivers and themselves, and repeated questions about death.
  - **Searching** for the deceased, since they do not understand that death is permanent.
  - Somatic symptoms (sleep, eating, digestion, energy).
  - Yearning expressed as a literal wish to reunite (e.g., climbing a ladder to heaven).
  - Young children do not describe numbness, whereas adolescents may report "not feeling anything".
- **Adolescents and older children:**
  - Preoccupation may center on the deceased's physical decline or a caregiver's inability to function.
  - Identity disruption: feeling profoundly different from peers when faced with loss reminders (e.g., making Mother's Day cards).
  - Reluctance to join reminder activities, and pain at being "robbed" of the deceased's help with developmental milestones.
  - Failing to reach developmental milestones is a form of failed reintegration (C5).
  - Meaninglessness may look like giving up aspirations or indifference to risk ("so what if I get hurt").
  - Fear of sharing the deceased's fate.
  - Loneliness worsened by keeping grief private to protect a grieving parent or avoid stigma.
  - Separation distress predominates in younger children. Identity disruption and depression risk grow with age.
- **Risk:**
  - Environmental: greater **dependency on the deceased**, **death of a child**, **violent or unexpected death**, and economic stressors. Higher after loss of a **spouse or partner or child** than other kin.
  - For children: disrupted caregiver availability and support.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- **Culture:** seen across cultures, with culture-specific expression and expected duration.
  - Nightmares about the deceased may be especially distressing because of the meaning attributed to them.
  - Rates of hallucinations of the deceased and of somatic grief vary.
  - Indirect expression (drinking, poor self-care) may be more common than direct expression of grief.
  - Being unable to perform **funerary rituals** may worsen symptoms (concern for the deceased's spiritual status).
  - Possibly higher symptom levels in African Americans than in non-Hispanic Whites, perhaps from more exposure to sudden or violent death.
  - Mourning practices and the gendered social status of the bereaved (e.g., sanctions on remarriage) affect intensity and duration.
  - Diagnosis requires that responses **go beyond cultural norms** and are not better explained by culturally specific mourning rituals.
- **Sex/gender:** findings are mixed. Some studies show higher prevalence or severity in women, others a small or nonsignificant gap.

## Suicide risk
- **Heightened suicidal ideation**, even after controlling for MDD and PTSD, consistent across the life span and across nations.
- It is not established whether this translates into more suicidal **behavior**.
- Correlates of ideation: stigma, isolation, **thwarted belongingness**, avoidance, distress.
- Higher risk after **violent loss** (homicide, suicide, accident) and after the **death of a child**, especially a child younger than 25.

## Functional consequences
- Impaired work and social functioning.
- More tobacco and alcohol use.
- Markedly increased risk of cardiac disease, hypertension, cancer and immune deficiency, and reduced quality of life.
- Youth: dropping out of school, lower aspirations and attainment. Young women may hesitate to marry.
- Impaired cognition, especially in middle-aged and older adults.

## Differential diagnosis
- **Normal grief:** PGD requires **severe** grief persisting ≥12 months (≥6 months in youth) that **impairs function** and **exceeds norms**. Anniversary or holiday spikes alone do not qualify.
- **Depressive disorders (MDD, PDD):** they share low mood, crying and suicidal thinking. In PGD the distress centers on **loss of and separation from** the deceased, not pervasive low mood. MDD may also follow a death, with or without PGD.
- **PTSD:** both may follow a violent or accidental death, and both involve intrusions and avoidance.
  - *Intrusions:* in PTSD, about the **traumatic event**. In PGD, about many aspects of the **relationship**, including positive ones, and distress over the separation.
  - *Avoidance:* in PTSD, of memories of the traumatic event (e.g., the crash). In PGD, of reminders that the person is **gone** (e.g., activities once shared).
  - *Quality:* PTSD reexperiencing is perceptual and "here and now". PGD memories usually are not.
  - *Yearning:* present in PGD, absent in PTSD.
- **Separation anxiety disorder:** anxiety about separation from **living** attachment figures. PGD involves distress about separation from the **deceased**.
- **Psychotic disorder:** seeing, hearing or sensing the deceased (often hypnagogic, often comforting) is common in normal grief across cultures. A psychotic diagnosis requires **other** psychotic symptoms (delusions, disorganization, negative symptoms).
- **Adjustment disorder:** used for out-of-proportion grief **before** 12 months. After 12 months, use PGD if criteria are met, otherwise other specified trauma- and stressor-related disorder.

## Comorbidity
- Most common: **MDD, PTSD, substance use disorders**.
- PTSD is more frequent after violent or accidental death.
- Separation anxiety disorder involving **living** attachment figures may co-occur.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)

Use examples only when supported by the actual assessment. Attribute client/collateral reports, distinguish observations from interpretations, and leave unassessed findings unknown.
- **Criterion A:** name the deceased and the relationship, the date of death, the cause (natural, sudden, violent, suicide), and the **elapsed time** (≥12 mo adult, ≥6 mo youth).
- **Criterion B:** record the yearning and longing or the preoccupation in the client's own words ("thinks about her son constantly, can't stop"). Note frequency: most days since the death, nearly daily this past month.
- **Criterion C, ≥3:** map each one, e.g., identity ("part of me died"), disbelief ("still expect him to call"), avoidance of reminders ("won't enter his room"), emotional pain (anger, bitterness, guilt), failure to reintegrate (stopped socializing, cannot plan ahead), numbness, meaninglessness, loneliness.
- **Criterion E:** the cultural and religious context of mourning, the expected norms, and why this reaction exceeds them. Use collateral or cultural informants where possible.
- **Criterion F rule-outs:** MDD (is low mood pervasive or loss-focused?), PTSD (trauma-specific perceptual reexperiencing; screen for Criterion A if the death was violent or witnessed), substances, medical causes. Diagnose comorbid MDD or PTSD separately if their criteria are met.
- **Grief hallucinations:** describe them (e.g., "brief, comforting sense of husband's presence at bedtime; no other psychotic features") so they are not misread as psychosis.
- **Youth:** play themes, regression, searching, separation and reunion protest, school decline, milestone avoidance.
- **Safety:** document suicidal ideation (wishing to join the deceased, meaninglessness), especially after violent loss or the death of a child. Note health behaviors (alcohol, tobacco, self-care) and medical follow-up.
- **MSE phrases:** "tearful when discussing late spouse; mood 'empty'; affect sad, reactive to loss themes"; "thought content: preoccupation with the deceased, yearning"; "no delusions; reports hypnagogic sense of presence, ego-syntonic".
