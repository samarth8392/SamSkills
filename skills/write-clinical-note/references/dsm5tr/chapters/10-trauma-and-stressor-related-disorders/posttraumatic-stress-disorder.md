# Posttraumatic Stress Disorder

> **Spark note:** PTSD requires a qualifying **Criterion A** exposure (actual or threatened death, serious injury or sexual violence) followed by >1 month of symptoms in four clusters: intrusion (B, ≥1), avoidance (C, ≥1), negative cognitions and mood (D, ≥2) and arousal (E, ≥2). Children ≤6 have a separate, lower-threshold criteria set. Common errors: using PTSD when the stressor does not meet Criterion A (consider adjustment disorder if its own criteria are met), counting media exposure that is not work related, and calling depressive rumination an "intrusion".

## Criteria at a glance: individuals older than 6 years (adults, adolescents, children >6)
**A. Exposure** to actual or threatened death, serious injury or sexual violence, by ≥1 route:
1. Directly experiencing it.
2. Witnessing it **in person** as it happened to others.
3. Learning it happened to a **close family member or close friend**. For an actual or threatened death, the event must have been **violent or accidental**; natural-cause death does not qualify.
4. **Repeated or extreme exposure to aversive details**, e.g., first responders collecting remains or police repeatedly exposed to child-abuse details. Electronic media, TV, movies and pictures **do not count unless the exposure is work related**.

**B. Intrusion: ≥1 of 5**, beginning after the event:
1. Recurrent, involuntary, intrusive distressing memories. *Children >6:* may show repetitive play with trauma themes.
2. Recurrent distressing dreams whose content and/or affect relates to the event. *Children:* frightening dreams without recognizable content.
3. Dissociative reactions (flashbacks): feeling or acting as if the event were recurring. These lie on a continuum up to complete loss of awareness of the surroundings. *Children:* trauma-specific reenactment in play.
4. Intense or prolonged psychological distress at internal or external cues resembling or symbolizing the event.
5. Marked physiological reactions to such cues.

**C. Avoidance: ≥1 of 2**, beginning after the event:
1. Avoiding, or trying to avoid, distressing trauma-related memories, thoughts or feelings.
2. Avoiding, or trying to avoid, external reminders (people, places, conversations, activities, objects, situations).

**D. Negative alterations in cognition and mood: ≥2 of 7**, beginning or worsening after the event:
1. Inability to recall an important aspect of the event, typically from dissociative amnesia and not head injury, alcohol or drugs.
2. Persistent, exaggerated negative beliefs or expectations about self, others or the world (e.g., "I am bad", "the world is completely dangerous").
3. Persistent distorted cognitions about the event's cause or consequences, leading to blame of self or others.
4. Persistent negative emotional state (fear, horror, anger, guilt, shame).
5. Markedly diminished interest or participation in significant activities.
6. Feeling detached or estranged from others.
7. Persistent inability to experience positive emotions (happiness, satisfaction, love).

**E. Alterations in arousal and reactivity: ≥2 of 6**, beginning or worsening after the event:
1. Irritability or angry outbursts with little or no provocation, typically verbal or physical aggression.
2. Reckless or self-destructive behavior.
3. Hypervigilance.
4. Exaggerated startle.
5. Concentration problems.
6. Sleep disturbance (initiation, maintenance, or restless sleep).

**F.** B, C, D and E last **>1 month**.
**G.** Clinically significant distress or impairment (social, occupational, other).
**H.** Not attributable to a substance (medication, alcohol) or another medical condition.

**Minimum count:** 1 B + 1 C + 2 D + 2 E = **6 symptoms**, all present >1 month.

## Criteria at a glance: children 6 years and younger
**A. Exposure** by ≥1 route:
1. Directly experiencing it.
2. Witnessing it in person, **especially as it happens to primary caregivers**.
3. Learning it happened to a **parent or caregiving figure**.
- There is **no A4** (aversive-details or occupational) route in this age group.

**B. Intrusion: ≥1 of 5.** Same five as the older-age set, with these notes:
- B1: spontaneous intrusive memories **may not look distressing** and may show up as **play reenactment**.
- B2: it may not be possible to confirm that frightening dream content relates to the trauma.
- B3: trauma-specific reenactment may occur in play.
- B5: marked physiological reactions to reminders.

**C. ≥1 symptom from a combined avoidance / negative-cognition pool of 6**, beginning or worsening after the event:
- Persistent avoidance:
  1. Avoids activities, places or physical reminders.
  2. Avoids people, conversations or interpersonal situations that bring up recollections.
- Negative alterations in cognitions:
  3. Substantially increased frequency of negative emotional states (fear, guilt, sadness, shame, confusion).
  4. Markedly diminished interest or participation in significant activities, **including constriction of play**.
  5. Socially withdrawn behavior.
  6. Persistent reduction in expression of positive emotions.

**D. Arousal and reactivity: ≥2 of 5**:
1. Irritability or angry outbursts, **including extreme temper tantrums**.
2. Hypervigilance.
3. Exaggerated startle.
4. Concentration problems.
5. Sleep disturbance.
- There is **no reckless or self-destructive item** in this age group.

**E.** Duration **>1 month**.
**F.** Distress or impairment in **relationships with parents, siblings, peers or other caregivers, or in school behavior**.
**G.** Not attributable to a substance or another medical condition.

**Minimum count:** 1 B + 1 C + 2 D = **4 symptoms**.

### Quick comparison
| Feature | >6 years | ≤6 years |
|---|---|---|
| Exposure routes | A1–A4 | A1–A3 (caregiver-focused, no A4) |
| Avoidance and cognition/mood | C ≥1 of 2 **and** D ≥2 of 7 | Combined C ≥1 of 6 |
| Arousal | ≥2 of 6 (includes reckless behavior) | ≥2 of 5 (tantrums; no reckless behavior) |
| Amnesia, negative beliefs, blame items | Yes | Not included |
| Impairment domain | Social/occupational/other | Caregivers, siblings, peers, school |

## Specifiers & severity
Both age sets use the same two specifiers.
- **With dissociative symptoms.** Full PTSD criteria are met **plus** persistent or recurrent symptoms, in response to the stressor, of either:
  1. **Depersonalization:** feeling detached from one's mental processes or body, as an outside observer (dreamlike, unreality of self or body, time slowing).
  2. **Derealization:** surroundings experienced as unreal, dreamlike, distant or distorted.
  - The dissociation must **not** be due to a substance (blackouts, behavior during intoxication) or a medical condition (complex partial seizures).
- **With delayed expression:** full criteria are **not met until ≥6 months** after the event. Some symptoms may start immediately. The delay is in meeting full criteria, not in symptom onset. (DSM-IV called this "delayed onset".)
- There is **no severity specifier** in the criteria.
- **Current vs lifetime:** for a *current* diagnosis, B–E must all be met for >1 month, including at least the past month. For a *lifetime* diagnosis, there must have been a >1-month period in which B–E were all met during the same month.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- **Phenotypes vary:** fear-based reexperiencing; anhedonic or dysphoric mood with negative cognitions; arousal and reactive-externalizing; dissociative; or combinations. The disorder can be especially severe or prolonged when the stressor is **interpersonal and intentional** (torture, sexual violence).
- **Criterion A examples:**
  - **A1 (direct):** war (combatant or civilian), physical assault or threat perceived as imminent and realistic (attack, robbery, mugging, childhood physical abuse), kidnapping, hostage-taking, terrorism, torture, POW incarceration, natural or human-made disaster, severe MVA.
  - **Sexual trauma:** forced penetration, drug- or alcohol-facilitated nonconsensual penetration, other unwanted contact, and **noncontact** experiences (being forced to watch pornography, exhibitionism, unwanted sexual photography or video, or its dissemination). For children, developmentally inappropriate sexual experiences qualify even without violence or injury.
  - **Bullying** qualifies as A1 only if there is a credible threat of serious harm or sexual violence.
  - **Medical events:** a life-threatening illness or debilitating condition is **not automatically** traumatic. What qualifies is an acute emergency (MI, anaphylaxis) or a treatment event that evokes catastrophic terror, pain, helplessness or a sense of imminent death (waking during surgery, burn debridement, emergency cardioversion).
  - **A2 (witnessed):** threatened or serious injury, unnatural death, abuse, domestic violence, accident, war, disaster. Includes a parent witnessing a child's life-threatening incident or medical catastrophe (e.g., a hemorrhage).
  - **A3 (learned):** limited to **violent or accidental** events involving close relatives or friends: murder, assault, combat, terrorism, sexual violence, **suicide**, serious accident.
  - **A4 (occupational and indirect):** first responders, military collecting remains, police reviewing crime reports or interviewing victims, drone operators, news media, and **psychotherapists** exposed to patients' trauma details. Exposure may be through photos, video, or verbal or written accounts.
- **Multiple traumas:** identify the **worst discrete event** to anchor B and C. If the person cannot pick one, the whole exposure can be treated as meeting A.
- **B1 vs rumination:** intrusions are involuntary, vivid, sensory and emotional. Depressive rumination is not an intrusion.
- **B3 flashbacks** usually last seconds and rarely longer. They range from brief sensory intrusions with intact reality testing, to partial loss of awareness, to complete loss of awareness.
- **B4/B5 cues** may be external (windy days after a hurricane, someone resembling the perpetrator) or **somatic** (dizziness after head trauma, rapid heartbeat).
- **C1** includes substance use or distraction used to suppress internal reminders.
- **D1** amnesia is dissociative, not encoding failure from TBI or intoxication.
- **E2** reckless behavior means voluntary acts that directly risk serious harm or death: drunk or high-speed driving, excessive alcohol or drug use, risky sex, self-directed violence including suicidal behavior. It **excludes** required job hazards taken with reasonable precautions, and unwise but not immediately dangerous behavior (gambling, binge eating, poor financial choices).
- **E3** hypervigilance may extend to threats unrelated to the trauma (e.g., fear of a heart attack).
- **E4 startle vs B5:** startle is reflexive and **need not be trauma-related**. B5 reactivity needs some conscious appraisal that the cue is linked to the trauma.
- **E5** includes forgetting daily facts and trouble following conversations. **E6** sleep problems may come from nightmares, safety concerns or general arousal.

## Associated features
- Developmental regression, such as loss of language in young children.
- **Auditory pseudo-hallucinations** (hearing one's thoughts in other voices) and paranoid ideation can occur. These are not by themselves evidence of psychosis.
- After prolonged, repeated, severe trauma (childhood abuse, torture): emotion-regulation difficulty, unstable relationships and dissociation.
- After the violent death of someone close, PTSD and **prolonged grief disorder** may both be present.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- **Prevalence:**
  - US adult lifetime: **6.8%** (DSM-IV); **6.1–8.3%** lifetime and **4.7%** 12-month (DSM-5 national studies).
  - US adolescents: lifetime 5.0–8.1%, 6-month 4.9%.
  - World Mental Health Surveys (24 countries): **3.9%** lifetime.
  - Conflict-affected populations: point prevalence **11%** (with impairment).
  - Highest rates (**one-third to over one-half** of those exposed): rape, military combat and captivity, ethnically or politically motivated internment, genocide.
  - Higher among veterans, police, firefighters and EMS.
  - Higher among US Latinx, African American and American Indian people than White people, linked to past adversity, racism and discrimination, and access to treatment, support and resources.
- **Course:**
  - Can begin at any age **after the first year of life**.
  - Symptoms usually start **within 3 months**, but meeting full criteria can be delayed by months or years.
  - Often begins as acute stress disorder.
  - About **half of adults recover completely within 3 months**. Some stay symptomatic >12 months, occasionally >50 years.
  - Recurrence or worsening can follow reminders, ongoing stressors or new trauma.
- **Development:**
  - Young children: frightening dreams without trauma-specific content. Reexperiencing is expressed through **play** (direct or symbolic), sometimes without visible fear. Play and stories may feature imagined rescues. Children may become preoccupied with reminders as well as avoid them. Negative mood or cognition shows mainly as mood change.
  - Children ≤6 may develop PTSD from **severe emotional abuse** (e.g., threats of abandonment), and from the intensity of treatment for life-threatening illness.
  - In chronic or co-occurring trauma (abuse plus witnessed domestic violence), children may not be able to identify when symptoms began.
  - Avoidance shows as restricted play and exploration (young), less participation in new activities (school age), or reluctance to pursue milestones such as dating and driving (adolescents).
  - Adolescents may see themselves as cowardly or changed, socially undesirable and without a future.
  - Older adults: negative health perceptions, more primary-care use and suicidal thoughts. Declining health, worsening cognition and isolation can exacerbate PTSD.
- **Risk factors:**
  - **Pretraumatic:**
    - Temperamental: emotional problems by age 6 (externalizing or anxiety), prior mental disorders (panic, depression, PTSD, OCD), and negative affectivity (neuroticism). **Trait impulsivity** predicts an externalizing presentation and SUD or aggression.
    - Environmental: lower SES and education, prior or childhood trauma, childhood adversity, lower intelligence, ethnic discrimination and racism, and family psychiatric history. Prior social support is **protective**.
    - Genetic and physiological: modest heritability, three genome-wide significant loci that vary by ancestry, and epigenetic effects.
  - **Peritraumatic:** trauma severity ("dose"), perceived life threat, personal injury, and interpersonal violence (especially perpetrated by a caregiver, or a witnessed threat to a caregiver in children). In the military: being a perpetrator, witnessing atrocities, killing. Also peritraumatic dissociation, fear or panic that persists afterward.
  - **Posttraumatic:**
    - Temperamental: negative appraisals, poor coping, and developing ASD.
    - Environmental: repeated upsetting reminders, later adverse events, financial or trauma-related losses, forced migration, and daily stressors. Racial and ethnic discrimination predicts a **more chronic course** among African American and Latinx adults. Social support, including family stability for children, is **protective**.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- **Culture:**
  - Exposure levels and conditional risk vary by group.
  - The meaning given to the event affects severity, e.g., being unable to perform funerary rites, or living among unpunished perpetrators.
  - In communities with **pervasive, ongoing trauma**, individual Criterion A events predict PTSD less well.
  - Where social image ("face") matters, public shaming can magnify the impact.
  - Some cultures attribute PTSD-like syndromes to supernatural experiences.
  - Symptom expression: many non-Western groups show **less avoidance and more somatic symptoms** (dizziness, dyspnea, heat sensations). Anger, distressing dreams and **sleep paralysis** are common across cultures.
  - Self-blame may be culturally normative (karma, destiny, "spoiled medicine law" in West Africa), so it should not automatically count as a distorted cognition.
  - Cultural concepts of distress shape both expression and comorbidity (see Section III).
- **Sex/gender:**
  - More prevalent in women across the life span: lifetime **8.0–11.0% in women vs 4.1–5.4% in men** (DSM-5 US studies).
  - Part of the gap reflects more exposure to sexual and interpersonal violence. Processing differences and reproductive hormones also contribute, and the gap persists for matched stressors.
  - Women have a longer course. Symptom profiles and factor structure are similar in men and women.

## Suicide risk
- Childhood abuse and sexual trauma raise suicide risk in civilians and veterans.
- PTSD is associated with suicidal ideation, attempts and death by suicide.
- PTSD increases the likelihood of **moving from ideation to plan or attempt**, independent of comorbid mood disorders.
- In adolescents, the link to suicidal thoughts and behavior holds after adjusting for comorbidity.
- Reckless or self-destructive behavior (E2) includes suicidal behavior, so screen every case.

## Functional consequences
High impairment in social, occupational and physical functioning, reduced quality of life and physical health problems. Seen in poor family and social relationships, work absenteeism, lower income, and lower educational and occupational attainment.

## Differential diagnosis
- **Adjustment disorders:**
  - The stressor does not meet A (spouse leaving, being fired) but the PTSD symptom pattern is present.
  - Or the stressor meets A but the response does not meet full PTSD criteria (or another disorder's).
  - A persistent subthreshold PTSD-like response >6 months is an other-specified example (p. 488). Adjustment disorder also requires its own onset and offset criteria; its offset clock is tied to stressor/consequence termination.
- **Other posttraumatic disorders:** symptoms after trauma are not automatically PTSD. If they meet another disorder's criteria, give that diagnosis instead of or in addition to PTSD. Exclude other diagnoses that PTSD better explains (e.g., panic attacks occurring only with trauma reminders). Severe responses may warrant an extra diagnosis, such as dissociative amnesia.
- **Acute stress disorder:** limited to 3 days to 1 month after exposure. PTSD requires >1 month.
- **OCD:** intrusions are obsessions, not tied to an experienced trauma, compulsions are usually present, and other PTSD symptoms are absent.
- **Panic disorder and GAD:** the arousal, dissociation, avoidance and worry are not tied to a specific traumatic event.
- **Separation anxiety disorder:** symptoms concern separation from home or family, not a trauma.
- **Major depressive disorder:** has no B or C symptoms and lacks several D and E symptoms. If both are fully met, diagnose **both**.
- **ADHD:** attention problems begin **before age 12**. In PTSD they begin after Criterion A and relate to alertness to danger and startle.
- **Personality disorders:** interpersonal problems that **began or greatly worsened after trauma** suggest PTSD. In a personality disorder they would be present regardless of trauma.
- **Dissociative disorders** (amnesia, DID, depersonalization/derealization): may occur with or without trauma. If full PTSD criteria are met, consider PTSD **with dissociative symptoms**.
- **Functional neurological symptom disorder:** new somatic symptoms in the setting of posttraumatic distress may point to PTSD instead.
- **Psychotic disorders** (and mood disorders with psychotic features, delirium, substance-induced or medical causes): flashbacks are directly trauma-related and occur **without other psychotic or substance-induced features**.
- **TBI / neurocognitive disorder:** the two often co-occur (combat, blasts, abuse, IPV, MVAs). Postconcussive symptoms (headache, dizziness, light or sound sensitivity, irritability, poor concentration) occur in both. **Reexperiencing and avoidance point to PTSD**. **Persistent disorientation and confusion point to TBI**. Memory for the event: in TBI it is an encoding failure; in PTSD, dissociative amnesia. Sleep problems occur in both.
- **Prolonged grief disorder:** see the PGD file. PGD involves yearning and relationship-focused memories. PTSD involves here-and-now perceptual reexperiencing of the event.

## Comorbidity
- More likely than people without PTSD to meet criteria for ≥1 other disorder, especially depressive, bipolar, anxiety and substance use disorders.
- Increased risk of **major neurocognitive disorder**.
- Women were more likely to develop PTSD after mild TBI in one US study.
- Young children: most have ≥1 other diagnosis, mainly **ODD and separation anxiety disorder**.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)

Use examples only when supported by the actual assessment. Attribute client/collateral reports, distinguish observations from interpretations, and leave unassessed findings unknown.
- **Criterion A:** name the event, the date (or period for chronic exposure), the route (A1–A4; ≤6: A1–A3), and why it qualifies (threat to life, serious injury, sexual violence). For A3, record that the death was violent or accidental. For A4, record the work context and, if media was involved, that it was work related. For multiple traumas, record an index event if useful; when none can be selected, document the relevant exposure period rather than forcing one.
- **B (≥1):** frequency and content of intrusive memories ("3–4×/week, unbidden, vivid images of..."), nightmares (frequency and themes), flashbacks (description, any loss of awareness), and cue-triggered distress or physiological reactivity (palpitations, sweating at specific reminders). In young children: repetitive trauma play, reenactment, nightmares without clear content.
- **C (≥1):** specific avoided thoughts or feelings (including substance use to numb) and avoided places, people or activities, with the functional cost ("stopped driving on the highway").
- **D (≥2):** gaps in memory for the event, global negative beliefs (record the client's own wording), self- or other-blame, persistent guilt, shame or anger, loss of interest, detachment, emotional numbing. In ≤6: more frequent negative emotion, constricted play, social withdrawal, less positive affect.
- **E (≥2):** anger outbursts, reckless behavior (specific acts), hypervigilance (checking locks, sitting facing the door), startle, concentration, and sleep (onset and maintenance hours, nightmares).
- **Duration and timeline:** date of trauma, symptom onset, and date full criteria were met. Document >1 month. Use **delayed expression** if full criteria were met ≥6 months post-event.
- **Dissociative specifier:** describe depersonalization ("feels like watching self from outside") or derealization ("world looks foggy, unreal"), and document that it is not due to substances or seizures.
- **Rule-outs:** substance or medication effects (intoxication, withdrawal), medical conditions including **TBI** (note any head injury and LOC), ASD (if <1 month), adjustment disorder (if A is not met), MDD, OCD, panic, psychosis (flashbacks vs hallucinations), ADHD (onset before 12?), personality disorder (pre-trauma functioning).
- **MSE phrases:** "hypervigilant, scanning room, startles at hallway noise"; "affect constricted, describes numbing"; "became tearful and tachypneic when describing the assault"; "avoided details of the event"; "thought content: guilt and self-blame, 'should have fought back'"; "no evidence of psychosis; reports trauma-specific intrusive images with intact reality testing".
- **Safety:** document a suicide risk assessment (ideation, plan, attempts, reckless behavior, substance use). PTSD increases the step from ideation to action.
- **Functioning:** work, school or relationship impairment (Criterion G). For ≤6, impairment with caregivers, siblings, peers or at school.
