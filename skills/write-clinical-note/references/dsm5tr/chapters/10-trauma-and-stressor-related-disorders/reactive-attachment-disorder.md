# Reactive Attachment Disorder

> **Spark note:** An internalizing disorder of absent or grossly underdeveloped attachment in a young child who has had pathogenically insufficient care (neglect, repeated caregiver changes, institutions). The child can form selective attachments but has never had the chance to, so when distressed they neither seek nor respond to comfort. The common error is confusing it with autism. In RAD the history is one of neglect, there are no restricted or repetitive interests, and social communication matches the child's overall cognitive level.

## Criteria at a glance
- **A. Inhibited, emotionally withdrawn behavior toward adult caregivers.** Requires **both**:
  1. Rarely or minimally seeks comfort when distressed.
  2. Rarely or minimally responds to comfort when distressed.
- **B. Persistent social/emotional disturbance, ≥2 of 3:**
  1. Minimal social and emotional responsiveness to others.
  2. Limited positive affect.
  3. Episodes of unexplained irritability, sadness or fearfulness, even in nonthreatening interactions with caregivers.
- **C. Extremes of insufficient care, ≥1 of:**
  1. Social neglect or deprivation: basic emotional needs (comfort, stimulation, affection) persistently unmet by caregiving adults.
  2. Repeated changes of primary caregiver that limit stable attachment (e.g., multiple foster placements).
  3. Rearing in unusual settings that severely limit selective attachment (e.g., institutions with high child-to-caregiver ratios).
- **D.** The care in C is presumed to cause the behavior in A (e.g., the A behaviors began after the inadequate care).
- **E.** Criteria for **autism spectrum disorder are not met** (an exclusion).
- **F.** Evident **before age 5**.
- **G.** Developmental age is **≥9 months**.

## Specifiers & severity
- **Persistent:** present for **>12 months**.
- **Severe:** the child shows **all** symptoms, each at relatively high levels.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Core problem: the child rarely turns preferentially to an attachment figure for comfort, support, protection or nurturance. Attachment between the child and the putative caregivers is absent or grossly underdeveloped.
- The child is presumed **capable** of selective attachment. The deficit comes from lack of opportunity, not lack of capacity.
- Diminished positive emotion in routine caregiver interactions, poor emotion regulation, and unexplained episodes of fear, sadness or irritability.
- Do not diagnose before the child can form selective attachments, which is why a **developmental age of ≥9 months** is required.
- Assessment works better with **multiple informants**, to show the symptoms appear across contexts.

## Associated features
- Developmental delays, especially in **cognition and language**, because neglect is the shared cause.
- Stereotypies, plus other signs of severe neglect such as malnutrition and signs of poor care.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- **Prevalence:** unknown and rare in clinical settings. Seen in young children severely neglected before foster care or institutional rearing. Occurs in **<10% of neglected children**, even when the neglect is severe.
- **Course:** neglect is often present from the first months of life. The presentation looks similar from **9 months to 5 years**, though expression varies with cognitive and motor ability. A normative caregiving environment can bring recovery. Without enhanced caregiving, signs may persist for at least several years. Persistent signs in early adolescence may go with social-functioning problems. Little is known about older children, so diagnose **with caution after age 5**.
- **Risk:** serious social neglect is both a diagnostic requirement and the **only known risk factor**, yet most severely neglected children do not develop RAD. **Prognosis depends on the quality of caregiving** after the neglect.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- Data across cultures are limited, and cultural expectations of attachment and caregiving shape how the behaviors present and whether they cause concern. Be cautious in cultures where attachment has not been studied.
- RAD symptoms may be more common when the attachment figures themselves are heavily traumatized (e.g., war zones). Attachment styles may vary among migrant and refugee children during resettlement.

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed.

## Functional consequences
Significantly impairs the young child's ability to relate to adults or peers, with impairment across many early-childhood domains.

## Differential diagnosis
- **Autism spectrum disorder:** both can show dampened positive affect, cognitive and language delay, and impaired social reciprocity, and **both can show stereotypies** (rocking, flapping). They are told apart in four ways:
  - History: ASD rarely has a history of neglect. RAD requires it, though the details may not be available at the first evaluation.
  - Restricted interests and rituals, adherence to routines, and unusual sensory reactions occur in ASD only.
  - Social communication: ASD has a **selective deficit in intentional communication**. In RAD, social communication matches overall intellectual level.
  - Attachment: children with ASD regularly show attachment behavior typical for their developmental level. Children with RAD show it rarely or inconsistently.
  - Structured observation helps.
- **Intellectual developmental disorder:** social and emotional skills match cognitive level, without the profound loss of positive affect and the emotion-dysregulation. Children who have reached a cognitive age of 7–9 months **should show selective attachments** whatever their chronological age.
- **Depressive disorders (young children):** also show reduced positive affect, but depressed young children **still seek and respond to comfort**.
- **Disinhibited social engagement disorder:** same neglect etiology, but DSED shows indiscriminate approach to strangers (externalizing), whereas RAD is withdrawn (internalizing).

## Comorbidity
- Neglect-related conditions: cognitive delay, language delay, stereotypies, and medical conditions such as severe malnutrition.
- Internalizing symptoms may co-occur.
- Links to externalizing problems and ADHD have been suggested but **not clearly established**.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)

Use examples only when supported by the actual assessment. Attribute client/collateral reports, distinguish observations from interpretations, and leave unassessed findings unknown.
- **Criterion A, both needed:** record observed or reported responses to distress, e.g., "When hurt, child did not approach foster mother; when picked up, remained stiff and did not settle." Note who reported it and where it was observed.
- **Criterion B, ≥2:** minimal social responsiveness (little eye contact or social smiling with caregiver), restricted positive affect in play, and episodes of unexplained fear, sadness or irritability during calm caregiver interactions.
- **Criterion C:** document the care history specifically: dates and number of placements, institutional rearing, CPS findings of neglect. If the history is incomplete, record what is known and the sources.
- **Criterion D:** give the timeline linking onset of the behavior to the inadequate care.
- **Developmental age ≥9 months:** cite developmental screening or testing, and note language and cognitive levels.
- **Onset before age 5:** record the age at which the signs were first evident.
- **Rule-outs to document:** ASD (absence of restricted or repetitive behavior, social communication consistent with cognitive level, neglect history present), intellectual developmental disorder, depression (child does not seek or accept comfort), DSED.
- MSE and observation phrases: "affect constricted, little positive affect with caregiver"; "does not seek proximity to caregiver when distressed"; "no differential preference for caregiver over examiner"; "episodic unprovoked tearfulness or irritability".
- Note the **persistent** (>12 mo) and **severe** specifiers when they apply. Record collateral from multiple settings (home, daycare, foster parent).
