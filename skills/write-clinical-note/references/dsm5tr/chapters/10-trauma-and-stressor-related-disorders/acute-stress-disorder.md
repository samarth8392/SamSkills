# Acute Stress Disorder

> **Spark note:** A trauma response (same Criterion A as PTSD) with **≥9 of 14 symptoms** from any of five categories (intrusion, negative mood, dissociation, avoidance, arousal), lasting **3 days to 1 month** after the trauma. Unlike PTSD there are no per-cluster minimums; any 9 will do. If symptoms persist past 1 month and meet PTSD criteria, the diagnosis changes to PTSD. Symptoms resolving before 3 days do not meet acute stress disorder; assess other explanations and clinical needs separately.

## Criteria at a glance
**A. Exposure** to actual or threatened death, serious injury or sexual violence, by ≥1 route. This is identical to PTSD A1–A4:
- Direct experience.
- Witnessing in person.
- Learning it happened to a close family member or friend (a death must have been **violent or accidental**).
- Repeated or extreme exposure to aversive details (occupational). Media does not count **unless work related**.

**B. ≥9 of the 14 symptoms below**, from any category, beginning or worsening after the event:
- *Intrusion*
  1. Recurrent involuntary intrusive distressing memories. *Children:* repetitive trauma-themed play.
  2. Recurrent distressing dreams related to the event. *Children:* frightening dreams without recognizable content.
  3. Dissociative reactions or flashbacks, on a continuum up to complete loss of awareness. *Children:* reenactment in play.
  4. Intense or prolonged psychological distress **or** marked physiological reactions to trauma cues. This one item covers PTSD B4 and B5 together.
- *Negative mood*
  5. Persistent inability to experience positive emotions.
- *Dissociative*
  6. Altered sense of the reality of surroundings or self (seeing oneself from outside, being in a daze, time slowing).
  7. Inability to remember an important aspect of the event (dissociative amnesia, not head injury or substances).
- *Avoidance*
  8. Efforts to avoid distressing memories, thoughts or feelings.
  9. Efforts to avoid external reminders.
- *Arousal*
  10. Sleep disturbance.
  11. Irritability or angry outbursts with little or no provocation (verbal or physical aggression).
  12. Hypervigilance.
  13. Concentration problems.
  14. Exaggerated startle.

**C. Duration:** **3 days to 1 month** after the trauma. Symptoms usually begin immediately, but they must persist ≥3 days.
**D.** Clinically significant distress or impairment.
**E.** Not due to a substance or another medical condition (**e.g., mild TBI**), and **not better explained by brief psychotic disorder**.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- The presentation usually includes an anxiety response with some reexperiencing of, or reactivity to, the event.
- Some people present mainly with **dissociation or detachment**, though they still usually react strongly to reminders. Others present with a strong **anger** response.
- Negative emotions (fear, sadness, anger, guilt, shame) can still be present alongside B5, the inability to feel positive emotions.
- B6 (dissociation) includes depersonalization, derealization, and not registering events one would normally encode.
- Startle (B14) need not be trauma-related.
- B13 concentration: forgetting familiar facts (one's phone number), recent events (something just read), or losing the thread of a conversation.

## Associated features
- **Catastrophic appraisals** of one's role in the event, one's reaction to it, or the chance of future harm. Excessive guilt about not preventing it or not coping better. Symptoms themselves interpreted catastrophically (e.g., numbing taken as a sign of mental decline).
- **Panic attacks** are common in the first month, cued or seemingly spontaneous.
- Chaotic or impulsive behavior: reckless driving, irrational decisions, excessive gambling.
- Children: significant **separation anxiety** and excessive need for caregiver attention.
- **Traumatic bereavement:** symptoms may take the form of acute grief, e.g., intrusive memories of how the person died, disbelief, anger about the death.
- **Postconcussive symptoms** (headache, dizziness, light or sound sensitivity, irritability, poor concentration) are common in ASD and **equally common** in people with and without brain injury, so they may reflect ASD rather than TBI.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- **Prevalence** (within 1 month of trauma): **<20%** after non-assault trauma (MVA, mild TBI, severe burns, industrial accidents) and **19–50%** after interpersonal trauma (assault, rape). Data from Australia, the UK and the US.
- **Course:** cannot be diagnosed until **day 3**. It may progress to PTSD after 1 month or remit within the month. About **half of people who later develop PTSD first present with ASD**. Symptoms may remit, stay the same or worsen, largely depending on ongoing stressors or new trauma.
- **Development:** young children may wake frightened without being able to link dream content to the trauma. Children ≤6 tend to reexperience through play, direct or symbolic (e.g., drawing flames after a fire). They may show no fear at the time of exposure. Parents report anger, shame, withdrawal, or even **excessively bright positive affect**. Children can avoid reminders and also be preoccupied with them (e.g., a child bitten by a dog talks about dogs constantly but will not go outside).
- **Risk factors:**
  - Temperamental: prior mental disorder, high negative affectivity (neuroticism), greater perceived severity of the trauma, avoidant coping. **Catastrophic appraisals** (exaggerated future harm, guilt, hopelessness) are **strongly predictive**.
  - Environmental: the trauma exposure itself and prior trauma history.
  - Genetic and physiological: elevated **acoustic startle reactivity** before the trauma.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- **Culture:** the symptom profile varies, especially for dissociation, nightmares, avoidance and somatic symptoms (dizziness, dyspnea, heat sensations, pain). Norms about expressing extreme emotion shape the reaction. Some groups show **possession or trance-like** dissociation in the first month. Panic is prominent in Cambodians because of **khyâl attacks**. **Ataque de nervios** may follow trauma in Latin Americans.
- **Sex/gender:** more prevalent in women across countries. This reflects more exposure to high-risk trauma (rape, interpersonal violence, childhood sexual abuse), differences in processing, sex-linked neurobiology of the stress response, and sociocultural factors.

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed.

## Functional consequences
Impaired social, interpersonal and occupational functioning after accidents, assault and rape. Anxiety disrupts sleep, energy and attention. Generalized avoidance can lead to missed medical appointments, avoiding driving to necessary appointments, and work absenteeism.

## Differential diagnosis
- **Adjustment disorders:**
  - Use adjustment disorder when the stressor does not meet A but the ASD pattern is present (spouse leaving, being fired).
  - Use it when an A event produces a response that does not meet ASD criteria.
  - Severe reactions to **life-threatening illness** with some ASD symptoms may fit adjustment disorder better.
  - Acute responses dominated by anger, depression or guilt may fit adjustment disorder if its criteria are met and another syndrome does not better explain them. Adjustment disorder involves **rumination**; ASD involves involuntary intrusions.
- **Panic disorder:** panic attacks are common in ASD. Diagnose panic disorder only if attacks are unexpected **and** there is persistent worry about future attacks or maladaptive behavior change.
- **Dissociative disorders:** severe dissociation **without** ASD's characteristic symptoms suggests depersonalization/derealization disorder. Persistent severe amnesia without ASD symptoms suggests dissociative amnesia.
- **PTSD:** ASD must resolve within 1 month. If PTSD criteria are met after >1 month, change the diagnosis to PTSD.
- **OCD:** obsessions, not tied to an experienced trauma, with compulsions and without other ASD symptoms.
- **Psychotic disorders** (schizophrenia, mood disorders with psychotic features, delirium, substance-induced, medical): flashbacks are trauma-specific and occur without other psychotic or substance features. Criterion E also excludes **brief psychotic disorder**.
- **TBI:** a head injury can also be a psychological trauma, and the two can coexist. Reexperiencing and avoidance point to ASD. **Persistent disorientation and confusion** point to TBI. ASD symptoms last at most 1 month.

## Comorbidity
Not discussed separately in the text. Panic attacks and postconcussive symptoms are listed as associated features.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)

Use examples only when supported by the actual assessment. Attribute client/collateral reports, distinguish observations from interpretations, and leave unassessed findings unknown.
- **Criterion A:** event, date and time, and route (direct, witnessed, learned (violent or accidental), occupational). Document the **number of days since exposure** (must be ≥3 days and within 1 calendar month; also record how long the full symptom pattern persisted).
- **Criterion B:** list each endorsed symptom against B1–B14 and **count to ≥9**. Example: "Endorses 10/14 ASD symptoms: intrusive memories, nightmares, cue reactivity, numbing, derealization, avoidance of thoughts, avoidance of the site, insomnia, hypervigilance, startle."
- **Dissociation:** record the client's own description ("everything felt like slow motion", "watching myself from above") and any gaps in memory, distinguishing these from LOC or intoxication.
- **Criterion E rule-outs:** substance or alcohol use at the time of the trauma and since, head injury or LOC (TBI screen), medical condition, and psychotic features (brief psychotic disorder).
- **Associated features:** catastrophic appraisals ("I'm going crazy"), guilt, panic attacks, impulsive behavior, and separation anxiety in children.
- **MSE phrases:** "appears dazed, delayed responses"; "startles to door noise"; "affect blunted; reports cannot feel happy since accident"; "tearful and tremulous when recounting event"; "oriented x4" (helps distinguish from TBI confusion).
- **Plan:** schedule reassessment at the 1-month mark to decide whether to recode as PTSD, document resolution, or reconsider adjustment disorder.
