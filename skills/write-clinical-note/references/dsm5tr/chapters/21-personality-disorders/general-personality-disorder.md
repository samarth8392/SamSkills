# General Personality Disorder

> **Spark note:** These six criteria frame assessment of the ten specific personality disorders; acquired personality change due to a medical condition has its own criteria. The core test is
> *long-term, cross-situational, early-onset, and not explained by something else*. The commonest mistake
> is diagnosing personality pathology from a single cross-sectional interview during an acute episode.

## Criteria at a glance (p. 982)
- **A.** Enduring pattern of inner experience/behavior that deviates markedly from the person's cultural expectations,
  shown in **≥2 of 4** areas:
  1. Cognition (how self, others, events are perceived/interpreted)
  2. Affectivity (range, intensity, lability, appropriateness of emotion)
  3. Interpersonal functioning
  4. Impulse control
- **B.** Inflexible and pervasive across a broad range of personal and social situations.
- **C.** Clinically significant distress or impairment (social, occupational, other).
- **D.** Stable, long duration; onset traceable to at least adolescence or early adulthood.
- **E.** Not better explained as manifestation/consequence of another mental disorder.
- **F.** Not attributable to physiological effects of a substance (drug, medication, toxin) or another medical condition (e.g., head trauma).

## Coding
No code for the general criteria themselves; code the specific PD, or F60.89 / F60.9 (see other-specified-and-unspecified.md).

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls (p. 982–983)
- Traits become a disorder only when inflexible, maladaptive, and cause significant impairment or subjective distress.
- Requires evaluating **long-term** functioning; features evident by early adulthood.
- Separate traits from reactions to situational stressors or transient states (bipolar, depressive, anxiety disorders; intoxication).
- Assess stability across time and situations; a single interview can suffice but often multiple spaced interviews are needed.
- Traits are often **ego-syntonic** → use collateral informants.

## Development & course (p. 983)
- Recognizable in adolescence/early adulthood; relatively stable.
- Antisocial and borderline PD tend to become less evident or remit with age; less so for OCPD and schizotypal.
- Children/adolescents: use only when maladaptive traits are pervasive, persistent, unlikely to be stage-limited or due to
  another disorder; must be present **≥1 year** if <18 y. Childhood traits often do not persist unchanged.
- **Antisocial PD cannot be diagnosed <18 y.**
- People may first present late in life; PD may worsen after loss of supportive persons (e.g., spouse) or stabilizing
  situations (e.g., job).
- New personality change in middle/late adulthood → evaluate for personality change due to AMC or unrecognized SUD.

## Culture-related issues (p. 983–984)
- Emotion regulation and interpersonal functioning are culturally shaped; judge against the person's ethnic, cultural, social background.
- Do not confuse with acculturation problems after migration, or expression of customs, religious or political values.
- Apparently rigid behavior may be adaptive to cultural constraints (e.g., staying in an abusive relationship where divorce
  is proscribed; conscientious political protest is not necessarily callousness).
- Improve accuracy by attending to cultural conceptions of self/attachment, clinician cultural bias, instruments not normed
  for the population, and social determinants (poverty, acculturative stress, racism, discrimination).
- Collateral from informants familiar with the person's culture is useful.

## Sex- and gender-related issues (p. 984)
- ASPD diagnosed more in men; borderline, histrionic, dependent more in women (BPD difference may reflect help-seeking).
- Guard against over/underdiagnosis driven by gender-role stereotypes.
- Insufficient evidence on cis- vs. transgender differences.

## Differential diagnosis (p. 984–985)
- **Other mental disorders & traits:** Diagnose PD only if features appeared before early adulthood, typify long-term
  functioning, and do not occur only during another disorder's episode. Early-onset persistent disorders (e.g., persistent
  depressive disorder) may be hard and not useful to separate. Some "spectrum" links: schizotypal–schizophrenia;
  avoidant–social anxiety disorder. Subthreshold traits ≠ PD.
- **Psychotic disorders:** Paranoid/schizoid/schizotypal exclusion when pattern occurs only during psychotic illness or
  mood disorder with psychotic features. If PD preceded schizophrenia, record PD + "(premorbid)".
- **Anxiety and depressive disorders:** Be cautious diagnosing PD during episodes; they mimic traits and bias retrospective report.
- **PTSD:** Personality changes that emerge/persist after extreme stress → consider PTSD.
- **Substance use disorders:** Don't base PD on intoxication/withdrawal effects or behavior that sustains use (e.g., antisocial acts).
- **Personality change due to AMC:** Enduring change caused physiologically by a medical condition (e.g., brain tumor).

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
These are assessment prompts, not observed findings. Record only assessed/reported evidence, attribute its source, and preserve uncertainty; an MSE list does not describe every person with the diagnosis.

- **Criterion A mapping:** name the ≥2 domains affected with concrete examples (e.g., "interprets neutral feedback as hostile"
  = cognition; "rapid anger escalation across work and home" = affectivity/impulse control).
- **Criterion B:** describe the broad range of contexts (such as work, family, intimate, or treatment relationships) in which the pattern appears; the source gives no numerical context count.
- **Criterion C:** functional consequences — job losses, relationship ruptures, legal problems, subjective distress.
- **Criterion D:** timeline evidence — examples from adolescence/early adulthood (school records, collateral history);
  if <18, document ≥1 year duration.
- **Rule-outs to document:** current mood/anxiety episode status; substance use pattern and whether traits predate/persist
  beyond use; medical/neurological history (head injury, seizures, endocrine); psychotic disorder history; trauma exposure.
- **Cultural formulation:** note cultural norms considered, informant input, and why the behavior exceeds cultural expectations.
- Note ego-syntonicity and source of information (self-report vs. collateral vs. observation over multiple sessions).
