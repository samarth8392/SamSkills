# Assessment Measures: Overview (Section III)

> **Spark note:** This chapter gives the DSM's **dimensional** tools that sit beside the categorical diagnoses. There are three families: **cross-cutting symptom measures** (a psychiatric "review of systems" that runs across diagnoses), **disorder-specific severity measures**, and the **WHODAS 2.0** for disability. None of them makes a diagnosis. They flag areas to explore, put numbers on severity and track change over time (measurement-based care). A common mistake is to read a Level 1 "positive" as a diagnosis. It only means the domain needs more inquiry.

## Why the chapter exists (p. 1108)
- The text gives these limits of a purely categorical system:
  - no "zones of rarity" between disorders
  - a need for in-between categories (e.g., schizoaffective disorder)
  - high comorbidity
  - heavy use of other specified/unspecified diagnoses
  - few unique validators
  - poor treatment specificity
- Dimensional ratings capture **severity of individual symptoms** (intensity, duration, functional impact). That includes symptoms that are associated with a disorder but are not criteria.
- They also pick up **subthreshold yet disabling** presentations that need treatment even though no disorder's criteria are met.
- Until biomarkers exist, the dimensional approach rests on the person's self-report plus the clinician's interpretation. Practice guidelines endorse it.

## What is printed vs online (p. 1110)
- **Printed in the book:** the adult self-rated Level 1, the parent/guardian-rated Level 1, the Clinician-Rated Dimensions of Psychosis Symptom Severity and the WHODAS 2.0.
- **Online only** (www.psychiatry.org/dsm5): the child-rated Level 1 (ages 11–17), all Level 2 measures, the other disorder-specific severity measures, the WHODAS proxy version and the CFI supplementary modules. The book leaves the child-rated Level 1 out of print because its items, scoring and instructions are similar to the parent version.

## Notable points stated in the text
- The cross-cutting measures were built for use **at the first interview and at follow-up**. They are core tools for **measurement-based care** (p. 1108–1109).
- Field trials found the adult Level 1 and parent Level 1 **clinically useful with good reliability** in US/Canadian adult and US pediatric samples. Patients said seeing their own ratings made the encounter easier to talk through (p. 1111).
- The severity measures cover many but not all diagnoses. The text names GAD, social anxiety disorder, psychotic disorders, PTSD, autism spectrum disorder and social (pragmatic) communication disorder (p. 1109).
- **Functioning matters for diagnosis.** Most criteria sets require clinically significant distress or impairment.
  - People with mental disorders tend to have *more* severe functional impairment than people with chronic medical conditions.
  - Residual impairment after symptoms resolve can predict recurrence or relapse, e.g., in MDD and anxiety disorders (p. 1109).

## Pitfalls and practice logic
- **A screen is not a diagnosis.** A Level 1 threshold means "inquire further". The next step can be a Level 2 measure, a clinical interview or both. Clinical judgment governs (p. 1113–1114).
- **The threshold differs by domain.** Most domains trigger at **mild (2)**. Suicidal ideation, psychosis and substance use (adult) trigger at **slight (1)**. On the parent form, inattention and psychosis trigger at slight, and the yes/no items trigger on **Yes** *or* **Don't Know**.
- **Score the highest item in each domain**, not the mean. Write it in the "Highest Domain Score" column.
- **Some domains have no Level 2 measure** (adult suicidal ideation, psychosis, memory, dissociation, personality functioning). A positive screen there sends you straight to a clinical interview or a disorder-specific tool.
- **Use the same informant over time** (same parent/guardian or caregiver) so that changes across visits can be interpreted (p. 1113–1114).
- **WHODAS documents impairment.** It helps support Criterion "clinically significant distress or impairment" language with numbers, and it tracks functional recovery separately from symptom remission.

## Shared conventions
- 0–4 frequency/severity anchors on the cross-cutting forms: 0 none/not at all; 1 slight/rare (less than a day or two); 2 mild/several days; 3 moderate/more than half the days; 4 severe/nearly every day.
- The psychosis dimensions use 0–4 severity anchors: none, equivocal, present-mild, present-moderate, present-severe. The rating is for the **worst point in the past 7 days**.
- WHODAS item anchors run from 1 none to 5 extreme. The DSM-5-TR **average** scores map back onto that 1–5 metric.
- None of these measures has an ICD-10-CM code. They support documentation and do not replace a diagnosis.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Name the instrument, version (self/parent/child/informant/clinician), date and time frame, e.g., "DSM-5 Level 1 CCSM–Adult, self-rated, past 2 wk".
- List the domains at or above threshold, with each one's highest item score. State what follow-up was done, e.g., "Level 2 Depression administered" or "suicide risk assessment completed".
- For trend tracking, record the earlier score next to the current one and say who rated each, so the reader can see the informant was the same.
- Tie WHODAS domain or general disability scores to the impairment criterion of the working diagnosis, e.g., "WHODAS average general disability 3.1 (moderate), consistent with functional impairment criterion".
- Record when a measure could not be completed (capacity, language, literacy) and what was used instead (informant/proxy version, interpreter).
