# Schizophrenia Spectrum and Other Psychotic Disorders — Overview

> **Spark note:** The chapter is defined by abnormality in ≥1 of **five domains**: delusions,
> hallucinations, disorganized thinking (speech), grossly disorganized/abnormal motor behavior
> (incl. catatonia), and negative symptoms. Disorders are separated mainly by **which domains**
> are involved, **how long** the disturbance lasts, **how mood episodes line up in time** with
> the psychosis, and **cause** (substance/medication, medical condition). The commonest
> error is diagnosing a primary psychotic disorder before excluding mood disorders with
> psychotic features, substances and medical causes, and before counting duration correctly. (pp. 208–211)

## What unites the chapter / how disorders differ

The chapter runs along a **gradient of psychopathology** (p. 210):
1. Subthreshold or one-domain conditions first: **schizotypal (personality) disorder**
   (subthreshold), **delusional disorder** (delusions only), **catatonia** (psychomotor only).
2. Then **time-limited** conditions: **brief psychotic disorder**, **schizophreniform disorder**.
3. Then **schizophrenia** / **schizoaffective disorder**, which require exclusion of other causes of psychosis.
4. Etiologic categories: **substance/medication-induced** and **due to another medical condition**.
5. Residual categories: other specified (F28) / unspecified (F29); unspecified catatonia.

### Duration and structure ladder

| Disorder | Required domains (Crit A) | Duration | Functional decline required? | Mood rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Schizotypal PD | Subthreshold odd beliefs/perceptions + interpersonal deficits | Pervasive pattern (from early adulthood) | Personality-level | n/a |
| Delusional disorder | ≥1 delusion; schizophrenia Crit A never met | ≥1 month | No: functioning not markedly impaired apart from the delusion | Mood episodes brief relative to delusional periods |
| Brief psychotic disorder | ≥1 of 4 (≥1 must be delusions/hallucinations/disorg. speech) | ≥1 day, <1 month, full return to baseline | No | Not better explained by mood disorder w/ psychotic features |
| Schizophreniform | ≥2 of 5 (≥1 from items 1–3), ≥1 month active phase | ≥1 month, <6 months total | **No** | Mood episodes absent or minority of active+residual |
| Schizophrenia | ≥2 of 5 (≥1 from items 1–3), ≥1 month active phase | ≥6 months continuous signs | **Yes** (Crit B) | Mood episodes absent or minority of active+residual |
| Schizoaffective | Major mood episode concurrent with schizophrenia Crit A | Uninterrupted period; ≥2 wk delusions/hallucinations without mood episode | No (not a criterion) | Mood episodes present for **majority** of active+residual illness |
| Substance/medication-induced | Delusions and/or hallucinations only | During/soon after intoxication/withdrawal/exposure; independent disorder suspected if persists ~1 month after | Distress/impairment | n/a |
| Due to another medical condition | Prominent delusions or hallucinations | Tied to medical course | Distress/impairment | n/a |
| Catatonia (3 diagnoses) | ≥3 of 12 psychomotor features | None stated | Distress/impairment (medical type) | n/a |

## Key features that define the psychotic disorders (pp. 208–210)

### Delusions
- **Definition:** fixed beliefs that do not change in the face of conflicting evidence.
- **Themes:** persecutory (most common), referential (also common), grandiose, erotomanic,
  nihilistic (conviction that a major catastrophe will happen), somatic (health/organ function), religious.
- **Bizarre vs nonbizarre:** bizarre = clearly implausible, not understandable to same-culture
  peers, not derived from ordinary life experience (e.g., organs replaced by an outside force with
  no scar). Nonbizarre example: believing police surveillance without convincing evidence.
- **Loss-of-control delusions are generally bizarre:** thought withdrawal, thought insertion,
  delusions of control (body/actions manipulated by an outside force).
- **Delusion vs strongly held idea:** depends on the **degree of conviction** despite clear or reasonable contrary evidence.
- **Culture:** beliefs such as the evil eye, illness caused by curses or spirit influence may be normative in some contexts. Elevated religiosity can still be part of psychosis.
- **Trauma:** survivors of torture, political violence or discrimination may report fears that look
  persecutory but represent fear of recurrence or posttraumatic symptoms. Judge whether the fear is
  justified given the trauma.

### Hallucinations
- Perception-like experiences **without external stimulus**; vivid, with the force of normal perception, **not under voluntary control**.
- Any modality; **auditory** most common in schizophrenia-related disorders. Voices (familiar or not) are experienced as distinct from one's own thoughts.
- Must occur with a **clear sensorium**. Hypnagogic and hypnopompic experiences are within normal range.
- May be a normal part of religious experience in some cultural contexts.

### Disorganized thinking (speech)
- Formal thought disorder, inferred from speech: **derailment/loose associations** (topic switching),
  **tangentiality** (oblique or unrelated answers), **incoherence/"word salad"** (rare; resembles receptive aphasia).
- Must be severe enough to **substantially impair communication**. Mild disorganization is common and nonspecific.
- Hard to judge across linguistic backgrounds. Glossolalia and possession trance are not psychosis
  unless accompanied by other clearly psychotic symptoms.
- Milder forms occur in prodromal and residual phases of schizophrenia.

### Grossly disorganized or abnormal motor behavior (including catatonia)
- Ranges from childlike "silliness" to unpredictable agitation. Disrupts goal-directed behavior and ADLs.
- **Catatonic behavior** = marked decrease in reactivity to the environment: negativism → rigid,
  inappropriate or bizarre posture → mutism and stupor. Also **catatonic excitement** (purposeless,
  excessive motor activity), stereotyped movements, staring, grimacing, echolalia.
- Catatonia is **nonspecific**: it occurs in mood disorders and medical conditions, not only schizophrenia.

### Negative symptoms
- Account for much of schizophrenia's morbidity. Less prominent in other psychotic disorders.
- Two most prominent in schizophrenia: **diminished emotional expression** (face, eye contact,
  prosody, emphatic hand/head movements) and **avolition** (less self-initiated purposeful activity).
- Others: **alogia** (less speech output), **anhedonia** (reduced capacity for pleasure; in
  schizophrenia, in-the-moment enjoyment and recall may be preserved but the person engages less
  often), **asociality** (apparent lack of interest in social interaction; may reflect limited opportunity rather than a symptom).

## Severity: Clinician-Rated Dimensions of Psychosis Symptom Severity (pp. 211–212; Section III)
- Psychotic disorders are heterogeneous. Symptom severity predicts aspects such as cognitive and neurobiological deficits.
- Section III ("Assessment Measures") holds the dimensional instrument. It rates the primary
  psychotic symptoms: **hallucinations, delusions, disorganized speech, abnormal psychomotor behavior, negative symptoms**,
  plus **depression**, **mania** and **cognitive impairment**.
- **Scale:** each domain is rated for **current severity, defined as the most severe in the last 7 days**, on a
  **5-point scale, 0 (not present) to 4 (present and severe)**.
- **Disorganized speech is not rated** for substance/medication-induced psychotic disorder or
  psychotic disorder due to another medical condition. Their severity specifiers list delusions,
  hallucinations, abnormal psychomotor behavior and negative symptoms only.
- Mood ratings flag mood pathology needing treatment. Mood severity in psychosis has prognostic value.
- Cognition: impairments across domains predict function. Brief assessment can suffice for
  diagnosis. If formal neuropsychological testing is done, trained personnel should administer and score it. Otherwise, use the best available information.
- The severity specifier is **optional** for every disorder ("can be made without using this severity specifier").
- The text frames these measures as prototypes to stimulate research. Clinical utility is still being established.

## Cultural considerations (p. 212)
- Use culturally adapted or validated tools and the Cultural Formulation Interview.
- When interviewing via interpreters or in a 2nd/3rd language, do not mistake unfamiliar metaphors for delusions.
- Underserved ethnic and racialized groups (in the US especially African Americans) are more likely to be
  **misdiagnosed with schizophrenia** when the true diagnosis is a mood disorder with psychotic features (see schizophrenia and schizoaffective files).

## TR changes and notable updates in the text
- **Catatonia coding:** in the TR, catatonia associated with another mental disorder is coded **F06.1**
  as an *additional* code after the primary disorder (e.g., F25.1 then F06.1). Brief psychotic,
  schizophreniform, schizophrenia and schizoaffective disorders each carry a "with catatonia" specifier with this coding note.
- **Unspecified catatonia** = R29.818 first, then F06.1.
- **Schizoaffective Criterion C** (carried from DSM-5): mood episodes must be present for the
  **majority** of total illness (DSM-IV said "substantial portion"). Expect fewer schizoaffective
  diagnoses and more shifts to schizophrenia.
- **Substance-induced coding:** the 4th character encodes comorbid use disorder (1 = mild, 2 =
  moderate/severe, 9 = none). Do not give a separate use-disorder diagnosis.
- Text updates: anosognosia framed as a symptom of the illness itself. Expanded culture, racism and
  misdiagnosis discussion. Social-adversity risk factors (urbanicity, migration, discrimination,
  ethnic density effect). NMDA receptor autoimmune encephalitis named as a medical cause. Danish
  registry: 32% of substance-induced psychosis converts later (44% for cannabis).
- **Attenuated psychosis syndrome** is listed as an example under Other Specified (F28). It is also in Section III, Conditions for Further Study.

## Chapter-level pitfalls and differential logic
1. **Mood timing is the key cut.**
   - Psychosis **only during** mood episodes → MDD/bipolar *with psychotic features*.
   - Psychosis ≥2 wk **without** mood episode **and** mood episodes for the **majority** of illness → **schizoaffective**.
   - Mood episodes for a **minority** of illness → **schizophrenia/schizophreniform**.
   - Delusions only, with mood episodes **brief** relative to delusional periods → **delusional disorder**. If mood episodes are not brief → assess **other specified/unspecified psychotic disorder (F28/F29)** plus the appropriate residual depressive or bipolar disorder (p. 217).
2. **Count duration from prodrome through residual.** <1 mo = brief psychotic; ≥1 but <6 mo =
   schizophreniform (label "provisional" if not yet recovered); ≥6 mo = schizophrenia. Successful
   treatment can shorten the 1-month active phase ("or less if successfully treated").
3. **Exclude substances and medical causes before any primary diagnosis.** Use timing (onset during/soon
   after use; remission ~1 month after cessation), UDS/BAL, labs. Red flags for a medical cause: **atypical age at onset** (e.g.,
   new delusions in men >35 or onset in late life), **visual/olfactory hallucinations**, dreamlike quality,
   **temporal link to medical illness**. No single hallucination type is pathognomonic.
4. **Intact reality testing ≠ psychosis.** Substance-induced perceptions the person recognizes as drug effects =
   intoxication/withdrawal *with perceptual disturbances*. Medical hallucinations recognized as such → generally not a psychotic disorder.
5. **Delirium** trumps: psychotic or catatonic symptoms occurring only during delirium are not diagnosed separately.
6. **Delusional disorder vs schizophrenia:** any history of schizophrenia Criterion A (including catatonia + delusions) excludes delusional disorder.
7. **OCD/BDD with absent insight** → keep OCD/BDD with the "absent insight/delusional beliefs" specifier, not delusional disorder.
8. **PTSD:** flashbacks can resemble hallucinations and hypervigilance can look paranoid. Trauma fears may be realistic.
9. **ASD/communication disorders:** schizophrenia is added only if prominent delusions or hallucinations last ≥1 month.
10. **Catatonia:** always rule out medical causes and **neuroleptic malignant syndrome (G21.0)** before
    attributing catatonia to a mental disorder. Most catatonia occurs in **mood disorders**, not schizophrenia.
11. **Culture:** normative religious or supernatural beliefs, bereavement visions of the deceased, glossolalia,
    possession trance, and interpreter/metaphor confusion. Clinician bias is linked to over-diagnosis of
    schizophrenia in Black/African American and Hispanic individuals.
12. **Malingering/factitious:** psychotic symptoms produced intentionally (factitious) or for an understandable external goal (malingering).

## Shared specifiers and coding conventions
- **Course specifiers** (delusional disorder, schizophrenia, schizoaffective): use **only after 1 year** of illness.
  - First episode, currently in acute episode / partial remission / full remission
  - Multiple episodes, currently in acute episode / partial remission / full remission. Multiple episodes = ≥2 episodes, i.e., first episode, remission, and ≥1 relapse.
  - Continuous: criteria-level symptoms for most of the course; subthreshold periods very brief
  - Unspecified
  - Definitions: *acute episode* = symptom criteria fulfilled. *Partial remission* = improvement maintained, criteria only partly met. *Full remission* = no disorder-specific symptoms.
- **With catatonia** (brief psychotic, schizophreniform, schizophrenia, schizoaffective): add **F06.1 catatonia associated with [disorder]**. Code the primary disorder first.
- **Severity:** Clinician-Rated Dimensions, 0–4 per domain, most severe in the past 7 days. Optional.
- **Medical-condition diagnoses** (F06.x): name the condition in the diagnosis and list its own code **immediately before** (e.g., C34.90 then F06.2).
- **Substance-induced:** record [severity] [substance] use disorder **with** [substance]-induced psychotic disorder, with onset during intoxication/withdrawal/after medication use.
