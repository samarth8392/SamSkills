# Schizophrenia

> **Spark note:** ≥2 of 5 core symptoms (≥1 positive: delusions, hallucinations or disorganized
> speech) for ≥1 month, inside **≥6 months** of continuous illness, with **decline in
> functioning**. No single symptom is pathognomonic. Common errors: not counting the
> prodromal and residual phases toward the 6 months; diagnosing schizophrenia when psychosis
> occurs only during mood episodes (mood disorder with psychotic features) or when mood
> episodes fill most of the illness (schizoaffective); and cultural misdiagnosis of mood disorders as schizophrenia. (pp. 223–232)

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** ≥2 of 5, each present for a significant portion of a **1-month** period (less if successfully treated). **≥1 must be items 1–3**:
  1. Delusions
  2. Hallucinations
  3. Disorganized speech (frequent derailment, incoherence)
  4. Grossly disorganized or catatonic behavior
  5. Negative symptoms (diminished emotional expression or avolition)
- **B.** For a significant portion of the time since onset, functioning in ≥1 major area (work, relationships, self-care) is **markedly below** the prior level. With childhood or adolescent onset: **failure to reach** the expected interpersonal, academic or occupational level.
- **C.** Continuous signs for **≥6 months**, including **≥1 month** of Crit A (active phase; less if treated). The rest may be prodromal or residual, shown by **only negative symptoms** or **≥2 Crit A symptoms in attenuated form** (e.g., odd beliefs, unusual perceptual experiences).
- **D.** Schizoaffective and depressive/bipolar disorder with psychotic features are ruled out: no MDE or manic episode **concurrent** with the active phase, **or** mood episodes present for a **minority** of total active + residual duration.
- **E.** Not attributable to a substance or another medical condition.
- **F.** With a history of **ASD or childhood-onset communication disorder**, schizophrenia is added only if **prominent delusions or hallucinations** (plus the other required symptoms) last **≥1 month** (less if treated).

## Specifiers & severity
- **Course specifiers**: use only after **1 year** and if consistent with course criteria:
  - First episode, currently in acute episode: first manifestation meeting symptom and time criteria. Acute episode = symptom criteria met.
  - First episode, currently in partial remission: improvement maintained; criteria only partly met.
  - First episode, currently in full remission: no disorder-specific symptoms.
  - Multiple episodes, currently in acute episode / partial remission / full remission: requires **≥2 episodes**, i.e., first episode, remission and ≥1 relapse.
  - Continuous: criteria-level symptoms for most of the course; subthreshold periods very brief.
  - Unspecified.
- **With catatonia:** add **F06.1 catatonia associated with schizophrenia**. Catatonia occurs in up to 35% of people with schizophrenia.
- **Current severity (optional):** Clinician-Rated Dimensions. Delusions, hallucinations, disorganized speech, abnormal psychomotor behavior and negative symptoms are each rated 0 (not present) to 4 (present and severe) for the most severe level in the **past 7 days**.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- A **heterogeneous** syndrome made up of cognitive, behavioral and emotional dysfunction. Diagnosis rests on a **constellation** of signs together with impairment.
- If active symptoms remit within 1 month with treatment, Crit A is still met if the clinician judges they would have persisted untreated.
- **Crit B:** dysfunction is persistent and not the result of any single feature. **Avolition** ties to social dysfunction, and **cognitive impairment** ties strongly to functional impairment. Comparing with unaffected siblings can help in youth-onset cases.
- **Prodrome/residual:** sub-delusional odd beliefs (ideas of reference, magical thinking), unusual perceptions (sensing an unseen presence), vague but understandable speech, odd but not grossly disorganized behavior (e.g., mumbling in public). **Negative symptoms** are common and can be severe. **Social withdrawal** is often the first sign.
- **Mood:** mood symptoms and full episodes are common and may overlap the active phase, but schizophrenia requires delusions or hallucinations **in the absence of** mood episodes, and mood episodes must total a **minority** of active + residual illness.
- Assess cognition, depression and mania domains.

## Associated features
- Inappropriate affect (e.g., laughing without a stimulus). Dysphoria (depression, anxiety, anger). Disturbed sleep (daytime sleeping, nighttime activity). Little interest in food or food refusal.
- Depersonalization, derealization and somatic concerns, sometimes reaching delusional intensity. Anxiety and phobias are common.
- **Cognitive deficits**, common and strongly linked to vocational and functional impairment: declarative and working memory, language, executive function, **slowed processing speed**, sensory processing, inhibition, attention.
- **Social cognition deficits:** theory-of-mind problems. May read irrelevant stimuli as meaningful, which can seed explanatory delusions. These persist during remission.
- **Anosognosia (lack of insight):** a symptom of the illness, not a coping strategy, and can last throughout the course. It is the **most common predictor of treatment nonadherence** and predicts relapse, involuntary treatment, poorer function, aggression and a worse course.
- **Aggression:** spontaneous or random assault is uncommon. Risk is higher with young male sex, prior violence, nonadherence, substance abuse and impulsivity. Most people with schizophrenia are **not aggressive** and are **more often victimized** than the general population.
- No radiological, lab or psychometric diagnostic test. At group level: reduced overall brain volume with steeper age-related loss, differences in prefrontal and temporal gray matter and white matter connectivity, and eye-tracking and electrophysiological differences.
- **Neurological soft signs:** poor motor coordination, sensory integration and motor sequencing; left-right confusion; disinhibited associated movements. Minor physical anomalies of the face and limbs.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- **Lifetime prevalence about 0.3%–0.7%**, varying fivefold across meta-analyses. Higher with migration or refugee status, urbanicity, country economics and latitude. Some groups are more likely to be misdiagnosed or overdiagnosed.
- **Sex ratio:** negative-symptom, long-duration (poor-outcome) definitions → more men. Broader mood-inclusive or brief definitions → equal. A large worldwide study found no sex difference in prevalence.
- **Onset:** late teens to mid-30s; prepubertal onset rare. **Peak early-to-mid 20s in men, late 20s in women.**
- Most have **gradual onset**: social withdrawal, emotional and cognitive change, and role decline. **Half** show depressive symptoms.
- **Prognosis** depends on duration, severity and gender. Men, especially with long untreated psychosis and poorer premorbid adjustment, have more negative symptoms, more cognitive impairment and worse outcomes. Sociocognitive deficits may precede psychosis and are **refractory to antipsychotics**.
- **Outcomes:** first-episode schizophrenia pooled **remission 56%** (mild or absent symptoms ≥6 months) and **recovery 30%** (symptomatic and functional improvement >2 years) across 79 studies. Broadly defined schizophrenia: median recovery **13.5%** (50 studies).
- Psychotic experiences tend to lessen in late life. **Cognition declines before psychosis and then stays relatively stable.** Developmental negative symptoms are stable traits. Negative symptoms that arise after psychosis onset vary more and may be **secondary**.
- Not generally a progressive neurodegenerative disorder, but severe chronic cases may show progressive dysfunction.
- **Children:** harder to diagnose. Delusions and hallucinations are less elaborate. **Visual hallucinations are more common**; distinguish them from fantasy play. Weigh ASD and ADHD first. Childhood onset resembles poor-outcome adult cases (gradual onset, prominent negative symptoms). It is preceded by nonspecific emotional-behavioral disturbance, intellectual and language changes and subtle motor delays.
- **Late onset (>40 years):** more women, often married. Psychotic symptoms predominate, with affect and social function preserved. It is unclear whether this is the same condition as onset before midlife (e.g., before 55).
- **Environmental risk:** season of birth (late winter/early spring; summer for the deficit form); urban upbringing; refugees, some migrant groups and socially oppressed groups facing discrimination; social deprivation and adversity. Positive and negative symptom severity correlates with **adverse childhood experiences** (trauma, neglect).
  - **Ethnic density effect:** higher rates for ethnic and racialized groups living where few others share their ethnicity. Possible reasons: discrimination, less support and more stigma, isolation, and fewer normalizing explanations for unusual experiences.
- **Genetic/physiological:** strong genetic contribution, yet **most patients have no family history** of psychosis. Polygenic common and rare alleles overlap with bipolar, depression and ASD. Obstetric hypoxia, **older paternal age**, and prenatal stress, infection, malnutrition, maternal diabetes and other conditions are linked to risk, but most exposed offspring are unaffected.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- **Culture:** the ratio of visual to auditory hallucinations varies (visual relatively higher in some regions). Delusion and hallucination content (command, abusive, religious) and associated fear vary. Evil eye, curses and spirit beliefs may be normative. Hearing God's voice can be normal religious experience.
- Narrative style differences complicate judging disorganized speech. Affect, eye contact and body language norms vary. **Alogia** must not be confused with language barriers.
- Culturally patterned distress may present as pseudo-hallucinations or overvalued ideas that resemble psychosis but are normative for the subgroup.
- **Misdiagnosis** of mood or other disorders as schizophrenia is more likely in underserved ethnic and racialized groups (in the US, especially **African Americans**). Possible causes are clinician bias, racism and discrimination, which limit information quality.
- **Sex/gender:** women have later onset with a **second midlife peak**, more affect-laden symptoms, more psychotic symptoms with a tendency to worsen late in life, fewer negative symptoms and less disorganization, and better-preserved social function.
- **Estrogen link:** symptoms worsen premenstrually; admissions rise before and during menses. Menopause may contribute to the midlife peak. Symptoms improve in pregnancy and **worsen postpartum**.

## Suicide risk
- About **5%–6% die by suicide**, about **20% attempt** at least once, and many more have significant SI.
- Suicide may follow **command hallucinations** to harm self or others.
- Risk persists across the lifespan. It is highest in **younger men with comorbid substance use**.
- Risk factors: depressive symptoms, hopelessness, unemployment, **period after a psychotic episode or hospital discharge**, number of admissions, recency of onset, older age at onset.
- Depressive symptoms during first-episode psychosis predict later suicidal behavior.
- Attempt risk factors: alcohol, tobacco and drug abuse; depression; number of hospitalizations; physical comorbidity; family history of depression or suicidal behavior.
- Suicide (death) risk factors: **male sex, younger age, higher IQ, prior attempts, hopelessness, poor adherence**.

## Functional consequences
- Significant social and occupational dysfunction.
- Reading deficits exceed what general cognitive impairment predicts. This is conceptualized as secondary or acquired dyslexia.
- Avolition impairs school and work even when cognitive skills are adequate. Most work at a lower level than their parents. Most, especially men, do not marry or have limited social contact outside the family.

## Differential diagnosis
- **MDD/bipolar with psychotic or catatonic features:** depends on timing and mood severity. Psychosis **only during** mood episodes → mood disorder with psychotic features.
- **Schizoaffective:** MDE or manic episode concurrent with the active phase **and** mood symptoms present for the **majority** of total active illness.
- **Schizophreniform / brief psychotic:** shorter duration (<6 months; ≥1 day to <1 month).
- **Delusional disorder:** absence of the other characteristic symptoms (prominent AH/VH, disorganization, catatonia, negative symptoms).
- **Schizotypal PD:** subthreshold symptoms tied to persistent personality features.
- **OCD and BDD:** may have poor or absent insight with delusional-intensity preoccupations, but are marked by obsessions, compulsions, appearance or odor preoccupation, hoarding, or body-focused repetitive behaviors.
- **PTSD:** flashbacks may seem hallucinatory and hypervigilance may seem paranoid. Requires a trauma plus reliving and reactivity symptoms.
- **ASD / communication disorders:** social-interaction deficits plus restricted, repetitive behavior. Schizophrenia is comorbid only with full criteria **plus prominent delusions/hallucinations ≥1 month**.
- **Other mental disorders with a psychotic episode (delirium, NCD):** psychosis is temporally tied to cognitive change.
- **Substance/medication-induced psychotic disorder:** timing of substance use relative to onset and remission.
- **Medical condition:** psychosis must be persistent and not physiologically caused.

## Comorbidity
- High rates of **substance use disorders**. **More than half have tobacco use disorder** and smoke regularly.
- Anxiety disorders increasingly recognized. **OCD and panic disorder** are elevated.
- **Schizotypal or paranoid PD** may precede onset.
- **Reduced life expectancy:** weight gain, diabetes, metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular and pulmonary disease. Contributors include poor health maintenance (screening, exercise), medications, lifestyle, smoking and diet, and possibly a shared vulnerability.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **Crit A (≥2, ≥1 of A1–A3):**
  - A1: delusion content and theme, conviction, bizarreness ("believes implant transmits thoughts; unshakable").
  - A2: modality and content; command content ("hears 2 male voices commenting; observed pausing and looking toward ceiling").
  - A3: derailment, tangentiality or incoherence, with an example.
  - A4: disorganized behavior, catatonic signs.
  - A5: blunted affect, reduced prosody or eye contact, avolition ("remains in bed most days, does not initiate activities").
- **Crit B:** concrete decline from premorbid level, e.g., job loss, dropped classes, neglected hygiene, withdrawal from friends. For youth, failure to reach expected milestones. Use collateral.
- **Crit C:** dated timeline of prodrome → active phase (≥1 mo) → residual, with a total of ≥6 months. Note if the active phase was shortened by treatment.
- **Crit D:** systematic mood history. Date any MDE or manic episode and estimate its share of total illness duration.
- **Crit E:** UDS, medication review, labs and neurological exam as indicated.
- **Crit F:** if ASD or communication disorder history, document prominent delusions or hallucinations for ≥1 month.
- **Insight/anosognosia:** record awareness of illness, attitude toward medication and adherence. Lack of insight predicts nonadherence and relapse.
- **Risk:** suicide (command AH, depression, hopelessness, post-discharge period), violence (history, substance use, nonadherence), victimization. Also tobacco/substance use and metabolic monitoring.
- **Cognition:** brief screen of attention, memory and processing speed; it predicts function.
- **Culture:** document the cultural or religious context of beliefs and perceptions, interpreter use, and steps taken to avoid bias.
- MSE phrasing: "grooming poor, malodorous"; "affect blunted, minimal prosody"; "speech sparse (poverty of speech), latency increased"; "thought process tangential with loosening of associations"; "thought content: persecutory delusions, thought broadcasting"; "perception: endorses AH, appears internally preoccupied"; "insight poor: denies illness"; "judgment impaired."
