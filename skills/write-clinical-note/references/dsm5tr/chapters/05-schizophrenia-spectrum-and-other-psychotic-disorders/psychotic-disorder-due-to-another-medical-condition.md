# Psychotic Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition

> **Spark note:** Prominent delusions or hallucinations that are a **direct physiological consequence**
> of a medical condition, as shown by history, exam or labs. Attribution rests on three
> considerations: **biological plausibility, temporality and typicality**. Temporality gives the most
> certainty. Do not overweight any single symptom: visual or olfactory hallucinations raise
> suspicion of a medical cause, but they also occur in schizophrenia and bipolar disorder. (pp. 244–248)

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** Prominent hallucinations or delusions.
- **B.** History, exam or labs show the disturbance is the **direct pathophysiological consequence** of another medical condition.
- **C.** Not better explained by another mental disorder.
- **D.** Not exclusively during **delirium**.
- **E.** Clinically significant distress or impairment.

## Specifiers & severity
- **Code by predominant symptom:**
  - **With delusions (F06.2):** delusions predominate.
  - **With hallucinations (F06.0):** hallucinations predominate.
- **Current severity (optional):** Clinician-Rated Dimensions. Delusions, hallucinations, abnormal psychomotor behavior and negative symptoms (disorganized speech **not** listed), each 0–4, most severe in the past 7 days.
- Also assess cognition, depression and mania domains.

## Coding
| Code | When |
|---|---|
| F06.2 | Psychotic disorder due to [condition], with delusions |
| F06.0 | Psychotic disorder due to [condition], with hallucinations |

- Put the medical condition's **name in the diagnosis**, and **code and list the condition immediately before** it. Example: *C34.90 malignant lung neoplasm; F06.2 psychotic disorder due to malignant lung neoplasm, with delusions*.
- May be coded **alongside** substance/medication-induced psychotic disorder if both contribute, and **alongside** major/mild NCD when the same pathology causes both (e.g., *psychotic disorder due to Lewy body disease, with delusions*).

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Not a **psychologically mediated** reaction to a severe illness. That would be **brief psychotic disorder, with marked stressor**.
- **Hallucinations:** any modality (visual, olfactory, gustatory, tactile, auditory), from simple and unformed to complex. Some etiologies produce characteristic phenomena: **olfactory → temporal lobe epilepsy**.
- If the person keeps **reality testing** and knows the hallucinations come from the medical condition → generally **not** diagnosed.
- **Delusions:** somatic, grandiose, religious, and most commonly **persecutory**. Their link to specific conditions is less specific than for hallucinations.
- **Three guiding considerations:**
  1. **Biological plausibility:** a condition able to cause psychosis (e.g., severe generalized infection, porphyria, lupus, temporal lobe epilepsy).
  2. **Temporality:** onset, exacerbation or remission tracks the medical condition. This gives the **greatest certainty**.
  3. **Typicality:** features atypical for a primary psychotic disorder (atypical onset age, visual or olfactory hallucinations).
  - Then rule out other causes (substances, side effects of medical treatment such as **steroids for autoimmune disease**).
- **Causes:**
  - **Neurological:** neoplasms, cerebrovascular disease, Huntington's, Parkinson's, MS, epilepsy, auditory/visual nerve injury, **deafness**, migraine, CNS infections.
  - **Endocrine:** hyper/hypothyroidism, hyper/hypoparathyroidism, hyper/hypoadrenocorticism.
  - **Metabolic:** hypoxia, hypercarbia, hypoglycemia; **B12 deficiency**; fluid/electrolyte imbalance; hepatic or renal disease.
  - **Autoimmune with CNS involvement:** **SLE**, **anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis**.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- Lifetime prevalence **0.21%–0.54%** (Sweden, Finland). **>65 years: 0.74%** (Finland).
- Most common associations: untreated endocrine and metabolic disorders, autoimmune disorders (SLE, NMDA-R encephalitis), temporal lobe epilepsy.
- **Epilepsy psychosis** is classed as ictal, postictal or interictal. **Postictal** is most common (**2%–7.8%** of people with epilepsy).
- About **60% of older adults with new-onset psychosis have a medical cause.**
- Possibly more common in older women. Otherwise, sex differences follow the underlying conditions.
- **Course:** a single transient state or **recurrent**, cycling with the medical illness. Treating the cause often resolves psychosis, but it can persist (e.g., focal brain injury). Chronic courses occur in MS and chronic **interictal** epilepsy psychosis.
- Phenomenology does not change much with age. Prevalence rises with age due to medical burden (e.g., atherosclerosis). Preexisting cognitive impairment and vision or hearing loss may lower the threshold for psychosis.
- Younger people: epilepsy, head trauma, autoimmune and neoplastic disease. Older people: neurodegeneration (Alzheimer's), stroke, anoxia, multimorbidity.
- **Course modifiers:** identifying and treating the medical condition has the greatest impact. Preexisting CNS injury (head trauma, cerebrovascular disease) predicts a worse outcome.

## Suicide risk
- Not clearly delineated. **Epilepsy and MS** carry higher suicide rates, which psychosis may raise further.

## Functional consequences
- Disability is typically **severe** and varies by condition. It likely improves as the condition resolves.

## Differential diagnosis
- **Delirium:** psychosis occurring only during delirium → no separate diagnosis.
- **Major/mild NCD:** the psychotic disorder can be **added** if the same pathological process causes the psychosis (e.g., Lewy body disease).
- **Substance/medication-induced:** evidence of recent or prolonged use, withdrawal, or toxin exposure (e.g., LSD intoxication, alcohol withdrawal). Onset **within 4 weeks** of intoxication, withdrawal or medication use especially suggests a substance cause. If both apply → give both diagnoses.
- **Primary psychotic disorder / mood disorder with psychotic features:** no demonstrable direct physiological mechanism.
  - Suggests a medical cause: **late onset**; **no personal or family history** of schizophrenia or delusional disorder (warrants thorough work-up); visual or olfactory hallucinations; **dreamlike delusions** (the person as an uninvolved observer).
  - Suggests schizophrenia: AH of voices speaking **complex sentences**.
  - **No pathognomonic signs.** Visual hallucinations occur in schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, and olfactory hallucinations are consistent with schizophrenia. Do not decide on one hallucination type alone.

## Comorbidity
- In people **>80**, associated with concurrent **major NCD (dementia)**. Psychosis commonly accompanies **Alzheimer's** and is a **defining feature of Lewy body disease**.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **Crit A:** describe the prominent delusions or hallucinations, their modality and content, and whether reality testing is intact.
- **Crit B:** name the medical condition and the supporting evidence (exam, labs, imaging, EEG). Document **temporality** (dated onset and exacerbation of both) and **typicality** (atypical age, modality). Coordinate with the medical team.
- **Crit C/D:** document no better psychiatric explanation and a delirium screen (attention, orientation, fluctuation).
- **Substance rule-out:** medication list (especially steroids, anticholinergics, dopaminergics), UDS, timing relative to medication changes.
- **Coding:** list the medical condition code first, then F06.2 or F06.0, with the condition's name in the diagnosis. Choose the predominant symptom.
- **Risk:** severity of impairment, safety, suicide risk (epilepsy, MS).
- MSE phrasing: "reports seeing small figures in room, appreciates may be due to illness" (reality testing intact → may not meet criteria) vs "convinced spouse is an impostor"; "olfactory hallucinations of burning rubber preceding episodes"; "symptoms began 3 days after steroid initiation."
