# Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder

> **Spark note:** A persistent (≥6 months) pattern of developmentally excessive inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity — ≥6 symptoms in a domain (≥5 at age 17+) — with **several** symptoms before age 12, present in **≥2 settings**, clearly impairing, and not better explained by another disorder. Symptoms may vanish in the clinician's office (novel, one-to-one, interesting setting), so collateral informants are essential; and first-ever "ADHD" after age 13 usually means something else (pp. 168–171).

## Criteria at a glance (pp. 168–170)
- **A.** Persistent pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity interfering with functioning/development, via A1 and/or A2. Each: **≥6 symptoms** for **≥6 months**, inconsistent with developmental level, directly impairing social and academic/occupational activities. **Age ≥17: ≥5 symptoms.** Symptoms are not solely oppositional behaviour, defiance, hostility or failure to understand tasks.
  - **A1 Inattention (9):**
    - a. careless mistakes / misses details
    - b. trouble sustaining attention (lectures, conversations, long reading)
    - c. seems not to listen when spoken to directly
    - d. does not follow through / fails to finish tasks, sidetracked
    - e. poor organization (sequential tasks, materials, time management, deadlines)
    - f. avoids/dislikes sustained mental effort (homework; adults: reports, forms, long papers)
    - g. loses necessary items (keys, wallet, phone, paperwork)
    - h. easily distracted by external stimuli (adults: unrelated thoughts)
    - i. forgetful in daily activities (chores, errands; adults: returning calls, bills, appointments)
  - **A2 Hyperactivity-impulsivity (9):**
    - a. fidgets, taps, squirms
    - b. leaves seat when expected to stay
    - c. runs/climbs inappropriately (adolescents/adults: may be only feeling restless)
    - d. cannot play/do leisure quietly
    - e. "on the go," as if motor-driven (uncomfortable being still in restaurants, meetings)
    - f. talks excessively
    - g. blurts answers, finishes others' sentences
    - h. trouble waiting turn
    - i. interrupts/intrudes (takes over others' activities, uses things without asking)
- **B.** Several inattentive or hyperactive-impulsive symptoms **before age 12**.
- **C.** Several symptoms in **≥2 settings** (home, school, work, friends/relatives, other activities).
- **D.** Clear evidence of interference with or reduced quality of social, academic or occupational functioning.
- **E.** Not exclusively during schizophrenia/other psychotic disorder; not better explained by another mental disorder (mood, anxiety, dissociative, personality, substance intoxication/withdrawal).

## Specifiers & severity (p. 170)
**Presentation (based on past 6 months):**
- **Combined (F90.2):** A1 and A2 both met.
- **Predominantly inattentive (F90.0):** A1 met, A2 not.
- **Predominantly hyperactive/impulsive (F90.1):** A2 met, A1 not.

**In partial remission:** full criteria previously met; fewer than full criteria for past 6 months; still impairing.

**Severity:**
- **Mild:** few if any symptoms beyond the required number; no more than minor impairment.
- **Moderate:** between mild and severe.
- **Severe:** many symptoms beyond the required number, or several particularly severe symptoms, or marked impairment.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls (pp. 170–171)
- **Inattention:** wandering off task, not following through, poor sustained focus, disorganization — **not** due to defiance or poor comprehension.
- **Hyperactivity:** inappropriate excessive motor activity, fidgeting, tapping, talkativeness; adults — extreme restlessness, wearing others out.
- **Impulsivity:** hasty acts without forethought, potential harm (darting into street); desire for immediate reward/poor delay of gratification; social intrusiveness; big decisions without considering consequences (taking a job without adequate information).
- Age-12 threshold: earlier onset not specified because retrospective dating is unreliable; **adult recall is unreliable — get ancillary information**. Cannot diagnose with **no** symptoms before 12.
- Symptoms first appearing **after 13** → more likely another mental disorder or cognitive effects of substance use.
- Cross-setting confirmation usually requires informants who see the person in those settings.
- Signs may be minimal/absent with frequent rewards, close supervision, novel settings, especially interesting activities, constant external stimulation (screens), or **one-to-one** situations (the clinician's office).

## Associated features (pp. 171)
- Language, motor, social delays often co-occur (not specific).
- **Emotional dysregulation/impulsivity** common: quick to anger, easily frustrated, emotionally overreactive.
- Academic/work impairment even without SLD.
- Neurocognitive deficits (working memory, set shifting, reaction-time variability, response inhibition, vigilance, planning) — **not sensitive/specific enough to diagnose**.
- Minor physical anomalies (hypertelorism, high-arched palate, low-set ears) may be elevated; subtle motor delays and soft signs.
- Known-cause NDDs (fragile X, 22q11 deletion) often show ADHD symptoms → diagnose ADHD if full criteria met.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- **Prevalence (pp. 171–172):** ~**7.2%** of children worldwide (cross-national range 0.1–10.2%); higher in foster care and correctional settings; **2.5%** of adults (meta-analysis).
- **Course (p. 172):** toddler overactivity hard to separate from normal before age 4; most often identified in **elementary school** when inattention becomes impairing. Relatively stable into early adolescence; some develop antisocial behaviour. Hyperactivity fades in adolescence/adulthood but restlessness, inattention, poor planning, impulsivity persist; a substantial proportion remain impaired as adults.
  - Preschool: mainly hyperactivity. Elementary: inattention more prominent. Adolescence: fidgetiness, inner jitteriness, impatience. Adulthood: inattention, restlessness, impulsivity.
- **Temperamental:** low behavioural inhibition/effortful control, negative emotionality, novelty seeking (not specific).
- **Environmental:** very low birth weight and prematurity (dose-response); prenatal smoking (even after controlling for parental psychiatric history and SES); diet reactions in a minority; lead, encephalitis, in utero alcohol correlated (causality unknown).
- **Genetic:** heritability ~**74%**; GWAS loci in constrained regions, loss-of-function genes, brain-expressed regulatory regions; no single gene. Consider visual/hearing impairment, metabolic abnormalities, nutritional deficiencies as influences; elevated in idiopathic epilepsy.
- **Course modifiers:** early family interaction patterns unlikely to cause ADHD but may shape course or secondary conduct problems.

## Diagnostic markers (p. 173)
- **No biological marker**. Theta/beta EEG findings not replicated in later review; neuroimaging meta-analyses show no differences → **no neuroimaging for diagnosis**.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues (p. 173)
- Regional prevalence differences mainly reflect diagnostic methods (interviews, impairment requirement) and cultural norms for child behaviour and adult interpretation.
- US: lower identification in African American and Latinx populations; symptoms may be **mislabeled as oppositional/disruptive** due to clinician bias → overdiagnosis of disruptive disorders. Higher prevalence in non-Latinx White youth may partly reflect parental demand. Informant ratings influenced by culture of child and informant.
- Male:female ~**2:1** in children, **1.6:1** in adults. Females more often **predominantly inattentive**.

## Suicide risk (p. 173)
- Risk factor for suicidal ideation and behaviour in children.
- Adults: increased suicide attempts when comorbid with mood, conduct or substance use disorders, even after controlling for comorbidity.
- Suicidal thoughts more common than in controls; ADHD predicted persistence of suicidal thoughts in US Army soldiers.

## Functional consequences (pp. 173–174)
- Lower school performance and attainment. Inattention → academic deficits, school problems, **peer neglect**; hyperactivity/impulsivity → **peer rejection**, accidental injury.
- Inconsistent effort misread as laziness, irresponsibility, non-cooperation.
- Adults: poor job stability, lower occupational performance/attendance, more unemployment, interpersonal conflict; less schooling and lower intellectual scores on average (high variability).
- Family discord; lower self-esteem; peer rejection, neglect, teasing.
- Higher risk of **conduct disorder** in adolescence and **antisocial PD** in adulthood → substance use disorders, incarceration.
- More injuries, trauma exposure and PTSD; more traffic accidents/violations; **higher overall mortality** (accidents/injuries); possible obesity and hypertension.

## Differential diagnosis (pp. 174–176)
- **ODD:** resists tasks to resist others' demands (negativity, hostility, defiance) vs ADHD aversion due to difficulty sustaining effort, forgetting, impulsivity. ADHD can develop secondary oppositional attitudes.
- **Intermittent explosive disorder:** serious aggression toward others, no sustained-attention problem, rare in childhood; can be diagnosed with ADHD.
- **Other NDDs:** stereotypic movement disorder = fixed, repetitive movements (rocking, self-biting) vs generalized fidgetiness; **Tourette's** multiple tics may mimic fidgetiness → prolonged observation.
- **SLD:** inattention from frustration/processing deficits, much reduced on tasks not requiring the impaired skill.
- **IDD:** ADHD-like symptoms when academic placement exceeds ability, absent in nonacademic tasks. ADHD in IDD requires symptoms **excessive for mental age**.
- **ASD:** ADHD social dysfunction/peer rejection vs ASD social disengagement and indifference to facial/tonal cues. ASD tantrums from intolerance of change; ADHD tantrums from impulsivity/poor self-control during transitions.
- **Reactive attachment disorder:** social disinhibition without full ADHD cluster; lack of enduring relationships.
- **Anxiety disorders:** ADHD inattention = drawn to novel/stimulating/enjoyable things; anxiety inattention = worry/rumination. Restlessness in ADHD not tied to worry.
- **PTSD:** concentration problems; in children <6, PTSD shows as restlessness, irritability, inattention; parents may minimize and teachers may not know of trauma → **comprehensive trauma history**.
- **Depressive disorders:** poor concentration prominent only during episodes.
- **Bipolar disorder:** episodic vs persistent; elevated mood, grandiosity; ADHD mood shifts within a day ≠ episode (mania/hypomania ≥4 days, even in children). Bipolar rare in preadolescents even with severe irritability; ADHD common in irritable youth.
- **DMDD:** pervasive irritability and frustration intolerance; impulsivity/disorganized attention not essential; most with DMDD also meet ADHD (diagnose separately).
- **Substance use disorders:** hard if ADHD symptoms first appear after substance onset → need informant/records showing ADHD **before** use.
- **Personality disorders (borderline, narcissistic, others):** shared disorganization, intrusiveness, emotional dysregulation; ADHD lacks fear of abandonment, self-injury, extreme ambivalence; extended observation/informants.
- **Psychotic disorders:** not diagnosed if symptoms occur only during psychosis.
- **Medication-induced:** bronchodilators, isoniazid, neuroleptics (akathisia), thyroid replacement → other specified/unspecified other (or unknown) substance-related disorder.
- **Neurocognitive disorders:** complex attention impairment must be a **decline**, usually adult onset; ADHD inattention present before 12, no decline.

## Comorbidity (pp. 176–177)
- Females with ADHD have higher rates of ODD, ASD, personality and substance use disorders.
- **ODD** in ~half of combined presentation and ~a quarter of predominantly inattentive. **Conduct disorder** in ~a quarter of combined presentation (age/setting dependent).
- Most DMDD youth meet ADHD; fewer ADHD youth meet DMDD.
- Anxiety, MDD, OCD, IED in a minority but above population rates. SUDs relatively more frequent but in a minority of adults. Antisocial and other PDs in adults.
- NDDs: SLD, ASD, IDD, language disorders, DCD, tic disorders.
- **Sleep:** ¼–½ report sleep problems — insomnia, circadian rhythm sleep-wake disorder, sleep-disordered breathing, restless legs; daytime sleepiness may meet hypersomnolence disorder; sleep disorders worsen daytime inattention.
- Medical: allergy, autoimmune disorders, epilepsy.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **A1/A2:** count symptoms per domain with concrete examples and frequency ("often"); note the age-17+ threshold of 5. Use rating scales from ≥2 informants (parent, teacher, partner) plus self-report.
- **B:** evidence of several symptoms before 12 — report cards ("talks in class," "doesn't finish work"), parent recall, prior evaluations. Note unreliability of adult retrospective recall and seek collateral.
- **C:** specify the ≥2 settings and who observed.
- **D:** concrete impairment (grades, discipline, job loss, accidents, relationship conflict).
- **E / rule-outs:** mood episodes (episodic vs persistent), anxiety/rumination, trauma history, substance use timeline, sleep (snoring, insomnia, RLS), medications (bronchodilators, thyroid, antipsychotic akathisia), vision/hearing, learning disorder testing, IQ/placement fit, personality features, psychosis.
- Note if presentation in office is calm (expected; does not rule out).
- **Specifiers:** presentation over past 6 months; severity; partial remission.
- **Risk:** suicide screen; driving safety; injury history; substance use.
- MSE phrases: "fidgety, shifting in seat, tapping; left chair twice"; "interrupts examiner; answers before question completed"; "tangential due to distractibility, redirectable"; "reports internal restlessness"; "attention variable, needed repetition of instructions; no evidence of psychosis or mood elevation".
