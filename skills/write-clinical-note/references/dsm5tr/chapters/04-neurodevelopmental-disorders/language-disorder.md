# Language Disorder

> **Spark note:** Persistent problems acquiring and using language — vocabulary, grammar and discourse — in any modality (spoken, written, sign), from comprehension and/or production deficits, substantially below age expectation. Comprehension deficits are often **underestimated** because children use context to guess meaning. A verbal–nonverbal IQ discrepancy is **not** required, and bilingualism does not cause it (pp. 143–146).

## Criteria at a glance (pp. 143–144)
- **A.** Persistent difficulty acquiring and using language across modalities (spoken, written, sign, other) from deficits in comprehension **or** production, including:
  1. Reduced vocabulary (word knowledge and use).
  2. Limited sentence structure (grammar and morphology — putting words and endings together).
  3. Impaired discourse (using vocabulary and connected sentences to explain, describe or converse).
- **B.** Abilities **substantially and quantifiably below** age expectations → functional limits in communication, social participation, academic achievement or occupational performance (any combination).
- **C.** Onset in the early developmental period.
- **D.** Not attributable to hearing/other sensory impairment, motor dysfunction, or another medical/neurological condition; not better explained by IDD or global developmental delay.

## Specifiers & severity
None formally defined; standardized test scores guide severity estimates (p. 144). Assess receptive and expressive skills separately — they may differ in severity.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls (p. 144)
- Usually affects vocabulary and grammar, which then limit discourse.
- Late first words/phrases; smaller, less varied vocabulary; shorter, simpler sentences with grammatical errors, **especially past tense**.
- Word-finding problems; poor verbal definitions; weak grasp of synonyms, multiple meanings, age-appropriate word play.
- Verbal memory problems: trouble following increasingly long instructions, rehearsing strings (phone number, shopping list), remembering novel sound sequences (needed to learn new words).
- Discourse: cannot give key information or narrate a coherent story.
- Diagnosis = synthesis of history, direct observation across contexts (home, school, work), and standardized language test scores.

## Associated features (p. 145)
- People accommodate to limited language: may appear shy or reluctant to talk; prefer talking only with family/familiar people. Not diagnostic, but notable persistent reticence warrants full language assessment.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- **Prevalence:** not given in the text.
- **Course (p. 145):** language develops from toddlerhood to adult competence in adolescence; early vocabulary varies widely. Single early indicators predict poorly, though **late language onset at 24 months** best predicted age-7 outcome in a population sample. By **age 4**, differences are stable and predictive; disorder diagnosed at ≥4 is likely to persist into adulthood (profile of strengths/weaknesses shifts).
- Social consequences across lifespan: peer victimization; females with childhood language disorder may have almost **3×** risk of adult sexual assault.
- **Prognosis (pp. 145–146):** receptive impairment → worse prognosis, more treatment-resistant, frequent reading-comprehension problems.
- **Environmental:** bilingualism neither causes nor worsens it; bilingual children may show delays/differences; a true disorder affects **both** languages — assess both.
- **Genetic:** highly heritable (twin studies); family history common; multiple interacting genes.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- Consider regional, social, cultural/ethnic variation (dialects) (p. 146).
- Bilingual assessment across both languages (p. 146).
- Communication disorders overall more prevalent in boys (p. 143).

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed.

## Functional consequences
Limits in communication, social participation, academics, occupation (Criterion B); peer victimization and later sexual-assault risk (p. 145).

## Differential diagnosis (p. 146)
- **Normal variation:** hard to distinguish before age 4; consider dialects.
- **Hearing/other sensory impairment:** exclude hearing loss as primary cause; diagnose language disorder only if deficits exceed those usually seen with the sensory/speech-motor problem.
- **IDD:** language impairment is often the presenting sign; definitive IDD diagnosis may wait for testing. Language disorder can occur at any IQ — no verbal–nonverbal discrepancy needed.
- **ASD:** delayed language common, but ASD adds lack of social interest/unusual interaction (pulling someone by the hand without looking at them), odd play (carrying toys without playing), unusual communication (knows alphabet but no response to name), rigid routines, repetitive behaviours (flapping, spinning, echolalia).
- **Neurological disorders:** acquired language disorder with epilepsy (acquired aphasia, Landau-Kleffner syndrome).
- **Language regression:** any loss of speech/language at any age → thorough workup; may be seizure symptom → **routine and sleep EEG**. Loss of social-communication behaviours in the first 2 years → assess for ASD.

## Comorbidity (p. 146)
- SLD (literacy, numeracy), IDD, ADHD, ASD, DCD; also SPCD.
- Co-occurs with speech sound disorder in clinical samples, but a US population sample of 6-year-olds found only **1.3%** comorbidity.
- Positive family history of speech/language disorder common.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **A1–A3:** examples of vocabulary (word-finding pauses, vague words "thing/stuff"), grammar (omitted tense markers, short sentences), discourse (cannot retell a story or give sequence of events). Note receptive vs expressive findings separately.
- **B:** name standardized language tests and scores (by SLP); describe functional impact at school/work/social.
- **C:** milestone history (age of first words, first phrases); "late talker" history.
- **D rule-outs:** audiology results; oral-motor/neurological status; IDD/GDD status (IQ or developmental testing); EEG if any regression.
- Bilingual: document language exposure and that deficits appear in both languages.
- MSE phrases: "speech fluent but simplified grammar, frequent tense errors"; "needs instructions repeated and broken into steps"; "difficulty organizing narrative; tangential due to word-finding rather than thought disorder".
