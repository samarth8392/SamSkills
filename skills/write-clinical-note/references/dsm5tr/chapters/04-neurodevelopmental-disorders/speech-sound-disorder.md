# Speech Sound Disorder

> **Spark note:** Persistent trouble producing speech sounds (phonological knowledge and/or articulator coordination) that hurts intelligibility and communication, beginning early and **not** caused by cleft palate, cerebral palsy, hearing loss, TBI or other medical/neurological conditions. Isolated misarticulation of a "late eight" sound can be normal up to age 8 — don't over-diagnose (pp. 146–148).

## Criteria at a glance (p. 147)
- **A.** Persistent difficulty producing speech sounds that interferes with intelligibility or prevents verbal communication of messages.
- **B.** Limits effective communication → interferes with social participation, academic achievement or occupational performance (any combination).
- **C.** Onset in the early developmental period.
- **D.** Not attributable to congenital/acquired conditions (cerebral palsy, cleft palate, deafness/hearing loss, TBI, other medical/neurological conditions).

## Specifiers & severity
None.

## Coding
| Code | Use |
|---|---|
| F80.0 | Speech sound disorder |
- Genetic conditions that impair speech (Down syndrome, 22q deletion, FoxP2 mutation) should also be coded if present (p. 148).

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls (p. 147)
- Speech sound production = clear articulation of phonemes; needs phonological knowledge **plus** coordination of jaw, tongue, lips with breathing and voicing.
- Diagnosed when production is below age/developmental expectation and not due to physical, structural, neurological or hearing impairment.
- **Norms:** age 2 → ~50% intelligible; age 3 → overall intelligible; age 5 → most sounds/words correct per community norms (pp. 147–148).
- **"Late eight"** (l, r, s, z, th, ch, dzh, zh): misarticulating one alone may be normal up to age 8; when several are involved, target some early to improve intelligibility.
- **Lisping** (sibilant errors, frontal or lateral airflow) is common; may go with tongue-thrust swallow.
- Children with the disorder keep using immature phonological simplification processes past the expected age.

## Associated features (p. 147)
- Co-occurring language disorder (rare by age 6); family history of speech/language disorder common.
- If rapid articulator coordination is the problem: history of delayed/incoordinated chewing, mouth closure, nose blowing; may have DCD.
- **Childhood apraxia of speech / verbal dyspraxia** = speech production problems with motor components.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- Boys > girls (~1.5–1.8 : 1) (p. 147).
- Most respond well to treatment and improve; may not be lifelong (p. 148).
- **Worse prognosis with co-occurring language disorder**; then may be associated with SLD.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- Consider regional, social, cultural/ethnic speech variation (p. 148).
- **Bilingual** children tested only in English may show lower intelligibility, more consonant/vowel errors and unusual error patterns — not necessarily disorder.

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed.

## Functional consequences
Interference with social participation, academics, occupation (Criterion B).

## Differential diagnosis (p. 148)
- **Normal variation** in speech (dialect, bilingualism).
- **Hearing/sensory impairment:** diagnose only if errors exceed those expected from the hearing loss.
- **Structural deficits:** e.g., cleft palate.
- **Dysarthria:** motor disorder (e.g., cerebral palsy); distinguished by neurological signs and distinctive voice features; hard under age 3 when body motor involvement is minimal (e.g., Worster-Drought syndrome).
- **Selective mutism:** limited speech in some settings due to anxiety; may arise from embarrassment about a speech disorder, but many speak normally at home/with close friends.

## Comorbidity (p. 148)
- Language disorder (rare at 6), DCD, SLD (when language disorder co-occurs); genetic conditions (Down syndrome, 22q deletion, FoxP2).

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **A:** specific error patterns (substitutions, omissions, distortions), sounds affected, estimated intelligibility to unfamiliar listeners by age.
- **B:** functional effects (teased, avoids speaking in class, others can't understand at work).
- **C:** early onset per caregiver/SLP history.
- **D rule-outs:** audiology; oral-structure exam (cleft, palate); neurological exam (dysarthria); TBI/medical history.
- Note bilingual exposure and whether errors appear in the home language.
- MSE phrases: "speech of reduced intelligibility with consistent /r/ and /s/ distortions; language content age-appropriate"; "lateral lisp"; "speaks freely despite errors (not selectively mute)".
