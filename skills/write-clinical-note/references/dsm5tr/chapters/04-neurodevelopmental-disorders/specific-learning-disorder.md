# Specific Learning Disorder

> **Spark note:** Persistent difficulty acquiring foundational academic skills requires a synthesis of educational history, intervention response, achievement assessment, and functional impact. A low test score alone is insufficient; a gifted individual may compensate through exceptional effort until demands increase (pp. 178–183).

## Criteria at a glance
- **A:** ≥1 of six problems persists ≥6 months despite intervention directed at it: inaccurate/slow effortful word reading; poor reading comprehension; spelling difficulty; impaired written expression (grammar, punctuation, organization or clarity); poor number sense, facts or calculation; impaired mathematical reasoning.
- **B:** Skills are substantially and measurably below age expectations and significantly interfere with school, work or everyday function. Confirm with individual standardized achievement testing plus comprehensive clinical assessment; at age ≥17, documented impairing learning history may replace standardized assessment.
- **C:** Begins in school years; the deficit may become apparent only when workload, complexity, speed or deadlines exceed capacity.
- **D:** Not better explained by intellectual disability, uncorrected hearing/vision problems, another mental/neurological disorder, adversity, inadequate instruction or insufficient proficiency in the instructional language.
- Meet all four through clinical synthesis; no one information source establishes the diagnosis (pp. 178–183).

## Specifiers & severity
Record **every affected domain and subskill** (pp. 179–180):
- Reading: accuracy, rate/fluency, comprehension. *Dyslexia* describes impaired word recognition/decoding and spelling; it does not replace specification of other difficulties.
- Written expression: spelling, grammar/punctuation, clarity/organization.
- Mathematics: number sense, arithmetic fact recall, calculation accuracy/fluency, mathematical reasoning. *Dyscalculia* describes numerical processing, facts and calculation difficulties; record additional subskills separately.
- **Mild:** limited difficulty in one or two domains; accommodations/support can permit compensation.
- **Moderate:** marked deficit in ≥1 domain; periods of intensive specialized instruction needed, with some ongoing accommodation/support.
- **Severe:** several domains seriously affected; intensive individualized instruction through much of schooling and substantial support needed, with possible residual inefficiency despite this.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Persistence means failure to catch up despite extra help; inspect cumulative school records, work samples, intervention measures and previous assessment. Avoidance may reveal impairment in adults.
- Standardized scores ≥1.5 SD below the age mean (roughly ≤78, below seventh percentile) give greatest diagnostic certainty. The source permits clinical judgment around 1 SD below with converging evidence; cutoffs are not natural boundaries. Tests must be appropriate to language/culture (p. 182).
- An IQ–achievement discrepancy is not a required criterion. The source describes generally intact intellectual function, while permitting co-diagnosis with IDD only for learning deficits beyond those expected from IDD (pp. 182, 188).
- Cognitive processing tests, neuroimaging and genetic tests are not diagnostic requirements or established individual diagnostic markers (pp. 183–184).

## Associated features
Uneven abilities and preschool language, attention or motor difficulties are common. Test profiles and putative processing deficits vary; similar academic symptoms need not share one cognitive mechanism (pp. 183–184).

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- School-age prevalence across reading/writing/math: 5%–15% in cited countries; adult prevalence unknown (p. 184).
- Usually identified in elementary school; persistent across life with changing expression. Slow fluency, spelling, comprehension and everyday numeracy may remain after basic decoding improves (pp. 184–185).
- Family history, prematurity/very low birthweight, prenatal/early toxicant exposure, socioeconomic disadvantage and early language problems increase risk. First-degree relative risk cited: approximately 4–8-fold for reading and 5–10-fold for mathematics difficulties (p. 186).
- ADHD and internalizing/externalizing problems may worsen outcome; intensive individualized instruction and social support can improve function (pp. 186, 188).

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
Reading manifestations vary by writing system: English commonly shows inaccurate and slow reading, whereas more transparent orthographies may show slow but accurate reading. Assess proficiency in both native and instructional languages and educational opportunity; do not equate second-language learning or migrant adversity with disorder. Male:female ratios are about 2:1–3:1 (p. 187).

## Suicide risk
The cited adolescent and adult studies associate poor reading/SLD with suicidal thoughts or attempts beyond measured confounders. Depression and witnessing chronic domestic violence further increased risk in the adult study; this is group-level association, not a prediction for an individual (pp. 187–188).

## Functional consequences
Lower educational attainment, dropout, underemployment, reduced earnings and distress may occur. Adequate support moderates adverse mental-health outcomes (p. 188).

## Differential diagnosis
- **Instructional/language variation:** persistent difficulty despite adequate exposure and proficiency supports SLD.
- **IDD:** generalized learning impairment; add SLD only for excess domain-specific deficit.
- **Sensory/neurological causes:** history, examination and sensory assessment identify an alternative cause.
- **Neurocognitive disorder:** acquired decline contrasts with developmental academic difficulty.
- **ADHD:** impaired deployment of learned skills differs from failure to acquire skills; diagnose both if independently met.
- **Psychotic disorders:** later cognitive decline differs from developmental onset; co-diagnosis is possible (pp. 188–189).

## Comorbidity
Reading, writing and mathematics impairments commonly coexist, as do ADHD, language disorders, DCD, ASD, anxiety and depression. Attribute the academic deficit carefully when another disorder also affects testing or learning (p. 189).

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Record the affected skills, ≥6-month course, targeted interventions and actual response; identify the information source and dates.
- Summarize age-appropriate achievement evidence, test language/norms, educational opportunity and concrete school/work/everyday interference.
- Establish school-age onset, including compensated earlier difficulties; at ≥17 state the documented history used if current testing is omitted.
- Document sensory, intellectual, neurological, instructional and language-proficiency alternatives; specify each coded domain, its subskills and support-based severity.
