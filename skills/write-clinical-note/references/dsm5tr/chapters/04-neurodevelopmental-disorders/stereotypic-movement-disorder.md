# Stereotypic Movement Disorder

> **Spark note:** Early-onset patterned repetitive movements qualify when they interfere with activities; self-injury is possible but not required. Repetition alone, especially common self-soothing in young children without interference, does not establish the disorder (pp. 193–197).

## Criteria at a glance
- **A:** Recurrent motor acts appear driven and without an evident goal (e.g., flapping, rocking, head hitting or biting oneself).
- **B:** Movements disrupt social, academic or other activities and may cause bodily injury.
- **C:** Onset during early development.
- **D:** Not a substance/neurological effect or better explained by another developmental/mental disorder (pp. 193–194).

## Specifiers & severity
- **With self-injurious behavior:** includes injury that protective measures currently prevent; otherwise **without self-injurious behavior**.
- Specify association with a known genetic/medical condition, neurodevelopmental disorder or environmental factor; name and additionally code that condition/factor.
- **Mild:** distraction or sensory input readily interrupts movements.
- **Moderate:** explicit protective strategies and behavioral modification required.
- **Severe:** continuous observation and protection needed to prevent major injury.
- Assess frequency, functional effect and degree of tissue damage separately; noninjurious movements can still markedly disrupt daily life (p. 194).

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
Movements tend to be rhythmic, fixed and individually characteristic; episodes can last seconds to minutes or longer, with variable spacing. Excitement, fatigue, stress, boredom or absorbed activity can precipitate them. Apparently purposeless does not exclude a soothing/anxiety-reducing function. Self-restraint or protective devices can conceal injury potential (pp. 194–195).

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- Simple stereotypies occur in 5%–19% of typically developing young children in cited samples; complex movements around 3%–4%. These are movement frequencies, not equivalent to disorder prevalence.
- Stereotypy/self-injury occurs in 4%–16% of cited IDD samples; self-injurious disorder in about 10%–15% of residential IDD samples.
- Usually starts before age 3 and often wanes in typical development; self-injurious patterns with IDD may last years.
- Social isolation, environmental stress, lower cognitive function and neurogenetic syndromes increase risk. Pain (including dental/ear or reflux problems) can contribute and needs evaluation (pp. 195–196).

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
Cultural interpretation and tolerance of movements may alter recognition and help seeking (p. 196).

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed. The self-injury specifier describes repetitive bodily injury; it does not establish suicidal intent.

## Differential diagnosis
- **Normal development:** common, distractible movements without interference do not qualify.
- **ASD:** evaluate social communication and broader repetitive interests. Add this diagnosis with ASD only for self-injury or stereotypies severe enough to need separate treatment attention.
- **Tics:** tend to start later, change over time, remain brief/nonrhythmic and carry a premonitory urge; stereotypies are generally earlier, fixed, rhythmic and longer.
- **OCD:** rituals respond to obsessions or rigid rules; stereotypies lack that organization. Hair pulling/skin picking are more goal-focused and usually begin later.
- **Neurological/medical causes:** examine for chorea, dystonia, myoclonus, dyskinesias, neurogenetic conditions and medication effects.
- **Substance effects:** amphetamine-associated picking/scratching may fit substance-induced OCRD.
- **Functional movements:** sudden onset, marked pattern changes and other functional signs suggest a functional disorder (pp. 196–198).

## Comorbidity
ADHD, coordination deficits, tics/Tourette's and anxiety commonly accompany chronic motor stereotypies (p. 198).

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Describe movement pattern, onset, frequency, duration, settings, triggers and response to redirection; record actual activity interference.
- Separate observed tissue injury from risk currently controlled by devices/supervision; document intent independently if clinically assessed.
- Record required protection and monitoring to support severity, associated conditions and relevant neurological/substance/medication assessment.
- With ASD, explain why self-injury or independent treatment need warrants the additional diagnosis.
