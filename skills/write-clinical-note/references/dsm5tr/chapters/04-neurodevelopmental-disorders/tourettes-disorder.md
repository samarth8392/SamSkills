# Tourette's Disorder

> **Spark note:** Select the diagnosis from lifetime tic type, time since first onset and onset age. The hierarchy is Tourette's → persistent motor/vocal → provisional; temporary reduction in symptoms does not justify a lower-hierarchy diagnosis (pp. 198–201).

## Criteria at a glance
- **A:** Multiple motor tics and ≥1 vocal tic have occurred during the illness; simultaneous occurrence is unnecessary.
- **B:** Tics wax/wane but the span since first tic onset is **>1 year**.
- **C:** Onset before age 18.
- **D:** Not due to substance effects or another medical condition (p. 198).

## Specifiers & severity
No formal severity or motor-only/vocal-only specifier for this diagnosis. Describe tic burden and impairment clinically (pp. 199, 203).

## Coding
| Code | Use |
|---|---|
| F95.2 | Tourette's Disorder |

The source criteria use >1 year for chronic diagnoses and <1 year for provisional disorder. They do not resolve the literal exactly-one-year boundary; clarify chronology and use clinical judgment rather than inventing a different threshold.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
Tics are sudden, recurrent, generally rapid and nonrhythmic movements or sounds. Some are slower tightening/twisting movements. Simple forms use limited muscle groups or nonlinguistic sounds; complex forms include sequences, repetition of words/actions or taboo vocalizations. Coprolalia is neither required nor present in every case. Premonitory discomfort, temporary voluntary suppression and relief afterward are common, but their absence in a young child's report does not exclude tics (pp. 199–201).

The time anchor is **first tic onset**, not an uninterrupted daily run: weeks/months without tics do not restart the clock. Obtain lifetime motor/vocal history before applying the hierarchy. Adult first presentation can reflect mild childhood tics; true new adult onset warrants evaluation for substance, neurological or functional causes (pp. 200–201).

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- Transient childhood tics are common. Tourette's prevalence in cited Canadian school samples is 3–9/1,000; U.S. clinically identified prevalence was 3/1,000. These estimates are not specific rates for every tic diagnosis.
- Typical onset 4–6 years, peak severity 10–12, often declining in adolescence; severe persistence/worsening can occur.
- Genetic influence is strong (heritability estimates 70%–85%); perinatal adversity is associated with risk. Anxiety, excitement and exhaustion often worsen tics; focused calm activity often reduces them. Infectious/immune causation for proposed syndromes remains incompletely established (pp. 201–202).

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
Clinical form appears broadly similar across backgrounds, while interpretation, access and help seeking differ. Lower identified rates among African American and Latinx U.S. groups may reflect care access. Males are affected approximately 2–4 times as often; persistent tics in women may accompany more anxiety/depression (pp. 201–202).

## Suicide risk
The source reports increased attempts and suicide deaths in Tourette's/persistent tic disorder, including after adjustment for psychiatric comorbidity (Swedish odds ratios 3.86 and 4.39). Persistence into adulthood and prior attempts were major predictors. Those chronic-disorder findings must not be treated as a disorder-specific estimate for provisional tics (pp. 202–203).

## Functional consequences
Functional impairment is **not a required criterion** for these three specific tic diagnoses. Many affected people have little distress; others experience bullying, interference with work/school, pain or injury. Suppression itself may consume attention, and ADHD/OCD may create more impairment than tics (p. 203).

## Differential diagnosis
- **Stereotypies:** earlier onset, longer rhythmic/fixed patterns, often soothing, without tic-like urge.
- **Chorea/dystonia:** random flowing movement or sustained co-contraction/postures; use neurological findings.
- **Paroxysmal dyskinesia:** episodic dystonic/choreoathetoid movements often triggered by exertion or voluntary motion.
- **Myoclonus:** very rapid, generally unsuppressible jerks lacking premonitory urge; may persist in sleep.
- **OCD:** action is driven by an obsession/anxiety-prevention rule; tic-related repetition often seeks a bodily “just right” sensation. Both can coexist.
- **Body-focused repetitive behaviors:** picking/pulling are more goal-directed and complex.
- **Functional tic presentations:** prolonged attacks lasting roughly 15 minutes to hours warrant evaluation for functional symptoms; no single feature substitutes for an assessment (pp. 203–204).

## Comorbidity
ADHD, disruptive behavior and OCD are especially common; ASD, SLD and other movement disorders may coexist. Childhood separation anxiety and later mood/anxiety/substance disorders also occur (pp. 201, 204).

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Describe motor and vocal phenomena separately, including observed versus reported signs, onset age, first-ever tic date and changes/tic-free periods.
- Obtain lifetime higher-hierarchy diagnoses; do not downgrade an established Tourette's history when vocal tics temporarily disappear.
- Record premonitory urge, suppressibility, triggers and movement pattern when assessable; do not present these supportive features as mandatory criteria.
- Assess medication/substance and neurological causes, functional movement alternatives, distress/functional effect and coexisting ADHD/OCD; document individual suicide assessment when indicated rather than importing group statistics.
