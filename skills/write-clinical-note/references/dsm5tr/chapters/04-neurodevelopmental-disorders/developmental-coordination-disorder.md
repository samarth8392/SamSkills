# Developmental Coordination Disorder

> **Spark note:** Motor acquisition and execution lag substantially behind age and learning opportunity and persistently interfere with daily life. Handwriting impairment here concerns motor execution; language formulation/spelling deficits point toward specific learning disorder (pp. 189–190).

## Criteria at a glance
- **A:** Coordinated skill acquisition/execution is substantially below age and opportunity expectations, evident in clumsiness or slow/inaccurate movement.
- **B:** Persistent, significant interference with age-appropriate daily activities, school productivity, work preparation/work, recreation or play.
- **C:** Early developmental onset.
- **D:** Not better explained by intellectual disability or vision impairment and not caused by a neurological movement condition (e.g., cerebral palsy, muscular dystrophy or degeneration) (pp. 189–190).

## Specifiers & severity
No formal subtypes or severity specifiers. Fine motor, gross motor or both areas may predominate (p. 191).

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Synthesize developmental/medical history, physical examination, school/work reports and individual culturally appropriate motor assessment.
- Milestones may have occurred on time; later slowness dressing, using utensils, handwriting, typing or driving can still reveal the disorder.
- Usually avoid diagnosis before age 5 because skill acquisition and measurement are unstable and alternate causes may emerge.
- Include visual and neurological examination. With IDD, deficits must exceed mental-age expectations; no fixed IQ discrepancy is required (pp. 190–191).

## Associated features
Overflow or mirror movements may represent developmental soft signs; their diagnostic significance remains uncertain (p. 191).

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- Approximately 5%–8% of children ages 5–11 across cited countries; males more often affected (ratios 2:1–7:1).
- Coordination problems persist into adolescence in an estimated 50%–70%; complex new adult tasks can expose residual difficulty.
- Prematurity, low birthweight and prenatal alcohol exposure are associated risks. Proposed neural/genetic mechanisms remain uncertain. Coexisting ADHD increases impairment (pp. 191–192).

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
Motor norms, caregiving expectations and opportunity for practice differ across settings. Assess culturally relevant daily tasks and environmental restriction, including poverty; do not infer intrinsic motor impairment from lack of opportunity (p. 192). Male predominance is described above.

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed.

## Functional consequences
Reduced sport/play participation, low self-worth, poorer academic attainment, reduced fitness, obesity and lower quality of life may occur (p. 192).

## Differential diagnosis
- **Medical/neurological or visual disorder:** explanatory neurological/visual findings supersede DCD.
- **IDD:** add DCD only if motor impairment exceeds the global developmental level.
- **ADHD:** distraction/impulsivity may create apparent clumsiness; observe across contexts for an independent skill deficit.
- **ASD:** reluctance to engage may lower performance without proving motor incompetence; both diagnoses may apply.
- **Joint hypermobility:** examination and pain history may explain similar difficulty (pp. 192–193).

## Comorbidity
ADHD is the most frequent coexisting condition (about 50% co-occurrence in the text); communication disorders, SLD, ASD, emotional/behavioral problems and hypermobility also occur (p. 193).

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Describe specific motor errors/slowness and the age/opportunity comparison; record repeated daily-life interference rather than clumsiness alone.
- Establish early onset through collateral history; summarize individual motor testing and school/work observations.
- Document visual/neurological evaluation and relevant intellectual, ADHD, ASD or hypermobility alternatives; distinguish attention-related errors from motor capacity.
