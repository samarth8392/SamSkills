# Autism Spectrum Disorder

> **Spark note:** Persistent deficits in reciprocal social communication (**all 3** A-items) plus restricted, repetitive behaviours/interests/sensory differences (**≥2 of 4** B-items), present from early development — **currently or by history** — and causing current impairment, not better explained by IDD/GDD. The big misses: adults and women/girls who **mask**, and assuming absence of current RRBs rules ASD out (past RRBs count). Severity is rated **separately** for social communication and RRBs and is not a service-eligibility tool (pp. 154–161).

## Criteria at a glance (pp. 154–155)
- **A.** Persistent deficits in social communication/interaction across multiple contexts, **all 3**, current or by history:
  1. Social-emotional reciprocity (odd approach, failed back-and-forth conversation → reduced sharing of interests/emotions → failure to initiate/respond).
  2. Nonverbal communicative behaviours (poorly integrated verbal/nonverbal → abnormal eye contact/body language, poor gesture use/understanding → total lack of facial expression/nonverbal communication).
  3. Developing, maintaining, understanding relationships (difficulty adjusting behaviour to context → difficulty with imaginative play or friendships → no interest in peers).
- **B.** Restricted, repetitive patterns, **≥2 of 4**, current or by history:
  1. Stereotyped/repetitive motor movements, object use or speech (simple stereotypies, lining up/flipping objects, echolalia, idiosyncratic phrases).
  2. Insistence on sameness, inflexible routines, ritualized verbal/nonverbal behaviour (distress at small changes, transition difficulty, rigid thinking, greeting rituals, same route/food).
  3. Highly restricted, fixated interests abnormal in intensity or focus.
  4. Sensory hyper-/hyporeactivity or unusual sensory interest (indifference to pain/temperature, aversion to sounds/textures, excessive smelling/touching, visual fascination with lights/movement).
- **C.** Present in early developmental period (may not fully manifest until demands exceed capacity, or may be masked by learned strategies later).
- **D.** Clinically significant impairment in **current** functioning.
- **E.** Not better explained by IDD or GDD. For comorbid ASD + IDD, social communication must be **below general developmental level**.
- **Note:** well-established DSM-IV autistic disorder, Asperger's or PDD-NOS → give ASD. Marked social-communication deficits without full ASD → evaluate for SPCD.

## Specifiers & severity (pp. 156–158)
**Severity (Table 2) — rate each domain separately:**

| Level | Social communication | Restricted, repetitive behaviours |
|---|---|---|
| **Level 3 – requiring very substantial support** | Severe verbal/nonverbal deficits; very limited initiation; minimal response to overtures (e.g., few intelligible words, unusual approaches only to meet needs) | Inflexibility, extreme difficulty with change, or RRBs markedly interfere in all spheres; great distress changing focus/action |
| **Level 2 – requiring substantial support** | Marked deficits; impairment apparent even with supports; limited initiation; reduced/abnormal responses (e.g., simple sentences, interaction limited to narrow interests, markedly odd nonverbal) | RRBs frequent enough to be obvious to a casual observer; interfere across contexts; distress/difficulty changing focus |
| **Level 1 – requiring support** | Without supports, noticeable impairment; difficulty initiating; atypical/unsuccessful responses; may seem less interested (e.g., full sentences but conversation fails; odd, unsuccessful friendship attempts) | Inflexibility significantly interferes in ≥1 context; trouble switching activities; organization/planning problems hamper independence |

- Current symptoms may fall **below Level 1**; severity varies by context and over time.
- Severity categories should **not** decide eligibility for services; those with better skills may have different or greater psychosocial challenges.

**Other specifiers:**
- **With/without accompanying intellectual impairment** — need separate verbal and nonverbal estimates (e.g., untimed nonverbal tests) because profiles are uneven.
- **With/without accompanying language impairment** — describe current verbal level: no intelligible speech (nonverbal), single words, phrase speech; or full sentences/fluent speech. Rate receptive and expressive separately (receptive may lag).
- **Associated with a known genetic or other medical condition or environmental factor** — e.g., Rett, fragile X, Down syndrome, epilepsy, fetal valproate, fetal alcohol, fetal rubella; also a novel CNV or Crohn's disease that worsens behaviour. **Does not imply causation** — use when clinically relevant to care. Code the condition additionally.
- **Associated with a neurodevelopmental, mental, or behavioral problem** — e.g., irritability, sleep problems, self-injury, developmental regression that shape formulation or treatment. Full disorders (ADHD, DCD, disruptive, anxiety, depressive, bipolar, tics, feeding, elimination, sleep) are diagnosed separately as well.
- **With catatonia** — use criteria for catatonia associated with another mental disorder; also code **F06.1** catatonia associated with ASD. In ASD, worsening stereotypy and self-injury may be part of the catatonic picture along with posturing, negativism, mutism, stupor.

**Recording order (p. 157):** ASD → support level for each domain (e.g., "requiring very substantial support for social communication deficits and requiring substantial support for restricted, repetitive behaviors") → with/without intellectual impairment → with/without language impairment (+ current verbal level, e.g., "—phrase speech") → "associated with [condition/factor/problem]" (e.g., "ASD associated with tuberous sclerosis complex") → catatonia recorded separately.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls (pp. 158–161)
- Term "spectrum" reflects variation by severity, developmental level, age and possibly gender.
- Encompasses former early infantile autism, childhood autism, Kanner's, high-functioning, atypical autism, PDD-NOS, childhood disintegrative disorder, Asperger's.
- Most valid with **multiple sources**: clinician observation, caregiver history, self-report.
- Language ranges from none to delays, poor comprehension, echolalia, stilted/overly literal. Even with intact vocabulary/grammar, **reciprocal use** is impaired.
- **Reciprocity:** young children — little initiation, no sharing of emotion, reduced imitation; language used to request/label rather than comment or converse. Older verbal individuals — trouble processing complex social cues (when to join, what not to say).
- **Masking/compensation:** effortful, anxious "calculating" of what is intuitive for others; struggle in novel situations; may lower ascertainment, **especially in adult women**. Ask about the **costs** of socializing (exhaustion, concentration drain, self-esteem). Longer assessments and naturalistic observation help.
- **Nonverbal:** early sign = impaired **joint attention** (no pointing, showing, bringing objects; not following point/gaze). Small gesture repertoire; odd, wooden or exaggerated body language; deficits may show as poor **integration** of eye contact, gesture, posture, prosody, facial expression, or difficulty sustaining these under stress.
- **Relationships:** judged against age, gender, culture; rejection, passivity, or intrusive approaches; lack of shared pretend play, later insistence on fixed rules; trouble with situational norms, irony, white lies; preference for solitude or much older/younger people; one-sided or interest-based friendships.
- **RRB examples:** hand flapping, finger flicking, spinning coins, lining up toys, echolalia, pronoun reversal ("you" for self), stereotyped phrases/prosody; distress at route change, rule rigidity, repetitive questioning, pacing a perimeter; toddler attached to a pan or string, child preoccupied with vacuum cleaners, adult writing timetables for hours.
- **Sensory:** extreme reactions to sounds/textures, smelling/touching, fascination with lights/spinning, indifference to pain/heat/cold. **Food** rituals/restrictions (taste, smell, texture, appearance) are common and may be the presenting feature.
- Verbal individuals may suppress repetitive behaviours in public; rocking/finger flicking may be self-soothing.
- Special interests can drive education/employment.
- Criteria met if RRBs were clearly present in childhood or at some time in the past, even if absent now.
- **Criterion E:** social-communication deficits exceed developmental level.
- Standardized instruments improve reliability but there is **no universal cutoff**; diagnosis remains clinical.

## Associated features (p. 161)
- Frequent IDD and/or language impairment (comprehension behind production); uneven ability profiles; large gap between IQ and adaptive skills.
- Theory-of-mind deficits common but not universal; executive dysfunction and weak central coherence (detail focus) common but nonspecific.
- Motor: odd gait, clumsiness, toe-walking.
- Self-injury (head banging, wrist biting); disruptive/challenging behaviours more common than in other disorders including IDD.
- Catatonic-like slowing/freezing; full catatonia (mutism, posturing, grimacing, waxy flexibility) possible — **risk greatest in adolescence**.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- **Prevalence (pp. 161–162):** US 1–2% (similar child/adult). Lower reported in US African American (1.1%) and Latinx (0.8%) vs White (1.3%) children even after SES adjustment — may reflect misdiagnosis/delayed/underdiagnosis. Non-US ~1% (median global 0.62%). Male:female ~**3:1** in well-ascertained samples (underrecognition in women/girls).
- **Onset (p. 162):** record age and pattern of onset. Recognized at **12–24 months** typically (earlier if severe, later if subtle). Loss of skills usually between 12–24 months, gradual or rapid.
- Most cases show **declines** in social/communication behaviours in the first 2 years — rare in other NDDs and a useful indicator.
- **Regression after ≥2 years of normal development** (former childhood disintegrative disorder) is rare → extensive medical workup (continuous spike-waves in slow-wave sleep, Landau-Kleffner); losses often extend to self-care, toileting, motor skills.
- Early signs: delayed language with lack of social interest, pulling someone by the hand without looking, carrying toys without playing, knowing the alphabet but not responding to name; deafness often suspected and ruled out. Repetitive behaviours/absent play clearer in year 2. Preschool RRBs judged by type, frequency, intensity (e.g., lining up objects for hours daily with distress if moved).
- **Not degenerative**; learning and compensation continue. Symptoms most marked early childhood/early school years; gains later (e.g., more social interest). A small proportion deteriorate in adolescence. More adults now employed as diagnosis extends to those with stronger language/intellect; **vocational rehabilitation** improves competitive employment.
- Even lower-impairment adults remain socially naive and vulnerable, struggle with practical organization, prone to anxiety and depression; masking causes stress.
- Old age little studied; more co-occurring medical conditions.
- **Adult first diagnosis** (often prompted by a child's diagnosis or relationship/work breakdown): developmental history may be hard to obtain — weigh self-report. A compelling report of sustained reciprocal friendships and good nonverbal skills through childhood lowers likelihood; **absent or ambiguous history alone does not rule out** ASD.
- **Prognostic factors (p. 163):** IDD and language impairment (functional language by **age 5** = good sign) and additional mental health problems; epilepsy → greater ID, lower verbal ability.
- **Environmental:** advanced parental age, extreme prematurity, in utero valproate or other teratogens.
- **Genetic (p. 164):** heritability 37% to >90% (twin); five-country cohort ~**80%**. Up to **15%** linked to a known mutation (de novo CNVs/mutations), not fully penetrant; most cases polygenic. Limited inclusion of communities of color in genetic research.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues (p. 164)
- People with ASD are markedly impaired against **their own cultural norms**; culture shapes perception of behaviours and parenting expectations.
- Delayed diagnosis in socially oppressed ethnic and racialized children; **African American children more often misdiagnosed** with adjustment or conduct disorder.
- Diagnosed 3–4× more in males; females diagnosed later. Clinic females more often have IDD and epilepsy → girls without ID/language delay may be missed.
- Females may show better reciprocal conversation, sharing of interests, integration of verbal/nonverbal, situational adaptation, despite similar social understanding difficulties; may **mask** by copying socially successful women's dress, voice, manner. RRBs may be less evident; interests may be more social (singer, actor) or normative (horses) but unusual in intensity.
- Higher rates of **gender variance**, more so in females.

## Suicide risk (p. 165)
- Greater risk of **suicide death** vs people without ASD.
- Children with impaired social communication: more self-harm with suicidal intent, suicidal thoughts and plans by age 16.
- Adolescents/young adults: more suicide attempts even after adjusting for demographics and psychiatric comorbidity.

## Functional consequences (p. 165)
- Young children: learning hampered, especially social/peer-based learning; routines and sensory sensitivities disrupt eating, sleep, haircuts, dental care.
- **Adaptive skills typically below measured IQ.** Planning/organization/change difficulties impair academics even with above-average IQ.
- Adults: difficulty establishing independence; poor psychosocial outcomes (independent living, employment) even without IDD. Old age: isolation, reduced help-seeking.
- Premature mortality higher with IDD, epilepsy, mental and chronic medical conditions; more deaths from injury, poisoning, suicide. **Drowning is the leading cause of accidental death in children with ASD.**

## Differential diagnosis (pp. 165–167)
- **ADHD:** attention abnormalities and hyperactivity common in ASD; ADHD can show interrupting, loudness, ignoring personal space. Distinguish by developmental course and **absence of RRBs/unusual interests** in ADHD. Add ADHD when attention/activity problems exceed mental-age expectations — one of the most common ASD comorbidities.
- **IDD without ASD:** hard in very young children and in nonverbal individuals with repetitive behaviours. ASD fits when social communication is significantly impaired **relative to nonverbal skills** (fine motor, nonverbal problem solving); IDD alone when no discrepancy.
- **Language disorders / SPCD:** language disorder lacks abnormal nonverbal communication and RRBs. Social deficits without RRBs → SPCD. ASD supersedes SPCD — ask carefully about past and current RRBs.
- **Selective mutism:** early development normal; communicates appropriately in some settings; reciprocity intact even when mute; no RRBs.
- **Stereotypic movement disorder:** not added when stereotypies are explained by ASD; both given if stereotypies cause **self-injury** and become a treatment focus.
- **Rett syndrome:** social disruption during regressive phase (ages 1–4) can meet ASD criteria; most improve afterwards → diagnose ASD only if all criteria met.
- **Anxiety symptoms:** withdrawal and repetitive behaviour can be anxiety; most common comorbid anxiety disorders in ASD — specific phobia (up to **30%**), social anxiety and agoraphobia (up to **17%**).
- **OCD:** OCD compulsions respond to intrusive thoughts (contamination, order, sexual, religious) to relieve anxiety; ASD repetition is more stereotyped (flapping, finger shaking, routines, lining up) and may be **pleasurable/reinforcing**.
- **Schizophrenia:** childhood-onset usually follows normal/near-normal development; prodrome can mimic; hallucinations and delusions are not ASD features — but beware **concrete** answers ("Do you hear voices when no one is there?" "Yes — on the radio"). Both can co-occur.
- **Personality disorders:** in verbal adults without ID, behaviours may look narcissistic, schizotypal or schizoid; schizotypal overlaps most (odd preoccupations/perceptions, odd thinking/speech, constricted affect, social anxiety, no close friends, eccentricity). **Early developmental course** (no imaginative play, RRBs, sensory sensitivities) is the best discriminator.

## Comorbidity (pp. 167–168)
- IDD and language disorder frequent; SLD (literacy/numeracy) and DCD common.
- ~**70%** have ≥1 comorbid mental disorder; ~**40%** have ≥2. Anxiety, depression, ADHD especially common.
- **ARFID** fairly frequent presenting feature; narrow food preferences persist.
- In nonverbal/language-impaired people, **changes in sleep or eating and increased challenging behaviour** should prompt evaluation for anxiety, depression, and pain from undiagnosed medical/dental problems.
- Medical: **epilepsy, constipation**.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **A1:** e.g., "conversation one-sided, monologues on preferred topic, does not ask reciprocal questions"; "does not share enjoyment or show objects".
- **A2:** "eye contact fleeting and not coordinated with speech"; "limited gesture, flat or poorly modulated facial expression"; "prosody monotone/sing-song".
- **A3:** "no reciprocal friendships per parent; prefers adults"; "difficulty adjusting behaviour to setting (e.g., overly casual with principal)".
- **B1–B4:** document ≥2 with examples and whether current or historical (e.g., "lined up cars for hours age 3, distressed if moved"; "meltdowns with schedule changes"; "intense interest in train timetables"; "covers ears to hand dryers; eats only beige foods").
- **C:** age at first concern, milestone history, any regression (age, domains lost); informant name and relationship. For adults, note limited history and self-report weight.
- **D:** current impairment (school, work, independent living), including costs of masking.
- **E / IDD:** nonverbal vs social-communication level; IQ and adaptive scores.
- **Specifiers:** support level per domain; intellectual and language status with current verbal level; associated conditions (genetic testing, epilepsy, prenatal exposures) and problems (irritability, sleep, self-injury); catatonia signs (F06.1).
- **Rule-outs/comorbidity:** hearing test; SPCD (RRB history); ADHD; anxiety/OCD; psychosis (with care for literal interpretation); personality disorder; medical causes of behaviour change (pain, constipation, dental, seizures).
- **Safety:** suicide screening (elevated risk), self-injury, **water safety/elopement** in children, vulnerability to exploitation.
- MSE phrases: "reduced reciprocal eye contact; restricted range of affect not matching content"; "pedantic, overly formal speech; literal interpretation of idioms"; "perseverates on preferred topic despite redirection"; "hand flapping when excited"; "denies hallucinations; clarified concrete responses".
