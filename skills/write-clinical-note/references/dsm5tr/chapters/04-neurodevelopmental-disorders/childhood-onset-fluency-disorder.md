# Childhood-Onset Fluency Disorder (Stuttering)

> **Spark note:** Developmentally inappropriate, persistent disruption of speech fluency and timing (repetitions, prolongations, blocks, broken words, tense words, avoidance substitutions) with onset in early childhood, causing speaking anxiety or functional limits. It worsens under communicative pressure and often vanishes during singing, oral reading, or talking to pets. Don't confuse normal preschool whole-word/phrase repetitions with stuttering, and don't diagnose this when onset is in adolescence/adulthood (F98.5) (pp. 149–151).

## Criteria at a glance (p. 149)
- **A.** Disturbed fluency and time patterning of speech, inappropriate for age and language skills, persistent, with frequent and marked occurrences of **≥1** of:
  1. Sound and syllable repetitions.
  2. Sound prolongations (consonants and vowels).
  3. Broken words (pauses within a word).
  4. Audible or silent blocking (filled/unfilled pauses).
  5. Circumlocutions (substituting words to avoid problem words).
  6. Words produced with excess physical tension.
  7. Monosyllabic whole-word repetitions ("I-I-I-I see him").
- **B.** Causes **anxiety about speaking** or limits communication, social participation, academic or occupational performance.
- **C.** Onset in early developmental period. Later onset → **F98.5 adult-onset fluency disorder**.
- **D.** Not due to speech-motor or sensory deficit, neurological insult (stroke, tumor, trauma) or other medical condition; not better explained by another mental disorder.

## Specifiers & severity
None.

## Coding
| Code | Use |
|---|---|

| F98.5 | Adult-onset fluency disorder (onset after early developmental period) |

The source contains a terminology tension: Criterion C supplies F98.5 for adult-onset fluency disorder (p. 149), while the differential says adult-onset dysfluencies are not a DSM-5 diagnosis (p. 151). F98.5 is retained here as the source coding distinction, not a newly added neurodevelopmental disorder.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls (p. 149)
- Varies by situation; worse under pressure to communicate (school report, job interview).
- Often **absent** during oral reading, singing, or talking to inanimate objects or pets.
- May interfere with academic/occupational achievement and social communication.

## Associated features (p. 150)
- Fearful anticipation; avoidance via linguistic tricks (changing rate, avoiding words/sounds) or avoiding situations (phone calls, public speaking).
- Stress and anxiety are both features and **exacerbators**.
- Accompanying motor movements: eye blinks, tics, lip/face tremor, head jerks, breathing movements, fist clenching.
- Range of language abilities; link with language unclear.
- Structural and functional neurological differences; multifactorial genetic and neurophysiological causes. Males > females.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- **Prevalence:** not quantified in text; males more affected.
- **Onset (p. 150):** by age 6 in **80–90%**; range 2–7 y; insidious or sudden. Begins with repetitions of initial consonants, first words of phrases, or long words; child may be unaware. Progresses to more frequent dysfluencies on meaningful words; awareness brings avoidance, short simple utterances, emotional reactions.
- **Recovery:** **65–85%** recover; **severity at age 8** predicts recovery vs persistence into adolescence and beyond.
- **Genetic:** first-degree relatives have **>3×** population risk; mutations in 4 genes identified.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- Bilingualism: distinguish dysfluency of learning a new language from true stuttering, which shows in **both** languages (p. 151).
- Males > females.

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed.

## Functional consequences (p. 150)
- Anxiety → social impairment.
- Negative communication attitudes from preschool, increasing with age.

## Differential diagnosis (p. 151)
- **Sensory deficits / speech-motor deficit:** diagnose only if dysfluency exceeds what they explain.
- **Normal dysfluencies:** whole-word or phrase repetitions ("I want, I want ice cream"), incomplete phrases, interjections, unfilled pauses, parenthetical remarks. If they increase in frequency/complexity with age, consider the disorder.
- **SLD with reading impairment:** dysfluent oral reading may be misread as reading disorder; timed reading-rate scores may underestimate true reading ability.
- **Bilingualism:** new-language dysfluency vs disorder present in both languages.
- **Medication side effects:** temporal link to drug exposure.
- **Adult-onset dysfluencies:** onset during/after adolescence; linked to neurological insults, medical conditions, mental disorders; not a DSM-5 neurodevelopmental diagnosis (coded F98.5).
- **Tourette's disorder:** vocal tics/repetitive vocalizations differ in nature and timing from stuttering sounds.

## Comorbidity (pp. 151–152)
ADHD, ASD, IDD, language disorder, SLD, seizure disorders, **social anxiety disorder**, speech sound disorder, other developmental disorders.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **A:** list observed dysfluency types with examples and frequency (e.g., "silent blocks on initial plosives, ~8 per minute in conversation; visible jaw tension; substitutes words to avoid /k/").
- Note situational variability (fluent when reading aloud/singing; worse on phone).
- **B:** speaking anxiety, avoidance (won't order food, avoids class participation), occupational impact.
- **C:** age at onset (caregiver report); if onset adolescent/adult, code F98.5 instead.
- **D rule-outs:** hearing, neurological history (stroke, TBI, tumour), medication timeline, other mental disorder.
- Screen for social anxiety disorder; distinguish from tics.
- MSE phrases: "speech marked by part-word repetitions and prolongations, with secondary eye blinking"; "rate and prosody otherwise normal; language content intact"; "reports anticipatory anxiety before speaking".
