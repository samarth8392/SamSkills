# Major Depressive Disorder

> **Spark note:** ≥1 major depressive episode (≥2 wk of depressed mood or anhedonia plus enough
> associated symptoms to total ≥5/9, with distress/impairment) and **never** a manic or hypomanic
> episode. It is usually recurrent. Two common errors: (1) calling it MDD without asking about past
> hypomania/mania, which many bipolar cases start as depression; (2) assuming a loss "explains" the
> symptoms. Bereavement does not exclude MDD (there is no bereavement exclusion). Judge whether a
> full MDE is present *in addition to* normal grief (p. 310, footnote p. 348).

## Criteria at a glance
**Criteria A–C define a major depressive episode (MDE); D–E make it MDD** (pp. 309–310).

- **A.** ≥5 of 9 symptoms, **same 2-week period**, a **change from prior functioning**; **≥1 must be (1) depressed mood or (2) anhedonia**. Do not count symptoms clearly due to another medical condition.
  1. **Depressed mood** most of the day, nearly every day, by self-report (sad, empty, hopeless) or by others' observation (e.g., tearful). *Kids/teens: may be irritable mood.*
  2. **Markedly reduced interest/pleasure** in all or almost all activities, most of the day, nearly every day (self-report or observed).
  3. **Significant weight change** not from dieting (e.g., >5% body weight in 1 month), **or** appetite up/down nearly every day. *Kids: failure to make expected weight gain.*
  4. **Insomnia or hypersomnia** nearly every day.
  5. **Psychomotor agitation or retardation** nearly every day. Must be **observable by others**, not just a subjective feeling.
  6. **Fatigue/loss of energy** nearly every day.
  7. **Worthlessness or excessive/inappropriate guilt** (may be delusional) nearly every day. Self-reproach about being sick does not count.
  8. **Reduced ability to think/concentrate, or indecisiveness**, nearly every day (subjective or observed).
  9. **Recurrent thoughts of death** (not just fear of dying), recurrent SI without plan, a suicide attempt, or a specific plan.
- **B.** Clinically significant distress or impairment (social, occupational, other).
- **C.** Not due to the physiological effects of a substance or another medical condition.
- **Loss note (after C):** Reactions to major loss (bereavement, financial ruin, disaster, serious illness/disability) can produce intense sadness, rumination, insomnia, poor appetite and weight loss that look like an MDE. Even if the reaction seems understandable, **also consider whether a true MDE is present**. This is a clinical judgment based on the person's history and on cultural norms for expressing distress after loss. See *Bereavement vs. MDE* below.
- **D.** ≥1 MDE not better explained by schizoaffective disorder and not superimposed on schizophrenia, schizophreniform, delusional, or other specified/unspecified psychotic disorder.
- **E.** **Never** a manic or hypomanic episode. *Exception:* this does not apply if every manic-/hypomanic-like episode was substance-induced or due to another medical condition.

**Symptom-counting rules** (p. 311–312):
- A symptom counts only if it is **new or clearly worse** than before the episode.
- Most symptoms must be present **nearly every day for ≥2 consecutive weeks**. Exceptions: thoughts of death/SI need only be **recurrent**, and an attempt or specific plan needs to occur only **once**.
- Count symptoms that overlap with a medical illness (fatigue in cancer, weight loss in diabetes, sleep changes in pregnancy/postpartum) **unless they are clearly and fully due to the medical condition**.

## Specifiers & severity
Full definitions are in [specifiers.md](specifiers.md).
- **Episode course:** single vs **recurrent**. Recurrent requires **≥2 consecutive months** between episodes during which full MDE criteria are not met.
- **Severity (only if full MDE criteria are currently met):** mild / moderate / severe, based on symptom count, intensity and functional disability.
  - *Mild:* few or no symptoms beyond the minimum; distressing but manageable; minor impairment.
  - *Moderate:* between mild and severe.
  - *Severe:* well above the minimum count; seriously distressing and unmanageable; marked interference.
- **With psychotic features:** code it **regardless of severity**. Then specify mood-congruent or mood-incongruent.
- **Remission (only if full MDE criteria are NOT currently met):**
  - *Partial:* some symptoms of the last MDE remain but below full criteria, **or** symptom-free for <2 months after the episode.
  - *Full:* no significant signs/symptoms for the past 2 months.
  - Course section definition of remission: ≥2 months with no symptoms, or only 1–2 mild symptoms.
- **Uncoded specifiers** (apply to the current episode, or the most recent one if in remission): with anxious distress (graded mild to severe), with mixed features, with melancholic features, with atypical features, with mood-congruent/incongruent psychotic features, with catatonia (**add F06.1**), with peripartum onset, with seasonal pattern (**recurrent only**; describes the pattern of episodes).

Psychotic features: "F32.3 Major depressive disorder, single episode, with psychotic features, with mood-incongruent psychotic features."

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- **Presenting complaints are often insomnia or fatigue**, not sadness. If you don't ask about the other symptoms, MDD gets missed (underdiagnosis).
- Fatigue and sleep disturbance are very common. **Psychomotor change is less common but signals greater severity**, as does delusional or near-delusional guilt.
- **Depressed mood may be denied at first.** It can be elicited in interview (e.g., the person looks about to cry) or inferred from face and demeanor when they say they feel "blah", numb or anxious. Some people present with **somatic complaints** (aches, pains) instead of sadness.
- **Irritability is common** (persistent anger, angry outbursts, blaming others, frustration over small things). In youth, a cranky mood can replace sadness. Distinguish this from irritability that appears only when frustrated.
- **Anhedonia is nearly always present to some degree.** Look for withdrawal and dropped hobbies reported by family, and for lower libido.
- **Appetite:** can go down (forcing oneself to eat) or up (carbohydrate/sweet cravings).
- **Sleep:** insomnia is usually **middle** (waking during the night) or **terminal** (early waking); initial insomnia also occurs. Hypersomnia means long nights or daytime sleep. Sleep is sometimes the reason the person seeks care.
- **Psychomotor:** agitation (can't sit still, pacing, hand-wringing, rubbing skin or clothes) or retardation (slow speech, thought and movement; long response latency; low volume, inflection, amount or variety of speech; mutism). People with one type often have a history of the other.
- **Fatigue** drives much of the impairment, both in the acute episode and in incomplete remission. Simple tasks (washing, dressing) can take twice as long.
- **Guilt/worthlessness:** unrealistic negative self-evaluation, ruminating over minor past failings, reading neutral events as proof of personal defects. Blaming oneself for being sick is very common and **does not count unless delusional**.
- **Cognition:** distractibility and memory complaints. Watch for a sudden drop in grades in children. In older adults it can mimic dementia ("pseudodementia") and often clears with treatment, but an MDE can also be the **first sign of an irreversible dementia**.
- **SI spectrum:** ranges from a passive wish not to wake up, through "others would be better off," to transient recurrent thoughts, to a specific plan. Preparations (settling affairs, getting means, choosing time and place) mean higher risk. Motives include giving up, ending unbearable pain, seeing no future pleasure, and not wanting to be a burden. **Resolution of suicidal thinking is a better marker of lower risk than simply denying plans.**
- **Impairment** ranges from normal-looking functioning that takes markedly more effort, to inability to do basic self-care or hygiene.
- **Interview tips:** self-report can be distorted by poor concentration, poor memory or minimizing. **Collateral informants** help establish past episodes and past mania/hypomania. With gradual onset, **ask about the worst part of the current episode**.
- **Medically ill patients:** assess the **nonvegetative symptoms** (dysphoria, anhedonia, guilt/worthlessness, concentration/indecision, SI) with particular care. Using only those symptoms identifies nearly the same people as the full criteria.

### Bereavement vs. major depressive episode (p. 310 note; p. 320; footnote p. 348)
DSM-5-TR has **no bereavement exclusion**. Grief and an MDE can co-occur, and a loss should not be used to dismiss a full MDE. Bereavement causes great suffering but **does not typically cause** an MDE. When both are present, symptoms and impairment are more severe and the prognosis is worse. Bereavement-related MDEs tend to occur in people with other vulnerabilities to depression (e.g., past MDD). In a vulnerable person, bereavement can trigger a new episode or worsen an existing one.

| Feature | Grief | MDE |
|---|---|---|
| Predominant affect | Emptiness and loss | Persistent depressed mood; inability to anticipate happiness or pleasure |
| Time pattern | Comes in **waves ("pangs")** that lessen over days to weeks | Pervasive and persistent |
| Trigger of dysphoria | Thoughts/reminders of the deceased | Not tied to specific thoughts or preoccupations |
| Positive emotion | Pain can coexist with **positive emotions and humor** | Pervasive unhappiness and misery |
| Thought content | Preoccupied with memories of the deceased | Self-critical, pessimistic rumination |
| Self-esteem | **Generally preserved**. Any self-blame is about perceived failings toward the deceased (didn't visit enough, didn't say "I love you") | Worthlessness and self-loathing common |
| Death thoughts | Focused on the deceased, possibly "joining" them | Focused on ending one's own life (feels worthless, undeserving, unable to bear the pain) |

- The same logic applies to **other major losses** (financial ruin, disaster, disabling illness).
- Always weigh the individual's history and **cultural norms** for expressing grief.
- *Cross-chapter note:* grief that is persistent and impairing beyond expected norms is covered by **Prolonged Grief Disorder** (Trauma- and Stressor-Related Disorders chapter, new in DSM-5-TR). This chapter's text does not discuss it.

## Associated features
- **High mortality**, much of it from suicide but not all (e.g., depressed nursing-home admissions have a markedly higher 1st-year death rate).
- Tearfulness, irritability, brooding, obsessive rumination, anxiety, phobias, excessive health worry, pain complaints (headache, joint, abdominal). In children: separation anxiety.
- **No laboratory test is diagnostic.** HPA-axis hyperactivity is linked to melancholia, psychotic features and later suicide risk. Neurotrophic-factor and pro-inflammatory cytokine variants have been implicated. MRI shows abnormalities in emotion-processing, reward and emotion-regulation circuits.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
**Prevalence** (p. 314–315)
- US 12-month prevalence is **~7%**. Ages 18–29 have **3×** the prevalence of those ≥60.
- **Females ~2×** males. The gap peaks in adolescence, then stabilizes, and is largest from menarche to menopause.
- Rates vary 8–9-fold across world regions. US prevalence rose from 2005 to 2015, most steeply in youth. The rise was significant in non-Hispanic Whites, with no significant change in non-Hispanic Blacks or Hispanics.

**Development & course**
- Onset at any age; likelihood rises sharply at **puberty**; US incidence peaks in the **20s**.
- Course varies: some never remit, others have long well intervals. Social-structural adversity (poverty, racism, marginalization) shapes the course.
- **Ask about the last period of ≥2 months that was completely free of depressive symptoms.** Chronic presentations raise the likelihood of personality, anxiety and substance use disorders and lower the chance of full recovery. If symptoms are present more days than not, consider adding PDD.
- **Recovery begins within 3 months in 40%** and **within 1 year in 80%**. Recent onset strongly predicts near-term recovery. Lower recovery is linked to psychotic features, prominent anxiety, personality disorders and severity.
- **Recurrence risk** falls the longer remission lasts. It is higher after a severe episode, in younger people and after multiple episodes. **Residual mild symptoms during remission strongly predict recurrence.**
- **Switch to bipolar:** more likely with adolescent onset, psychotic features, family history of bipolar disorder, or the *with mixed features* specifier. MDD with psychotic features can also convert to **schizophrenia** (far more often than the reverse).
- Age does not clearly change course or treatment response. Younger people have more hypersomnia and hyperphagia; older people more melancholic and psychomotor symptoms. **Early onset** is more familial and more linked to personality disturbance. Time to recovery does not change across episodes.

**Risk & prognostic factors**
- *Temperamental:* **negative affectivity (neuroticism)** increases vulnerability to depression after stress.
- *Environmental:* **adverse childhood experiences** (especially multiple and varied types), low income, limited education, racism and discrimination. Stressful events often precipitate episodes, but they **do not guide prognosis or treatment choice**. Women carry a disproportionate share of ACEs and interpersonal trauma.
- *Genetic:* first-degree relatives have **2–4×** the risk (higher for early-onset and recurrent cases). **Heritability ~40%**, much of it shared with neuroticism.
- Women have reproductive-stage risks: premenstrual, postpartum, perimenopause.
- *Course modifiers:* nearly all major nonmood disorders (anxiety, SUD, trauma/stressor, eating, OCD-related) raise the risk of depression, and depression on top of them is more refractory. SUD, anxiety and **borderline PD** are common and can be masked by the depression. Treating the underlying disorder may be needed for sustained improvement. Chronic medical illness (diabetes, morbid obesity, CVD) raises risk, and those episodes become chronic more often.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- An MDD-like syndrome is found across cultures. Common symptoms outside the criteria include **social isolation/loneliness, anger, crying and diffuse pain**. Somatic complaints vary by culture, so explore what they mean locally.
- Depression may be under-detected in ethnic and racialized groups facing discrimination, which can lead to **overdiagnosis of schizophrenia spectrum disorders**.
- Higher income inequality is linked to higher prevalence. In the US, chronicity is higher in **African American and Caribbean Black** individuals than in non-Latinx Whites, possibly due to racism, adversity and poorer access to care.
- **Sex/gender:** no clear differences in treatment response or functional consequences. **Women** have more appetite/sleep disturbance, atypical features (hyperphagia, hypersomnia, leaden paralysis), interpersonal sensitivity and GI symptoms. **Men** report more maladaptive coping: alcohol/drug misuse, risk taking and poor impulse control.

## Suicide risk
- US age-adjusted suicide rate rose from **10.5 to 14.0 per 100,000** over two decades. Depressive illness carries **~17×** the general-population suicide risk.
- Attempts become less frequent in mid and late life, but **death by suicide does not**. Risk is present at **any point** in an MDE.
- **Strongest single risk factor:** past attempts or threats. However, **most suicide deaths are not preceded by a nonfatal attempt**.
- **Anhedonia** is strongly tied to SI.
- Other risk factors: being single, living alone, social disconnection, early adversity, **access to lethal means (firearms)**, sleep disturbance, cognitive and decision-making deficits, **hopelessness**.
- Women attempt more often and men die by suicide more often, though the gap is smaller among people with depression.
- Comorbid aggressive-impulsive traits, borderline PD, SUD, anxiety, medical illness and functional impairment raise future risk.

## Functional consequences
- Ranges from impairment others don't notice to complete incapacity (no self-care, mutism, catatonia).
- In medical settings, MDD brings more pain and physical illness and greater losses in physical, social and role functioning.
- Women report more relationship impairment.

## Differential diagnosis
- **Manic episode with irritable mood / with mixed features:** count manic symptoms carefully. Threshold is **3** if mood is elevated, **4** if mood is only irritable.
- **Bipolar I / II / other specified bipolar:** any history of mania means bipolar I; hypomania without mania means bipolar II. Periods of subthreshold hypomania can be called either other specified bipolar or MDD, depending on how clinically significant the hypomanic symptoms are.
- **Depressive disorder due to another medical condition:** requires an etiological medical condition (MS, stroke, hypothyroidism). Do not diagnose MDD if every MDE-like episode is the direct pathophysiological result of that condition.
- **Substance/medication-induced depressive disorder:** the substance is etiologically related (e.g., depression only during cocaine withdrawal = cocaine-induced depressive disorder).
- **Persistent depressive disorder:** depressed mood more days than not for ≥2 years. If both are met, **diagnose both**.
- **PMDD:** dysphoria in the final premenstrual week that improves within a few days of menses and is minimal/absent postmenses. MDD episodes are not tied to the cycle.
- **DMDD:** severe recurrent outbursts plus persistent irritability between them. In MDD, irritability is confined to the episodes.
- **MDE superimposed on schizophrenia, delusional, schizophreniform or other psychotic disorder:** usually an associated feature. If full MDE criteria are met, **other specified depressive disorder** can be added.
- **Schizoaffective disorder:** requires delusions/hallucinations for ≥2 weeks **without** a major mood episode. In MDD with psychotic features, psychosis occurs only during episodes.
- **ADHD:** distractibility and low frustration tolerance overlap. Diagnose both if both are met, but don't overdiagnose an MDE in children with ADHD whose mood is irritable rather than sad or anhedonic.
- **Adjustment disorder with depressed mood:** stressor-linked, but full MDE criteria are **not** met.
- **Bereavement:** see the table above.
- **Sadness:** normal. Requires severity (5/9), duration (most of the day, nearly every day, ≥2 wk) and distress/impairment. Subthreshold but impairing presentations can be coded as **other specified depressive disorder**.

## Comorbidity
- Frequently co-occurs with substance-related disorders, panic disorder, GAD, PTSD, OCD, anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa and **borderline PD**.
- Women report more comorbid anxiety, bulimia and somatic symptom disorders; men report more alcohol and substance misuse.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **Map each A symptom explicitly**, giving onset date, frequency ("nearly every day"), duration ≥2 wk and change from baseline. State which **core symptom** (mood or anhedonia) is present.
  - A1: patient reports low/empty/hopeless mood most of the day, or the clinician sees tearfulness or constricted, dysphoric affect. For youth, record observed irritability.
  - A2: name specific dropped activities, ideally confirmed by collateral ("stopped attending choir; no enjoyment from grandchildren's visits").
  - A3: weight and timeframe (kg/% over a month), appetite change, forced eating or cravings.
  - A4: sleep pattern type (initial/middle/terminal insomnia or hypersomnia) with hours.
  - A5: **observed** psychomotor signs in the MSE (latency of response, slowed gait/speech, low prosody, pacing, hand-wringing). Subjective restlessness alone does not count.
  - A6–A8: functional examples (takes twice as long to dress; cannot finish reading a page; can't decide what to order).
  - A7: specify content of guilt and whether it is delusional. Self-blame about being ill doesn't count.
  - A9: passive vs active SI, plan, intent, preparatory behavior, access to means (firearms), past attempts, protective factors, and whether suicidal thinking has *resolved*, not just whether the patient denies a plan.
- **Criterion B:** concrete impairment (missed work days, role failures, self-care) or marked distress, or "functions only with markedly increased effort."
- **Required rule-outs to document:**
  - Lifetime screen for **mania/hypomania** (state who was asked: patient and collateral).
  - Substances/medications (alcohol, stimulant withdrawal, steroids, interferon, isotretinoin, hormonal agents, varenicline, etc.).
  - Medical causes (thyroid, B12, neuro conditions, Cushing's) and relevant labs.
  - Psychotic disorders and schizoaffective disorder.
  - Menstrual-cycle linkage (PMDD).
  - Chronicity: last ≥2-month symptom-free period, to assess for PDD.
- **Episode accounting:** first vs recurrent (≥2 consecutive symptom-free months between episodes), current severity or remission status, psychotic features (congruent/incongruent), and any uncoded specifiers with their evidence (e.g., anxious distress: list which of the 5 symptoms).
- **Loss present:** document the grief vs MDE discriminators, e.g. "dysphoria pervasive, not wave-like or reminder-bound; self-esteem markedly reduced with global worthlessness; no capacity for humor or positive emotion; SI focused on own worthlessness rather than reunion with the deceased." Add a note on cultural norms for grieving and prior depressive history.
- **MSE phrases:** "affect constricted, dysphoric, non-reactive"; "speech slowed, low volume, increased latency"; "psychomotor retardation evident"; "thought content notable for themes of guilt and worthlessness, no delusions elicited"; "endorses passive death wish without plan or intent."
- **Medically ill patients:** emphasize nonvegetative symptoms and state whether somatic symptoms are judged "not fully attributable to [condition]."
