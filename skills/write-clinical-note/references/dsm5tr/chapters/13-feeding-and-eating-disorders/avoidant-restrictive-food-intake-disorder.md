# Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder (ARFID)

> **Spark note:** Food avoidance or restriction **not** driven by weight or shape concerns that causes one or more of: weight loss or faltering growth, nutritional deficiency, dependence on tube feeds or supplements, or marked psychosocial interference. The three classic drivers are **sensory sensitivity**, **fear of aversive consequences** (choking, vomiting) and **low interest in food**. Key pitfall: ARFID and AN are **never co-diagnosed**. Consider "non–fat phobic" AN, and rule out PANS when onset is acute or late.

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** An eating or feeding disturbance (e.g., apparent lack of interest in food; avoidance based on sensory features; concern about aversive consequences) **plus ≥1** of:
  1. Significant weight loss, or failure to achieve expected weight gain / faltering growth in children.
  2. Significant nutritional deficiency.
  3. Dependence on **enteral feeding or oral nutritional supplements**.
  4. Marked interference with **psychosocial functioning**.
- **B.** Not better explained by lack of available food or a culturally sanctioned practice.
- **C.** Not exclusively during AN or BN, **and no evidence of disturbance in how body weight or shape is experienced**.
- **D.** Not attributable to a concurrent medical condition and not better explained by another mental disorder. If it occurs in that context, severity **exceeds** what is usual and warrants additional attention.

## Specifiers & severity
- **In remission:** full criteria were met before and have not been met for a sustained period.
- There are no severity levels and no subtypes in the criteria. The text describes three presentations (sensory, aversive-consequence, lack of interest) only descriptively.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- It **replaces and extends** DSM-IV feeding disorder of infancy or early childhood to all ages (p. 550).
- **Sensory-based:** extreme sensitivity to appearance, color, smell, texture, temperature or taste. Other labels: "selective," "choosy," "perseverant" eating, "chronic food refusal," "food neophobia." Patients may refuse particular brands or be unable to tolerate others' food smells. Similar to ASD sensory sensitivities (p. 550–551).
- **Aversive-conditioned:** follows or anticipates choking, a traumatic GI procedure (e.g., esophagoscopy) or repeated vomiting. Older terms: "functional dysphagia," "globus hystericus."
- **Lack of interest** in eating or food.
- **A1** is a clinical judgment. In growing youth, look for failure to keep to the expected weight/height trajectory rather than weight loss.
- **A2** is judged by dietary assessment, physical exam and labs. Physical effects can be **as severe as AN** (hypothermia, bradycardia, anemia). In infants it can be life-threatening.
- **A3 "dependence"** means supplementary feeding is *required* for adequate intake. Examples: infant failure to thrive on NG tube; child with a neurodevelopmental disorder relying on complete supplements; G-tube or full oral supplements **without** an underlying medical condition (p. 551).
- **A4:** inability to eat with others, attend school or work, or sustain relationships. **Major family disruption** also counts (only certain foods allowed at home; inordinate trips to specific stores or restaurants).
- **Exclusions (B):** food insecurity, religious fasting, normal dieting.

## Associated features
- **Very young infants:** food refusal, gagging, vomiting. **Infants and young children:** may not engage with the caregiver during feeding or signal hunger, preferring other activities.
- **Older children and adolescents:** generalized emotional difficulties below the threshold for an anxiety, depressive or bipolar disorder ("food avoidance emotional disorder") (p. 551–552).
- Infants may be irritable and hard to console during feeds, or apathetic and withdrawn. Caregiver interaction can contribute (presenting food inappropriately; reading the infant's behavior as aggression or rejection).
- If feeding and weight improve after a **change of caregiver**, consider parental psychopathology, abuse or neglect (p. 552).

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- Little data. An Australian study found **0.3%** among people aged ≥15 years.
- Low-interest restriction most commonly begins in **infancy or early childhood** and may persist. Sensory avoidance arises in the **first decade**. Aversive-consequence avoidance can begin at **any age**.
- Sensory restriction is relatively **stable and long-lasting**, though adults with it may function relatively normally. There is insufficient evidence linking ARFID to later eating disorders.
- More common in children and adolescents than adults, often with a **long delay** between onset and presentation. Triggers for presentation can be physical, social or emotional.
- **Temperamental:** anxiety disorders, ASD, OCD and ADHD raise risk.
- **Environmental:** familial anxiety. Higher rates of feeding disturbance in children of mothers with eating disorders (p. 552–553).
- **Genetic/physiological:** history of GI conditions, GERD, vomiting and other medical problems.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- Similar presentations have been reported in the US, Canada, Australia, Europe, Japan and China. Do **not** diagnose ARFID when avoidance is solely due to religious or cultural practice.
- It appears about **equally common in boys and girls**. ARFID **comorbid with ASD is male-predominant**.
- Sensory-related avoidance in **pregnancy** is usually not extreme and usually does not meet criteria (p. 553).

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed.

## Functional consequences
- Impaired physical development, social difficulties, and major negative impact on family functioning. Mealtime stress at home and in social eating contexts. In young children, growth delay and harm to development and learning.

## Differential diagnosis
ARFID may be co-diagnosed with the conditions below (except AN) if all criteria are met **and** the eating disturbance needs specific clinical attention (p. 553).
- **Other medical conditions** (GI disease, food allergies or intolerances, occult malignancy): the disturbance must go **beyond** what the physical symptoms explain. It may persist after a medical trigger has resolved. For older adults, post-surgical patients or patients on chemotherapy, the eating disturbance must be a **primary focus** of intervention.
- **Neurological, neuromuscular, structural or congenital conditions** (hypotonia, tongue protrusion, unsafe swallow): ARFID can be diagnosed if all criteria are met.
- **OCD and related disorder due to PANS:** consider when onset is **acute**, onset age is **late**, or symptoms are **atypical**. PANS = abrupt, dramatic OC symptoms or severe food restriction with other neuropsychiatric symptoms (p. 553–554).
- **Reactive attachment disorder:** withdrawal from caregivers disrupts feeding. Co-diagnose only if both are met and feeding is a primary focus.
- **ASD:** rigid eating and sensory sensitivity are common but often not impairing enough. Co-diagnose only when specific treatment is needed.
- **Specific phobia (other type: choking or vomiting):** fear-based food avoidance can look like phobia. When **eating becomes the primary focus of clinical attention**, ARFID is the right diagnosis (p. 554).
- **Social anxiety disorder:** fear of being watched while eating can occur in both.
- **Anorexia nervosa:** shares low weight and food avoidance, but AN has fear of fat or behavior that interferes with weight gain **plus** body-image disturbance. **Never diagnose both.** Hardest in late childhood and early adolescence, and with "non–fat phobic AN" (denies fear but persists in preventing gain and fails to recognize seriousness). Use symptoms, course and family history over time. ARFID can **precede** AN (p. 554–555).
- **OCD:** restriction tied to food preoccupations or rituals. Co-diagnose only if aberrant eating needs specific intervention.
- **MDD:** appetite loss reduces overall energy intake and usually resolves with mood. Co-diagnose only if full criteria are met and the eating needs specific treatment.
- **Schizophrenia spectrum:** delusion-driven food avoidance (e.g., beliefs about consequences of foods). Co-diagnose only if full criteria are met and specific treatment is needed.
- **Factitious disorder / imposed on another:** reports of dramatic, inconsistent restriction or claimed need for supplements. In the imposed type, the caregiver may **induce** failure to gain, and the **caregiver** receives the diagnosis (p. 555).
- **Developmentally normal "picky eating":** a transient narrowing of foods in toddlers and children that resolves on its own. Not ARFID unless it causes nutritional failure or significant impairment.

## Comorbidity
- Anxiety disorders, OCD and neurodevelopmental disorders (**ASD, ADHD, IDD**) (p. 556).

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **Criterion A – the disturbance:** name the driver(s): sensory (list the textures, colors or brands tolerated or refused), aversive (the choking or vomiting event and the date), or low interest/appetite. List the accepted foods and how many.
- **Criterion A – consequences (≥1):** weight or BMI percentile trend and growth-curve deviation (A1); labs or deficiency findings, vitals such as bradycardia or hypothermia (A2); NG/G-tube or supplement dependence with no medical cause (A3); missed school or work, avoided shared meals, family accommodations (A4).
- **Criterion B:** food access and cultural or religious practice explored and excluded.
- **Criterion C:** **explicitly record that there is no fear of weight gain, no body-image disturbance and no weight-control motive**. Probe directly and use collateral. This is the core AN rule-out.
- **Criterion D:** medical evaluation (GI, allergy, swallow study as indicated). Explain why the severity exceeds what the coexisting ASD, anxiety, MDD or medical condition would explain.
- **Rule-outs:** PANS (acute onset), factitious (inconsistent history), RAD and caregiver factors in infants, normal picky eating.
- **Phrases:** "Eats ~8 'safe' foods, all beige/crunchy; gags on mixed textures." "Since choking episode in March, refuses solids; drinks supplements only." "Denies desire to lose weight; wishes to gain; no body checking." "Weight crossed two percentile lines over 12 months."
