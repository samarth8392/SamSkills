# Rumination Disorder

> **Spark note:** Repeated, effortless regurgitation of previously swallowed food for **≥1 month**. The food is re-chewed, re-swallowed or spat out, with **no nausea, retching or disgust**. It is usually daily and often observable. Before diagnosing, rule out GI disease (reflux, pyloric stenosis) and regurgitation used to dispose of calories in AN or BN.

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** Repeated regurgitation of food for **≥1 month**. The food may be re-chewed, re-swallowed or spat out.
- **B.** Not attributable to a GI or other medical condition (e.g., GERD, pyloric stenosis).
- **C.** Does **not** occur *exclusively* during AN, BN, BED or ARFID.
- **D.** If it occurs within another mental disorder (IDD or another neurodevelopmental disorder), it must be severe enough to warrant **additional clinical attention**.

## Specifiers & severity
- **In remission:** full criteria were met before and have not been met for a sustained period.
- There are no severity levels.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Regurgitation should be **frequent**: at least several times per week, **typically daily** (p. 548).
- Partially digested food comes back into the mouth **without** apparent nausea, involuntary retching or disgust.
- With IDD or another neurodevelopmental disorder, it must be a **primary** part of the presentation that needs intervention.
- It is often **directly observable** by the clinician. Otherwise rely on self-report or caregiver report. Patients may describe it as habitual or outside their control.

## Associated features
- **Infants:** strain and arch the back with the head held back and make sucking movements with the tongue. They may appear to get satisfaction from it, and may be irritable and hungry between episodes.
- Weight loss and failure to gain are common in infants. Malnutrition can occur **despite** apparent hunger and large intake, especially when every feed is followed by regurgitation that is expelled.
- **Older children and adults:** malnutrition is especially likely when combined with restricted intake. They may hide the behavior (hand over mouth, coughing), avoid eating with others, or skip meals before work or school (e.g., skipping breakfast) (p. 548–549).

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- Historically described mainly in people with intellectual disability. Limited European data suggest **~1–2% of grade-school-age children** (p. 549).
- Onset can be in infancy, childhood, adolescence or adulthood. **Infant onset is usually 3–12 months.**
- In infants it often remits spontaneously, but it can be protracted. Severe malnutrition is possible, and it **can be fatal, especially in infancy**.
- The course is episodic or continuous until treated.
- In infants and in people with IDD or neurodevelopmental disorders, it seems to serve a **self-soothing or self-stimulating** function, like head banging.
- **Environmental** predisposing factors (infants and young children): lack of stimulation, neglect, stressful life situations, parent–child relationship problems.

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed.

## Functional consequences
- Malnutrition → growth delay and harm to development and learning potential.
- Older patients may deliberately restrict intake because regurgitation is socially undesirable, which causes weight loss or low weight.
- Social functioning is affected more in older children, adolescents and adults.

## Differential diagnosis
- **GI conditions** (gastroparesis, pyloric stenosis, hiatal hernia, Sandifer syndrome in infants): usually ruled out by history and observation (p. 549).
- **AN / BN:** regurgitation and spitting out are used to get rid of calories because of weight concerns. If it occurs only during AN or BN, do not diagnose rumination disorder.

## Comorbidity
- It can occur alongside a medical condition or another mental disorder (e.g., GAD). Diagnose it only when severity is **beyond what that condition usually involves** and it needs additional attention.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **Criterion A:** frequency (episodes per day or week), duration ≥1 month, and what happens to the food (re-chewed, re-swallowed or spat out). Note whether it was directly observed or reported by a caregiver.
- **Effortless quality:** document that there is no nausea, retching or disgust. This separates it from vomiting.
- **Criterion B:** medical/GI evaluation done (e.g., pediatric GI consult, reflux work-up) and results.
- **Criterion C:** ask about weight or shape concerns and compensatory intent to exclude AN or BN, and about binge eating (BED) and sensory or fear-based restriction (ARFID).
- **Criterion D:** with IDD, record why the behavior needs its own intervention (weight faltering, aspiration risk, social exclusion).
- **Infant observation:** back arching, head held back, tongue sucking movements, apparent self-soothing. Note weight/growth percentiles.
- **Adolescents and adults:** concealment behaviors (covering mouth, coughing), avoiding shared meals, skipping meals before school or work, related weight loss.
- **Phrases:** "Observed effortless return of food to mouth ~10 min after lunch, re-chewed and re-swallowed without distress." "Denies nausea; describes behavior as automatic."
