# Pica

> **Spark note:** Persistent (≥1 month) eating of nonnutritive, **nonfood** items that is developmentally inappropriate and not a culturally normative practice. It often comes to attention only through medical complications such as bezoar, obstruction, lead poisoning or parasitic infection. Common errors: diagnosing it under age 2, when a cultural practice explains it (e.g., earth eating), or when nonfood intake is mainly a weight-control tactic in AN. In those cases AN is primary.

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** Persistently eats nonnutritive, nonfood substances for **≥1 month**.
- **B.** The behavior is inappropriate for the person's **developmental level**.
- **C.** It is **not** part of a culturally supported or socially normative practice.
- **D.** If it occurs within another mental disorder (IDD, ASD, schizophrenia) or medical condition (**including pregnancy**), it must be severe enough to warrant **additional clinical attention**.
- Note: "nonfood" means low-calorie diet products do **not** count. There is usually no general aversion to food (p. 545).

## Specifiers & severity
- **In remission:** full criteria were met before and have not been met for a sustained period.
- There are no severity levels.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Typical substances vary with age and availability: paper, soap, cloth, hair, string, wool, soil, chalk, talc, paint, gum, metal, pebbles, charcoal/coal, ash, clay, starch, ice (p. 545).
- The **minimum age of 2 years** is suggested to exclude normal mouthing of objects.
- If pica occurs **only** within another mental disorder, diagnose it separately only if it is severe enough to need its own clinical attention.
- In **pregnancy**, cravings (chalk, ice) are diagnosable only when they lead to actually eating nonfood items **to a medically risky extent** (p. 546).

## Associated features
- Vitamin or mineral deficiencies (zinc, iron) have been reported, but often there is no specific biological abnormality.
- It may present through complications: mechanical bowel problems, obstruction (bezoar), perforation, infections such as toxoplasmosis or toxocariasis (from eating feces or dirt), and poisoning (lead paint) (p. 545).
- **Diagnostic markers:** abdominal flat plate X-ray, ultrasound and other imaging can show obstruction. Blood and lab tests check for poisoning or infection (p. 546).

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- About **5%** of school-age children (limited data).
- About **one-third** of pregnant women, especially with food insecurity. A meta-analysis found **28%** during pregnancy and/or postpartum (p. 545–546).
- Associated with lack of available food and vitamin deficiency.
- Onset can be in childhood, adolescence or adulthood; **childhood onset is most common**. It occurs in children who are otherwise developing normally. In adults it is more often seen with IDD or other mental disorders.
- The course can be protracted. Medical emergencies include obstruction, acute weight loss and poisoning, and pica **can be fatal** depending on what is eaten.
- **Environmental** risks: neglect, lack of supervision, developmental delay.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- In some groups, earth eating or similar behavior is believed to have spiritual, medicinal or social value. This **does not** warrant pica (Criterion C). Do not *assume* a behavior is normative without evaluating it (p. 546).
- Pica occurs in all genders. Pregnancy cravings: see above.

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed.

## Functional consequences
- It can significantly impair **physical** functioning. It is rarely the sole cause of social impairment, but it often co-occurs with disorders that impair social functioning.

## Differential diagnosis
- **ASD, schizophrenia, Kleine-Levin syndrome:** nonfood eating can be part of these. Add pica only if it is persistent and severe enough to need separate attention.
- **Anorexia nervosa:** nonfood items (e.g., tissues) eaten to control appetite or weight → **AN is primary** (p. 547).
- **Factitious disorder:** deliberately swallowing foreign objects to falsify illness. Deception is the key feature.
- **NSSI / personality disorders:** swallowing harmful items such as pins, needles or knives as part of maladaptive behavior patterns or self-injury.

## Comorbidity
- Most common: **ASD** and **IDD**. Less often: schizophrenia, OCD.
- Associated with **trichotillomania** and **excoriation disorder**: the hair or skin is typically eaten.
- Associated with **ARFID**, especially when sensory features are strong.
- With known pica, always assess for GI complications, poisoning, infection and nutritional deficiency.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **Criterion A:** list the specific substances, how often and for how long (≥1 month), and how this was learned (self-report, caregiver, direct observation, imaging finding).
- **Criterion B:** chronological and developmental age. Note that the person is ≥2 years, or that developmental level makes the behavior inappropriate.
- **Criterion C:** record the cultural or religious context explored and why the behavior is *not* a normative practice. Do not assume either way.
- **Criterion D:** if IDD, ASD, schizophrenia or pregnancy is present, state why the behavior needs separate clinical attention (e.g., medical risk, repeated ED visits).
- **Rule-outs to document:** weight-control motive (AN), deception or illness falsification (factitious disorder), self-injurious intent (NSSI). Record medical work-up: iron/zinc status, lead level, imaging for obstruction, infection screen.
- **Supportive phrasing:** "Caregiver reports child repeatedly eats paint chips and soil despite redirection, for ~3 months." "Patient describes strong urges to chew ice/starch; denies weight-control intent." "Bezoar found on imaging."
- Note safety and supervision needs, and any hair/skin ingestion linked to trichotillomania or excoriation.
