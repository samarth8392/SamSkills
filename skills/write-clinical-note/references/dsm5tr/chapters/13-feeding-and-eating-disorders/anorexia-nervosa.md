# Anorexia Nervosa

> **Spark note:** Restriction that drives **significantly low weight**, plus fear of fat **or** behavior that keeps preventing weight gain, plus a disturbance in how weight or shape is experienced or valued, or failure to grasp how serious the low weight is. Common errors: requiring an *expressed* fear of fat (it can be inferred), requiring amenorrhea (not a criterion), and diagnosing AN at adult BMI ≥19 or with youth BMI above the median. Consider atypical AN (OSFED) only if the remaining criteria and significant weight-loss presentation are established. Mortality is about 5% per decade, and suicide is the second leading cause of death.

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** Energy intake restricted relative to requirements → **significantly low body weight** for age, sex, developmental trajectory and physical health. This means below minimally normal (adults) or below minimally expected (youth).
- **B.** **Intense fear** of gaining weight or becoming fat, **OR persistent behavior that interferes with weight gain**, despite significantly low weight.
- **C.** ≥1 of: disturbed experience of one's weight/shape; **undue influence** of weight/shape on self-evaluation; **persistent lack of recognition of the seriousness** of the current low weight.
- There is no duration criterion and **amenorrhea is not required**.

## Specifiers & severity
**Subtype** (based on the **last 3 months**; it sets the code):
- **Restricting type (F50.01):** no recurrent binge eating or purging (self-induced vomiting; misuse of laxatives, diuretics or enemas). Weight loss comes mainly from dieting, fasting and/or excessive exercise.
- **Binge-eating/purging type (F50.02):** recurrent binge eating **or** purging. Most who binge also purge. Some do **not** binge but purge regularly after small amounts of food (p. 557).
- **Crossover** between subtypes is common, so the subtype describes *current* symptoms, not lifetime course.

**Remission:**
- **Partial:** Criterion A (low weight) has not been met for a sustained period, but B **or** C is still met.
- **Full:** no criteria met for a sustained period.

**Current severity:** adults by **BMI** (WHO thinness categories); children and adolescents by the corresponding **BMI percentile**. Severity may be raised for clinical symptoms, functional disability or need for supervision.

|---|---|
| Mild | ≥ 17 |
| Moderate | 16–16.99 |
| Severe | 15–15.99 |
| Extreme | < 15 |

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- **Weight thresholds (adults)** (p. 557–558):
  - BMI **18.5** is the CDC/WHO lower limit of normal, so most adults at ≥18.5 are *not* significantly low.
  - BMI **<17.0** (WHO moderate/severe thinness) is likely significantly low.
  - **17.0–18.5, or even slightly above 18.5**, can count as significantly low if clinical history or physiology supports it.
  - BMI **≥19.0** → do **not** diagnose AN. Consider **OSFED – atypical AN**.
- **Children and adolescents:** use BMI-for-age percentile (CDC calculator). **<5th percentile** suggests underweight. Youth **above** the 5th percentile can still qualify if they fail to keep their expected growth trajectory. But if BMI is **above the median for age** → no AN; consider atypical AN (p. 558).
- In youth, failure to gain expected weight or to keep the growth trajectory can replace weight loss.
- **Criterion B:** fear often **intensifies as weight drops** and is not relieved by weight loss. Younger patients and some adults deny fear. If nothing else explains the low weight, the clinician may **infer B** from collateral history, observation, physical or lab findings, or a longitudinal course of weight-preventing behavior (p. 558).
- **Criterion C:** some feel globally overweight. Others accept that they are thin but see the abdomen, buttocks or thighs as "too fat." Body checking includes frequent weighing, obsessive measuring and mirror checking. Self-esteem depends on shape and weight. Weight loss feels like an achievement and self-discipline; gain feels like an unacceptable failure. Patients often acknowledge thinness but **deny the medical seriousness**.
- Patients are usually **brought in by family**. Self-referral usually stems from distress about starvation sequelae, and patients rarely complain of weight loss itself. Insight is poor or the problem is denied, so **collateral is essential** (p. 558–559).

## Associated features
- Semistarvation and purging cause potentially **life-threatening** effects across organ systems. **Amenorrhea** and vital-sign abnormalities are common. Most changes reverse with refeeding, but **loss of bone mineral density** often does not fully reverse.
- **Depressive features** when severely underweight (low mood, withdrawal, irritability, insomnia, low libido) may be secondary to starvation. Add MDD only if severe enough to warrant it.
- **Obsessive-compulsive features:** preoccupation with food, collecting recipes, hoarding food. These may be worsened by undernutrition. Add **OCD** only if obsessions or compulsions are *unrelated* to food, shape or weight.
- Other features: concern about eating in public, feelings of ineffectiveness, strong need for control, inflexible thinking, limited social spontaneity, overly restrained emotional expression.
- The **binge-eating/purging type** has more impulsivity and more alcohol and drug misuse than the restricting type.
- **Excessive physical activity** often precedes onset, speeds weight loss and is hard to control in treatment.
- **Medication manipulation** to lose weight. In diabetes, patients may **omit or reduce insulin** (p. 559).

## Diagnostic markers (labs, exam)
- **Hematology:** leukopenia (with relative lymphocytosis), mild anemia, thrombocytopenia, rarely bleeding.
- **Chemistry:** elevated BUN (dehydration), hypercholesterolemia, elevated liver enzymes. Occasionally low Mg, Zn and PO4 and elevated amylase. **Vomiting** → metabolic alkalosis (↑HCO3), hypochloremia, hypokalemia. **Laxatives** → mild metabolic acidosis.
- **Endocrine:** T4 low-normal, **T3 low, reverse T3 high**. Low estrogen (female) or low testosterone (male).
- **ECG:** sinus bradycardia, rarely arrhythmia, **QTc prolongation** in some.
- **Bone:** low BMD, osteopenia or osteoporosis, elevated fracture risk.
- **EEG:** diffuse abnormalities (metabolic encephalopathy) from fluid and electrolyte shifts.
- **Resting energy expenditure** is often markedly reduced (p. 561).
- **Physical:** emaciation is the most striking sign. Also hypotension, hypothermia, bradycardia, lanugo, peripheral edema (during refeeding or after stopping laxatives or diuretics), petechiae or ecchymoses (rare), hypercarotenemic yellow skin. In those who vomit: parotid hypertrophy, dental enamel erosion, **dorsal hand scars or calluses (Russell sign)**. Symptoms include amenorrhea (usually after weight loss, sometimes before), delayed menarche, constipation, abdominal pain, cold intolerance, lethargy, excess energy (p. 562).

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- 12-month prevalence (two US community studies): **0.0–0.05%** (women 0–0.08%; men 0–0.01%). Lifetime: **0.60–0.80%** (women 0.9–1.42%; men 0.12–0.3%). One adolescent study found similar rates by gender (p. 560).
- Most prevalent in postindustrialized, high-income countries. It is rising in the Global South (Asia, Middle East). In the US it is lower among Latinx and non-Latinx Black Americans than non-Latinx Whites.
- Onset is usually in **adolescence or young adulthood**. Rare before puberty or after 40, but **do not exclude it on older age alone**. Onset often follows a stressor (e.g., leaving home for college). Many patients have a period of changed eating before full criteria are met.
- Younger patients may show atypical features (denying fear of fat). Older patients have longer illness and more signs of chronic disease.
- Course: some recover fully after one episode; others fluctuate with relapse or follow a chronic course. Hospitalization may be needed. **Most remit within 5 years of presentation**; remission rates are lower in hospitalized samples.
- **Crude mortality about 5% per decade**, from medical complications or suicide.
- **Temperamental:** childhood anxiety disorders or obsessional traits.
- **Environmental:** cultures that value thinness; occupations such as modeling and elite athletics.
- **Genetic/physiological:** elevated risk of AN and other disorders in biological relatives. GWAS loci are shared with psychiatric and metabolic traits (insulin resistance, lipids). Imaging shows altered reward processing (unclear whether this is cause or effect of malnutrition).

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- Occurs across diverse populations, but presentation varies. **Absent "fat phobia"** is more common in **Asian** populations, where restriction is often explained by a culturally sanctioned complaint such as **GI discomfort** (p. 561).
- In the US, service use is significantly lower among underserved ethnic and racialized groups.
- Much higher prevalence in women in adult studies.

## Suicide risk
- Suicide rates are about **18×** those of age- and gender-matched comparison groups. Suicide is the **second leading cause of death** in AN.
- **One-quarter to one-third** have suicidal ideation. **9–25%** have attempted suicide.
- Contributors: greater exposure to sexual abuse, impaired decision-making, high rates of **NSSI**, mood-disorder comorbidity (p. 562).

## Functional consequences
- Ranges from preserved social or professional functioning to marked isolation and failure to reach academic or career potential.

## Differential diagnosis
- Be especially careful with atypical features, such as **onset after 40**.
- **Medical conditions** (GI disease, hyperthyroidism, occult malignancy, AIDS): weight loss without body-image disturbance, fear of gain or weight-preventing behavior. Acute weight loss from a medical illness can **trigger or mask** AN. AN can rarely follow **bariatric surgery** (p. 562–563).
- **MDD:** weight loss but no desire for excessive thinness and no fear of gain.
- **Schizophrenia:** odd eating and weight loss, but rarely fear of gain or body-image disturbance.
- **Substance use disorders:** low weight from poor intake without fear of fat. If someone uses **appetite-suppressing stimulants or cocaine and endorses fear of gain**, evaluate for comorbid AN, because the drug use may itself be Criterion B behavior.
- **Social anxiety disorder, OCD, BDD:** add social anxiety disorder only if the social fears are *not limited to eating*. Add OCD only if obsessions are unrelated to food (e.g., contamination). Add BDD only if the concern is unrelated to body shape or size (e.g., the nose) (p. 563).
- **Bulimia nervosa:** binge, compensation and overconcern with shape are shared, but in BN weight stays **at or above minimally normal**.
- **ARFID:** weight loss or deficiency **without** fear of fat or body-image disturbance. **Do not co-diagnose.**

## Comorbidity
- Bipolar, depressive and anxiety disorders are common. Anxiety often **precedes** the eating disorder.
- OCD, especially with the **restricting type**.
- Alcohol and other substance use disorders, especially with the **binge-eating/purging type** (p. 563–564).

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **Criterion A:** measured height and weight, **BMI** (adults) or **BMI-for-age percentile** and growth-curve history (youth), weight trajectory (peak weight, rate of loss), dietary restriction pattern (calories, meals skipped, foods eliminated). Note who weighed the patient and whether it was blind.
- **Criterion B:** direct quotes on fear of gain, **or** observed or collateral weight-preventing behavior (exercise logs, hidden food, purging, insulin omission, stimulant use, water-loading before weigh-ins). If the patient denies fear, document the basis for inference.
- **Criterion C:** body checking, overvaluation ("my day is ruined if the scale goes up"), perceived fat body parts, minimization of medical risk despite abnormal vitals or labs.
- **Subtype:** binge or purge behaviors in the **last 3 months**, with method and frequency.
- **Severity:** current BMI band. Note any reasons for upgrading severity (bradycardia, syncope, need for supervision).
- **Medical risk to record:** HR, orthostatic BP/HR, temperature, K+, PO4, Mg, ECG/QTc, BMD if known, refeeding risk. Menstrual history (not required, but relevant).
- **Rule-outs:** medical work-up for weight loss; MDD, psychosis or substance use as alternative explanations; ARFID (no weight/shape drive). Document why it is **not** atypical AN (BMI <19 or not above the median percentile).
- **Suicide:** SI, past attempts and NSSI must be screened and documented every time given the elevated risk.
- **MSE phrases:** "Emaciated, lanugo on forearms, cold extremities." "Expresses intense fear of gaining weight; describes thighs as 'huge' despite BMI 15.8." "Minimizes HR of 42 as 'athletic.'" "Rigid, perfectionistic, restricted affect around food topics."
