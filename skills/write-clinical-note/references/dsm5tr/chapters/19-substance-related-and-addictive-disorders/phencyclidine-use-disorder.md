# Phencyclidine Use Disorder

> **Spark note:** This use disorder includes PCP and pharmacologically related dissociatives such as ketamine. It shares the general behavioral/tolerance framework but has no established human withdrawal criterion. (pp. 806–810)
## Criteria at a glance
A clinically impairing/distressing pattern with ≥2 of the following in the same 12-month period:
1. More substance or longer use than intended.
2. Persistent wish to reduce use or unsuccessful attempts.
3. Much time spent obtaining/using/recovering.
4. Craving.
5. Repeated failures in major work/school/home roles.
6. Continued use despite recurrent interpersonal/social problems caused or worsened by use.
7. Important activities curtailed or abandoned.
8. Recurrent use in physically hazardous situations.
9. Continued use despite knowing a persistent physical/psychological problem is probably caused or worsened by it.
10. Tolerance: markedly greater dose needed or markedly weaker effect at the same dose.
Withdrawal is not an established DSM criterion for this class; count only these 10 items.
(pp. 806–810)
## Specifiers & severity
Mild = 2–3 criteria; moderate = 4–5; severe = ≥6. Early remission follows previously full criteria with no criteria other than possible craving for ≥3 but <12 months; sustained remission requires ≥12 months. Record restricted access with **in a controlled environment**, including the remission context. Frequency/dose trends supplement, rather than replace, symptom counting. (pp. 757–759)

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
Low doses may cause dissociation; high doses can cause stupor/coma. Routes include smoking, oral, intranasal and injected administration. Acute effects may last hours while elimination/detectability lasts much longer; prolonged psychotic effects may occur in vulnerable individuals. (pp. 808–809)

## Associated features
Nystagmus, analgesia, blood-pressure disturbance, altered perception/derealization and defensive or aggressive behavior may support exposure. Injuries, persistent cognitive complaints, urinary/GI symptoms and respiratory effects can occur. Do not infer dangerousness solely from the diagnosis. (pp. 808–809)

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
Specific use-disorder prevalence is unavailable; the overall hallucinogen disorder estimate is low, around 0.1% among people ≥12 in cited U.S. data. The section gives limited substance-specific prognostic data. (p. 809)

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
The source reports male predominance among PCP-primary treatment admissions, which is not a population prevalence estimate. (p. 809)

## Diagnostic markers
PCP can remain detectable in urine for ≥8 days; examination/history remain necessary to establish a disorder and current presentation. (p. 809)

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed.

## Functional consequences
Accidents/falls/fights and neurological, urinary, gastrointestinal, cardiovascular, sleep and mood problems may accompany repeated use. (p. 809)

## Differential diagnosis
- **Other substances:** PCP can be added to cannabis or cocaine; identify contributors separately.
- **Intoxication/induced mental disorder:** assess acute timing versus the 12-month behavior pattern.
- **Independent psychosis, depression or externalizing disorder:** establish symptoms before and outside exposure. (pp. 809–810)

## Comorbidity
Conduct/antisocial personality disorders and alcohol, cocaine and amphetamine use disorders are common. (p. 810)

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Name the agent, route, dates and exposure pattern; document item-specific consequences/count.
- Exclude an invented withdrawal criterion and distinguish persistent psychosis from ordinary intoxication.
- Record collateral, examination/toxicology evidence and other substance use, with remission dates and actual access restrictions.
