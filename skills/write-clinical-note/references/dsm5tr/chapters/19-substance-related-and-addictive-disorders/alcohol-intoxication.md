# Alcohol Intoxication

> **Spark note:** Recent drinking plus a **problematic** behavioral or psychological change plus **≥1 of 6 neurological signs** (slurred speech, incoordination, unsteady gait, nystagmus, attention or memory impairment, stupor or coma). Being "buzzed" with no problematic behavior is not the diagnosis. The main confusions are **sedative-hypnotic intoxication**, which looks the same but has no alcohol odor, and medical mimics such as **DKA**. It is a major driver of violence, accidents and suicide attempts, with risk rising **~30% per drink**.

## Criteria at a glance (p. 775)
- **A.** Recent alcohol ingestion.
- **B.** Clinically significant **problematic** behavioral or psychological change during or shortly after drinking, e.g., inappropriate sexual or aggressive behavior, mood lability, impaired judgment.
- **C.** **≥1** of the following, developing during or shortly after drinking:
  1. Slurred speech
  2. Incoordination
  3. Unsteady gait
  4. Nystagmus
  5. Impaired attention or memory
  6. Stupor or coma
- **D.** Not attributable to another medical condition, and not better explained by another mental disorder **including intoxication with another substance**.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls (pp. 775–776)
- The changes can be **life-threatening**: impaired judgment and decision-making, difficulty with complex tasks such as **driving**, impaired social and occupational function, and coma if severe.
- Exclude **medical** causes (e.g., diabetic ketoacidosis), **delirium**, and **other depressants** (e.g., benzodiazepines).
- Evidence of use comes from **breath odor**, history from the person or observers, and breath, blood or urine toxicology.
- **Rising vs falling BAC:** signs are more intense while BAC is **rising**. Early on there is **stimulation** (talkative, sense of well-being, bright expansive mood). Later, as BAC falls, the person becomes **depressed, withdrawn and cognitively impaired**.
- **Metabolism:** about **1 drink per hour**, so BAC falls **15–20 mg/dL per hour**.
- **Standard drink** ≈ **10–12 g ethanol**, raising BAC about **20 mg/dL**. Mild intoxication is seen in most people after **~2 drinks**.
- **Blackouts** (amnesia for the episode) are linked to **high BAC** and probably a rapid rise.
- Acute effects: hypoglycemia, electrolyte disturbance, and cardiovascular, respiratory and GI effects.
- **BAC anchors (non-tolerant person):** 200–300 mg/dL → falls asleep, first stage of anesthesia. **>300–400 mg/dL → respiratory and pulse inhibition, death.**

## Associated features
- Major contributor to **interpersonal violence**, **accidental injury and death**, self-harm, suicidal behavior and suicide.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- Most drinkers have been intoxicated at some point. In 2018, **43% of US 12th graders** had ever "been drunk" and **17.5%** had been drunk in the past 30 days (p. 776).
- **12-month high-risk drinking** (≥4 drinks/day for women, ≥5/day for men): Native American 17.4%, African American 15.1%, Latinx 13.5%, non-Latinx White 12.3%, Asian/Pacific Islander 7.2% (p. 777).
- Episodes develop over **minutes to hours** and last **several hours**.
- Average **first intoxication ~15 years**. Peak **18–25**. Declines with age.
- **Earlier onset of regular intoxication → higher likelihood of later AUD.**
- *Temperamental:* sensation seeking, impulsivity.
- *Environmental:* heavy-drinking peers, belief that heavy drinking is central to fun, **drinking to cope with stress**.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues (p. 777)
- Parallels cultural attitudes to drinking. Some fraternities and sororities encourage intoxication. Intoxication is frequent on culturally significant dates (New Year's Eve) and at some events (wakes). Some groups drink at religious celebrations (Jewish, Catholic holidays), while others prohibit drinking (some Mormon, fundamentalist Christian and Muslim groups).
- Western norms historically tolerated drunkenness more in **men**. This gap is shrinking, especially among youth. Women are generally **less tolerant** of the same amount.

## Diagnostic markers (p. 777)
- Usually established by **observed behavior plus alcohol on the breath**. Severity rises with blood or breath alcohol level and with **co-ingested sedating substances**.

## Suicide risk (p. 778)
- A 17-country ED study found acute use raises suicide-attempt risk **independent of chronic use**, with **each drink raising risk by ~30%**. See also Alcohol Use Disorder.

## Functional consequences (p. 778)
- Contributed to **>95,000 US deaths/yr** and 2.8 million years of potential life lost per year (2011–2015). Those who died lost on average **30 years** of life.
- Costs from **drunk driving**, lost school and work time, arguments and physical fights.

## Differential diagnosis (p. 778)
- **Medical and neurological conditions:** diabetic acidosis, cerebellar ataxia and **multiple sclerosis** can temporarily resemble intoxication.
- **Alcohol-induced mental disorders (onset during intoxication):** diagnose these when a symptom such as depressed mood is **in excess of** usual intoxication, **predominates**, and warrants clinical attention.
- **Sedative, hypnotic or anxiolytic intoxication** (and other sedating agents such as antihistamines and anticholinergics): nearly identical behavior, judgment and incoordination, with coma risk. **No alcohol odor.** Toxicology shows the depressant. Workup: breath odor, BAC or breathalyzer, medical workup, history.

## Comorbidity (p. 778)
- Often occurs together with **other substance intoxication**, especially in **conduct disorder / ASPD**. Otherwise see AUD comorbidity.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **A:** time, amount and type of the last drink. Source (self-report, collateral, police or EMS).
- **B:** describe the **problematic** behavior concretely, e.g., "verbally aggressive toward staff," "sexually disinhibited remarks," "labile, tearful then laughing," "attempted to drive home."
- **C:** record the objective signs: "slurred speech," "wide-based unsteady gait," "horizontal nystagmus," "unable to recall events of last hour," "responds only to sternal rub."
- **Objective data:** **BAC or breathalyzer** with time drawn, odor of alcohol, vitals, glucose (rule out hypoglycemia or DKA).
- **D rule-outs:** glucose and metabolic panel, head injury (falls), urine tox for co-ingested **sedatives**, delirium screen.
- **Tolerance clue:** high BAC (e.g., ≥150 mg/dL) with minimal impairment → document, because it supports AUD criterion 10.
- **Safety:** suicide screen once sober enough. Firearm access. Recent conflict.
- **Code** according to whether AUD is comorbid (F10.120 / F10.220 / F10.920).
