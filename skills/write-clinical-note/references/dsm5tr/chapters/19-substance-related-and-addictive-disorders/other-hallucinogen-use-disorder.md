# Other Hallucinogen Use Disorder

> **Spark note:** LSD, psilocybin, mescaline, MDMA and other non-PCP hallucinogens belong here; cannabis has its own class. The source does not recognize a hallucinogen withdrawal diagnosis despite emerging MDMA withdrawal observations. Specify the actual hallucinogen. (pp. 810–815)
## Criteria at a glance
A clinically impairing/distressing pattern with ≥2 of the following in the same 12-month period:
1. More substance or longer use than intended.
2. Persistent wish to reduce use or unsuccessful attempts.
3. Much time spent obtaining/using/recovering.
4. Craving.
5. Repeated failures in major work/school/home roles.
6. Continued use despite recurrent interpersonal/social problems caused or worsened by use.
7. Important activities curtailed or abandoned.
8. Recurrent use in physically hazardous situations.
9. Continued use despite knowing a persistent physical/psychological problem is probably caused or worsened by it.
10. Tolerance: markedly greater dose needed or markedly weaker effect at the same dose.
Withdrawal is not an established DSM criterion for this class; count only these 10 items.
(pp. 810–815)
## Specifiers & severity
Mild = 2–3 criteria; moderate = 4–5; severe = ≥6. Early remission follows previously full criteria with no criteria other than possible craving for ≥3 but <12 months; sustained remission requires ≥12 months. Record restricted access with **in a controlled environment**, including the remission context. Frequency/dose trends supplement, rather than replace, symptom counting. (pp. 757–759)

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
Agents differ greatly in duration and route. MDMA has both hallucinogenic and stimulant properties but is categorized here. Tolerance, risky use, persistence despite health/psychological effects, curtailed activities and time spent recovering can be diagnostically useful. No established human withdrawal criterion is counted. (pp. 812–813)

## Associated features
Visual/perceptual changes may be frightening. Routine toxicology may miss potent low-dose agents such as LSD. (pp. 813–814)

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
Cited U.S. past-year disorder prevalence is about 0.1% at ≥12; much higher estimates in selected heavy-use/clinical samples cannot be generalized. Disorder is concentrated in young adulthood. Early use, sensation seeking, peer drug use and environmental exposure increase risk. (pp. 813–814)

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
Religious/ritual hallucinogen use occurs in several traditions and is not itself diagnostic. Men predominate in U.S. disorder estimates; some MDMA subjective effects differ by sex. (p. 814)

## Suicide risk
For the uncertain hallucinogen-use/suicidality relationship, see [other hallucinogen intoxication](other-hallucinogen-intoxication.md); do not infer a protective causal effect. (p. 819)

## Functional consequences
Intoxication may produce serious hyperthermia, arrhythmia, injury, seizures and organ toxicity. The source discusses persistent effects particularly with MDMA; evidence about disorder-specific long-term functional consequences remains limited. (pp. 814–815)

## Differential diagnosis
- **Other drug use/withdrawal:** adulteration is common; investigate stimulants, alcohol and sedatives.
- **Intoxication or induced mental disorder:** distinguish time-linked syndrome from the longitudinal behavior pattern.
- **Primary psychotic/mood disorders:** determine pre-exposure symptoms and abstinent course; attribution to a past hallucinogen is not proof. (p. 815)

## Comorbidity
Other substance disorders, personality disorders, PTSD and panic attacks commonly coexist. (p. 815)

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Name the agent, route, setting, amount/frequency and suspected adulterants; assess cultural/ritual context.
- Record criterion count and consequences in the 12-month window, without counting withdrawal.
- Document independent psychiatric history, persistent perceptual symptoms, remission/access and limitations of toxicology.
