# Opioid Use Disorder

> **Spark note:** A problematic pattern of opioid use (prescribed or illicit, including fentanyl analogues) with ≥2 of the 11 standard SUD criteria in 12 months. Physiological tolerance/withdrawal from opioids taken **as prescribed under medical supervision does not count**, so a stable chronic-pain patient is not OUD by those two alone. Patients on methadone/buprenorphine/naltrexone who meet no other criteria are coded as being **in remission, on maintenance therapy**, not as having active OUD.

## Criteria at a glance
**A.** Problematic opioid use → clinically significant impairment/distress, **≥2 of 11 within 12 months** (pp. 832–833):
- *Impaired control*
  1. Taken in larger amounts / longer than intended
  2. Persistent desire or failed efforts to cut down/control
  3. Lots of time obtaining, using, recovering
  4. Craving (strong desire/urge)
- *Social impairment*
  5. Recurrent use → failure in major role obligations (work, school, home)
  6. Continued use despite persistent/recurrent social/interpersonal problems caused or worsened by opioids
  7. Important social/occupational/recreational activities given up or reduced
- *Risky use*
  8. Recurrent use in physically hazardous situations
  9. Continued use despite knowing of a persistent physical/psychological problem likely caused/worsened by opioids
- *Pharmacological*
  10. Tolerance: (a) need markedly more for effect, or (b) markedly less effect at same dose. **Not met if taking opioids solely under appropriate medical supervision.**
  11. Withdrawal: (a) characteristic opioid withdrawal syndrome (Criteria A+B of opioid withdrawal), or (b) opioids (or closely related substance) taken to relieve/avoid withdrawal. **Not met if solely under appropriate medical supervision.**

## Specifiers & severity
| Severity | Criteria count |
|---|---|
| Mild | 2–3 |
| Moderate | 4–5 |
| Severe | ≥6 |

- **In early remission:** full criteria previously met; **no criteria for ≥3 mo but <12 mo** (craving, A4, may persist).
- **In sustained remission:** no criteria for **≥12 mo** (craving excepted).
- **On maintenance therapy** (opioid-specific): taking a prescribed agonist (methadone, buprenorphine) and no OUD criteria met for that medication class **except tolerance to/withdrawal from the agonist**. Also applies to partial agonist, agonist/antagonist, or full antagonist (oral or depot naltrexone). Used as a further specifier of remission (p. 834).
- **In a controlled environment:** access to opioids restricted (closely supervised substance-free jails, therapeutic communities, locked units). Further specifier of remission.
- Track severity change over time by frequency (days/month) and dose (injections, pills) via self-report, collateral, observation, biological testing.

| F11.11 | Mild, in early or sustained remission |
| F11.20 | Moderate **or** severe OUD |
| F11.21 | Moderate or severe, in early or sustained remission |

- **Do not use these codes if opioid intoxication, withdrawal, or an opioid-induced mental disorder is also present.** The OUD is then carried in the 4th character of the induced-disorder code (F11.1x = mild OUD; F11.2x = moderate/severe OUD; F11.9x = no OUD). E.g., F11.14 mild OUD with opioid-induced depressive disorder; F11.24 moderate/severe OUD with opioid-induced depressive disorder (p. 834).
- Record the specific substance name (e.g., "F11.20 severe heroin use disorder", "oxycodone use disorder").
- Diagnose each SUD separately when several are present (e.g., F11.20 severe heroin use disorder; F14.20 moderate cocaine use disorder).

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Class includes natural opioids (morphine, codeine), semisynthetics (heroin, oxycodone, hydrocodone, hydromorphone, oxymorphone), synthetics (methadone, meperidine, tramadol, fentanyl, carfentanil), and mixed agonist/antagonists (pentazocine, buprenorphine), which at low doses act like classic agonists (p. 835).
- Medical uses: analgesia, anesthesia, antidiarrheal, antitussive. Heroin usually injected, but also smoked/snorted when pure; fentanyl injected nonmedically, transdermal/transmucosal medically.
- Arises from prescription or illicit opioids (recently, fentanyl-related synthetics).
- Core pattern: compulsive, prolonged self-administration for a non-legitimate purpose or in a "non-medical" manner (greatly exceeding the prescribed amount). A pain patient who takes much more than prescribed, **not only because of persistent pain**, is engaging in nonmedical use and may have OUD.
- Most have tolerance and withdrawal on abrupt stopping.
- Conditioned cue-reactive craving (paraphernalia, images) drives relapse and persists long after withdrawal ends.
- Daily life organised around obtaining/administering. Sources: family/friends, falsified or exaggerated complaints, multiple prescribers, illegal market. Health care professionals may self-prescribe or divert.

## Associated features
- Overdose (fatal or nonfatal) = unconsciousness + respiratory depression + pinpoint pupils; can occur without intoxication-seeking.
- US overdose trends: mostly prescribed opioids until 2009 → heroin rise from 2010 → since 2015 synthetic opioids (mostly fentanyl) exceed prescribed-opioid deaths.
- Drug-related crime (possession, distribution, forgery, burglary, robbery, larceny, receiving stolen goods); in clinicians, licensing-board/hospital-staff problems.
- Marital difficulties, divorce, unemployment/irregular employment at all SES levels.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
**Prevalence (p. 836)**
- Nonmedical Rx opioid use, US adults: 4.1–4.7% (18–25: 5.5% vs ≥26: 3.4%). Heroin use: 0.3–0.4% (0.5–0.7% at 18–25).
- Adolescents 12–17: nonmedical Rx use 2.8–3.9%; heroin <0.05–0.1%.
- Rx OUD, adults: 0.6–0.9%; heroin use disorder 0.1–0.3%. Ages 12–17: Rx OUD 0.4%, heroin UD ≈0%.
- Higher in men, young adults, lower income/education.
- Nonmedical Rx OUD by group (2012–13): Native American 1.42%, African American 1.04%, non-Latinx White 0.96%, Latinx 0.70%, Asian American/Pacific Islander 0.16%.
- Household surveys likely underestimate (exclude jails/prisons/institutions).
- Global 2016: 26.8 million cases of DSM-IV opioid dependence; 353/100,000; regional 0.14–0.46%.

**Development & course**
- Can begin at any age; first problems usually late teens/early 20s.
- Early use may be relief-seeking from stressors or psychological pain.
- Treated OUD often persists for many years; brief abstinence common, long-term abstinence only in a minority.
- Exception: Vietnam-era US soldiers, where >90% stayed abstinent from opioids after returning home, though many later had alcohol or amphetamine problems or suicidality.

**Risk & prognostic**
- Associated with most other SUDs; externalizing traits (novelty-seeking, impulsivity, disinhibition).
- Family, peer, social-environment factors; strong genetic contribution (family/twin studies); peer selection may reflect genetic predisposition.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- Historically overrepresented in socially oppressed ethnoracial groups; now increasingly common in White individuals, reflecting availability, poverty and unemployment. The criteria perform equally well across ethnoracial groups.
- Women: more likely to start in response to sexual abuse or violence, or to be introduced by a partner. **Telescoping**: faster progression to the disorder. More ill at treatment entry.

## Diagnostic markers
- Urine tox positive for most opioids (heroin, morphine, codeine, oxycodone, propoxyphene) for **12–36 h**.
- **Fentanyl and oxycodone are missed by standard (morphine-based) urine screens**; specialized assays detect them for several days.
- Methadone and buprenorphine (± naloxone) do not trigger a routine opiate screen; specific tests detect them for days to >1 week.
- Polysubstance positives common (cocaine, cannabis, alcohol, amphetamines, benzodiazepines).
- Hepatitis A/B/C antigen/antibody often positive in people who inject; mildly elevated LFTs; HIV prevalent.
- Subtle cortisol and thermoregulation changes for up to 6 months after withdrawal.

## Suicide risk
- Elevated risk of suicide attempts and suicide; shared risk factors.
- Repeated intoxication/withdrawal can cause severe, temporary depressions intense enough to prompt attempts.
- **Accidental overdose vs suicide attempt**: distinct and hard to tell apart. Try not to confuse them.
- Suicide is a common cause of death among regular opioid users and is undercounted in opioid-poisoning data.
- VHA data: OUD raises suicide mortality after psychiatric adjustment, more so in women. Among veterans on opioids for chronic pain, suicide mortality rises with dose.
- After an opioid overdose, SMR for suicide was 25.9, higher in women. Proposed shared drivers: comorbid mental disorders and pain.

## Functional consequences
- Dry mouth/nose, severe constipation, reduced visual acuity (miosis).
- Injection: "tracks", sclerosed veins, edema, migration to leg/neck/groin veins, then "skin-popping" with cellulitis, abscesses and round scars. Also tetanus, *C. botulinum*, endocarditis, hepatitis (HCV up to 90% of PWID), HIV (up to 60% in some US/Russian areas; lower where clean equipment is available), and TB (often latent, reactivation with HIV).
- Snorting: nasal mucosal irritation, septal perforation.
- Sexual dysfunction (ED in males; menstrual/reproductive disturbance in females).
- **Opioid-induced hyperalgesia** with chronic use.
- Neonatal physiological dependence in about half of infants born to females with OUD (neonatal withdrawal), with prevalence rising sharply.
- Mortality **6–20×** the general population. About 400,000 Rx-opioid overdose deaths in the US since 1999. By 2017, synthetic-opioid overdose rates were about double the Rx or heroin rates. Also deaths from infections, CV disease, accidents and injuries.

## Differential diagnosis
- **Opioid intoxication / withdrawal / opioid-induced mental disorders:** syndromes in the context of heavy use, whereas OUD is a pattern of impaired control, social impairment, risky use and pharmacological sx. Diagnose them in addition when present, with OUD carried in the code.
- **Alcohol or sedative-hypnotic intoxication:** look alike, but **no miosis and no response to naloxone**. In mixed intoxication, naloxone reverses only part of the sedation.
- **Sedative-hypnotic withdrawal:** shares anxiety/restlessness, but lacks **rhinorrhea, lacrimation, mydriasis**.
- **Hallucinogen or stimulant intoxication:** dilated pupils, but no nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal cramps, rhinorrhea or lacrimation.
- **Independent mental disorders** (e.g., persistent depressive disorder): opioid effects can mimic depressed mood. Opioids are *less* likely than most drugs to produce mental disturbance.

## Comorbidity
- Medical: viral (HIV, HCV) and bacterial infections, esp. injection heroin use (less in Rx OUD).
- Other SUDs: tobacco, alcohol, cannabis, stimulants, benzodiazepines.
- PDD/MDD (induced or exacerbated independent), especially in chronic intoxication or under stress. Insomnia, especially in withdrawal.
- Bipolar I, PTSD, antisocial/borderline/schizotypal PD; childhood conduct disorder is a significant risk factor; associated with serious mental illness.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **Count and name criteria** with dated examples within one 12-month window, e.g., "uses 2–3 bags heroin/day versus 1 intended (A1); 3 failed attempts to quit this year (A2); misses work for 'dope sick' days (A5)."
- **Medical-supervision rule:** for patients on prescribed opioids, state explicitly whether use follows the prescription. Document *non-prescribed* behaviours (early refills, multiple prescribers, using for euphoria or for reasons other than pain, buying illicitly). Don't count tolerance/withdrawal alone.
- **Maintenance therapy:** record the agent and dose (methadone, buprenorphine, naltrexone), the date of last non-agonist use, and that no criteria apart from craving and agonist tolerance/withdrawal were met. Then "in early/sustained remission, on maintenance therapy".
- **Remission clock:** document the date criteria were last met (3–12 mo = early; ≥12 mo = sustained), noting craving is allowed.
- Route (IV, intranasal, smoked, oral), substance (fentanyl exposure, pressed pills), overdose history (number, naloxone reversals), injection sequelae (tracks, abscesses), and labs (UDS with fentanyl/buprenorphine/methadone assays, HCV/HIV).
- **Suicide screen at every contact**, and ask whether past overdoses were intentional, ambivalent or accidental. Document naloxone kit access.
- MSE elements: miosis or mydriasis, nodding/psychomotor slowing, slurred speech, rhinorrhea, yawning, piloerection, restlessness; affect (dysphoric/irritable); craving intensity.
- Rule-outs to document: intoxication/withdrawal (code as combined code instead), opioid-induced depressive/anxiety/sleep disorder versus independent disorder (timeline relative to use and abstinence).
