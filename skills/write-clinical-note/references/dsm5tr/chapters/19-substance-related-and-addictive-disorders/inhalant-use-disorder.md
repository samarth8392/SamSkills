# Inhalant Use Disorder

> **Spark note:** This class covers intentional misuse of volatile hydrocarbons, not every gas inhaled. Nitrous oxide and nitrites belong to other (or unknown) substances; accidental exposure can produce intoxication but does not establish an inhalant use disorder. (pp. 823–829)
## Criteria at a glance
A clinically impairing/distressing pattern with ≥2 of the following in the same 12-month period:
1. More substance or longer use than intended.
2. Persistent wish to reduce use or unsuccessful attempts.
3. Much time spent obtaining/using/recovering.
4. Craving.
5. Repeated failures in major work/school/home roles.
6. Continued use despite recurrent interpersonal/social problems caused or worsened by use.
7. Important activities curtailed or abandoned.
8. Recurrent use in physically hazardous situations.
9. Continued use despite knowing a persistent physical/psychological problem is probably caused or worsened by it.
10. Tolerance: markedly greater dose needed or markedly weaker effect at the same dose.
Withdrawal is not an established DSM criterion for this class; count only these 10 items.
(pp. 823–829)
## Specifiers & severity
Mild = 2–3 criteria; moderate = 4–5; severe = ≥6. Early remission follows previously full criteria with no criteria other than possible craving for ≥3 but <12 months; sustained remission requires ≥12 months. Record restricted access with **in a controlled environment**, including the remission context. Frequency/dose trends supplement, rather than replace, symptom counting. (pp. 757–759)

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
Name a specific agent only when supported (many products are mixtures); otherwise record inhalant. Toluene, glue, fuel and paint-related hydrocarbons are examples. Withdrawal complaints can occur but the source does not recognize a class withdrawal diagnosis/criterion. Standard drug screens usually miss these agents. (pp. 825–826)

## Associated features
Odor, containers/rags, perioral or nasal rash, collateral exposure reports and intoxication with negative routine toxicology may suggest use. White-matter injury and neurological problems can accompany persistent use. None alone proves the behavioral disorder. (p. 826)

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
Cited U.S. past-year disorder prevalence is about 0.1% among youth and 0.04% among adults. Use often remits in early adulthood; earlier, more frequent and multi-agent use is associated with persistence. Impulsivity, trauma, disinhibition, poverty and easy product access are risk contexts. (pp. 826–827)

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
Some high-prevalence settings reflect poverty, homelessness and product availability; do not generalize these findings to all members of a population. Adolescent disorder rates are similar across sexes; adult female disorder is described as rare. (p. 827)

## Suicide risk
Use and use disorder are associated with suicidal thoughts/behavior, particularly with mood/anxiety symptoms and trauma. (p. 828)

## Functional consequences
Acute arrhythmia, anoxia and other toxicity can be fatal even apart from chronic consequences. Cognitive and neurological impairment, hepatic/renal disease and pulmonary problems can cause sustained disability. (pp. 828–829)

## Differential diagnosis
- **Unintentional exposure:** can cause intoxication; use disorder requires intentional use.
- **Intoxication/induced conditions:** distinguish syndrome and repeated behavior; one exposure is not automatically a use disorder.
- **Other SUD:** identify which symptoms persist when each agent is absent. (p. 828)

## Comorbidity
Mood/anxiety/personality disorders, conduct or antisocial personality disorders, other SUDs and hepatic/renal or other systemic toxicity often coexist. (pp. 828–829)

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Establish intentional use, product identity/mixture, route, duration/frequency, collateral and actual physical findings.
- Map the 10 countable criteria to dates and functional effects; do not count withdrawal.
- Document medical/cognitive injury, other agents, psychiatric differential and suicide assessment when performed.
