# Tobacco Use Disorder

> **Spark note:** Includes cigarettes, smokeless tobacco and electronic nicotine delivery products; ordinary prescribed nicotine replacement rarely produces the disorder. This class has distinctive coding: mild disorder is Z72.0, and DSM lists no tobacco intoxication diagnosis. (pp. 877–882)

## Criteria at a glance
Clinically impairing/distressing tobacco use with ≥2 in 12 months:
1. More/longer use than intended.
2. Persistent wish or failed efforts to cut down.
3. Excessive time obtaining or using (recovery is not part of this tobacco item).
4. Craving.
5. Repeated failures in major roles.
6. Continued use despite associated relationship/social problems.
7. Important activities reduced or abandoned.
8. Repeated use in dangerous settings (such as around flammables).
9. Continued use despite knowing related physical/psychological harm.
10. Tolerance (higher dose required or weaker effect of usual dose).
11. Characteristic withdrawal or nicotine/tobacco use to prevent/relieve it. (p. 877)

## Specifiers & severity
Mild 2–3; moderate 4–5; severe ≥6. Early remission ≥3–<12 months, sustained ≥12 months with no criteria except possible craving after a prior full syndrome. **On maintenance therapy** describes long-term medication with no medication-related disorder criteria apart from tolerance/withdrawal. **In a controlled environment** records restricted access, in remission context. (pp. 878–879)

The criteria list gives no separate mild-remission code. If withdrawal or induced sleep disorder is present, use its combination code instead; the source allows these only with moderate/severe tobacco use disorder. (p. 878)

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
Rapid nicotine delivery and higher content increase reinforcement. Some role/time criteria are less common than craving, failed quitting or continued use despite illness. Smoking soon after waking, night smoking and cue-induced urges support assessment but are not additional criteria. Quantity alone does not replace counting criteria. (p. 879)

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
Source U.S. adult past-year DSM-5 prevalence in 2012–2013 was about 20%, with about half of daily smokers affected. Smoking usually starts before 21; full criteria may emerge over years. Externalizing traits, other psychiatric disorders, low socioeconomic resources and genetic vulnerability increase risk. (p. 880)

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
Cultural norms, discrimination, education/income, product exposure and nicotine metabolism affect patterns and persistence. Male smoking rates exceed female rates overall; withdrawal/relapse patterns may vary. Avoid applying population averages as individual diagnostic criteria. (pp. 880–881)

## Diagnostic markers
Breath carbon monoxide and nicotine/cotinine in biological samples indicate exposure but correlate only weakly with disorder. (p. 881)

## Suicide risk
Source studies associate tobacco use/disorder with suicidal thoughts, behavior and mortality even after some covariate adjustment; this does not supply a causal individual risk estimate. (p. 881)

## Functional consequences
Tobacco-related cardiovascular/pulmonary disease and cancer cause major morbidity/mortality. Non-nicotine combustion constituents account for many harms; distinguish smoking from long-term replacement therapy. (pp. 881–882)

## Differential diagnosis
Assess clinically significant disorder versus exposure alone; differentiate withdrawal and psychiatric/medical comorbidity through timing and independent criteria. No tobacco intoxication syndrome is provided in the chapter. (pp. 759, 877–884)

## Comorbidity
Other SUDs, depression/bipolar/anxiety disorders, ADHD and personality disorders are common; cardiovascular disease, COPD, cancer and perinatal complications are associated medical conditions. (p. 882)

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Record all products, nicotine content/route if known, daily pattern and cessation history.
- Document dated item-specific evidence/count, actual role/health effects and remission interval.
- Distinguish controlled access, maintenance medication and current withdrawal; record biomarkers as exposure evidence only.
