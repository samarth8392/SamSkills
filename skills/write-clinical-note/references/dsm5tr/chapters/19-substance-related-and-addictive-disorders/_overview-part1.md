# Substance-Related and Addictive Disorders: Overview, Part 1

> **Scope of this file:** chapter intro, the general substance-use / intoxication / withdrawal / induced-disorder framework, and the ICD-10-CM combination-code logic, plus a section map for **alcohol, caffeine, cannabis, hallucinogens (PCP + other) and inhalants** (pp. 753–832). Opioids onward (p. 832+) and gambling disorder are mapped in [_overview.md](_overview.md). Use [_overview.md](_overview.md) for the complete chapter map.

> **Spark note:** Every class (except caffeine) uses the **same 11-item use-disorder template**: ≥2 items in 12 months, severity set by count (2–3 mild, 4–5 moderate, ≥6 severe). The thing people most often get wrong is **coding**. ICD-10-CM folds the use disorder into the code for any co-occurring intoxication, withdrawal or induced disorder. If one of those is present, you **do not** give a separate use-disorder code. The 4th character (1 = mild, 2 = moderate/severe, 9 = none) carries the use-disorder severity.

## 1. What unites the chapter (pp. 754–756)
- **10 classes:** alcohol; caffeine; cannabis; hallucinogens (split into **phencyclidine [or similar arylcyclohexylamines]** and **other hallucinogens**); inhalants; opioids; sedatives/hypnotics/anxiolytics; stimulants (amphetamine-type, cocaine, other); tobacco; other (or unknown). The classes overlap and are not fully distinct.
- **Common mechanism:** all drugs taken in excess directly activate the brain **reward system** (reinforcement and memory). The activation is intense enough that normal activities may be neglected. Each class gets there by a different pharmacological route, but the result is a "high."
- Low self-control (impaired brain inhibitory mechanisms) can be seen **before** use starts in some people. Use itself also damages inhibitory mechanisms.
- **Terminology:** DSM avoids "addiction" as a diagnostic term because its definition is uncertain and it can be stigmatizing. The neutral term is **substance use disorder** (mild to severe). Clinicians may still say "addiction" informally for severe cases.
- **Gambling disorder** is included because gambling activates similar reward systems and produces comparable behaviors. **Internet gaming disorder** sits in Conditions for Further Study. "Behavioral addictions" such as sex, exercise or shopping are **not** included because the evidence is insufficient.
- **Two groups:**
  1. **Substance use disorders** (SUD)
  2. **Substance-induced disorders:** intoxication, withdrawal and substance/medication-induced mental disorders (psychotic, bipolar, depressive, anxiety, OCRD, sleep, sexual, delirium, NCD). The induced disorders are written up in the chapters that share their phenomenology.
- "Substance/medication-induced mental disorder" covers CNS effects of drugs of abuse, psychotropic medications, other medications (e.g., steroids) and **environmental toxins** (e.g., organophosphates).

## 2. Substance use disorder: generic framework (pp. 756–759)

**Essence:** a cluster of cognitive, behavioral and physiological symptoms showing **continued use despite significant substance-related problems**. Applies to all classes **except caffeine**. Brain-circuit changes may persist after detox and show up as relapse and cue-triggered craving, which is why long-term treatment approaches can help.

### The 11 criteria in 4 clusters (Criterion A: ≥2 within a 12-month period, causing clinically significant impairment or distress)

| Cluster | # | Paraphrase |
|---|---|---|
| **Impaired control** | 1 | Uses more, or for longer, than intended |
| | 2 | Persistent wish to cut down, or failed attempts to cut down or control use |
| | 3 | Lots of time spent getting, using or recovering. In severe cases daily life revolves around the drug |
| | 4 | **Craving**: strong urge, most likely in drug-associated settings. Driven by classical conditioning. Current craving is a relapse signal and a treatment-outcome measure |
| **Social impairment** | 5 | Recurrent use leads to failure in major role obligations (work, school, home) |
| | 6 | Keeps using despite persistent or recurrent social or interpersonal problems caused or worsened by use |
| | 7 | Important social, occupational or recreational activities are given up or reduced |
| **Risky use** | 8 | Recurrent use in physically hazardous situations |
| | 9 | Keeps using despite knowing of a persistent physical or psychological problem likely caused or worsened by the substance. The key is **failure to abstain**, not the mere existence of the problem |
| **Pharmacological** | 10 | **Tolerance**: needs markedly more for the effect, or gets a markedly reduced effect from the same dose |
| | 11 | **Withdrawal**: the class-specific syndrome, or taking the substance (or a related one) to relieve or avoid it |

**Key rules**
- **Neither tolerance nor withdrawal is required** for the diagnosis. A history of withdrawal does predict a more severe course (earlier onset, more intake, more problems).
- **Withdrawal criterion does not apply** to PCP, other hallucinogens or inhalants. They have only 10 countable items, but the same thresholds apply.
- **Tolerance or withdrawal from appropriately prescribed medication** (opioid analgesics, sedatives, stimulants) taken as prescribed **does not count**. Diagnosing on those symptoms alone is a classic error. An SUD can still be diagnosed if there is other compulsive drug-seeking.
- Tolerance can be hard to judge from history alone. A high blood level with little sign of intoxication suggests tolerance. Distinguish it from innate low sensitivity, e.g., a first-time drinker who looks sober after 3–4 drinks.
- Rates of tolerance differ by effect: respiratory depression versus sedation versus motor effects.
- Withdrawal signs are **obvious** for alcohol, opioids and sedatives, and **often present but less apparent** for stimulants, tobacco and cannabis.

### Severity (by count of criteria met)
| Severity | Criteria met |
|---|---|
| Mild | 2–3 |
| Moderate | 4–5 |
| Severe | ≥6 |

Change over time is also tracked through frequency and dose of use, using self-report, collateral, clinician observation and biological testing.

### Course and descriptive specifiers (same wording across classes)
- **In early remission:** full criteria were met before, and then **no criteria for ≥3 months but <12 months**. **Craving (A4) is the only exception and may be present.**
- **In sustained remission:** no criteria (except craving) at any time for **≥12 months**.
- **In a controlled environment:** access to the substance is restricted. It is a **further specifier of remission** (e.g., "in early remission, in a controlled environment"). Examples: closely supervised substance-free jails, therapeutic communities, locked hospital units.
- **On maintenance therapy:** listed among the general SUD specifiers (p. 757). It applies only to classes with agonist or maintenance treatment, i.e., opioids and tobacco (see the complete chapter overview). **Not available** for alcohol, cannabis, PCP, other hallucinogens or inhalants.
- Remission codes: severity **mild** in remission → **F1x.11**. **Moderate or severe** in remission → **F1x.21**.

### Recording SUD (pp. 757–759)
- Use the **class code** but **write the specific substance name**, e.g., "F13.20 moderate alprazolam use disorder" or "F15.10 mild methamphetamine use disorder."
- **Substances with no class** (e.g., anabolic steroids) → other (or unknown) substance code **F19.x**, with the substance named (e.g., F19.10 mild anabolic steroid use disorder). An **unknown substance** also gets F19 (e.g., F19.20 severe unknown substance use disorder).
- **More than one SUD** → diagnose and code each separately.
- **Stand-alone SUD codes (F1x.10/.11/.20/.21) are used only when there is NO comorbid intoxication, withdrawal or induced mental disorder** (see §5).

## 3. Intoxication and withdrawal: generic framework (pp. 759–762)

### Generic intoxication
- **A** Recent ingestion. **B** Clinically significant problematic behavioral or psychological change (e.g., belligerence, mood lability, impaired judgment) during or shortly after use, due to CNS effects. **C** Class-specific signs. **D** Not due to a medical condition or better explained by another mental disorder.
- Common domains: perception, wakefulness, attention, thinking, judgment, psychomotor behavior, interpersonal behavior.
- **Does not apply to tobacco.**
- Common in people **without** an SUD as well.
- Acute and chronic intoxication can look different. Example: cocaine causes gregariousness at first but social withdrawal with repeated use over days or weeks.
- A physiological effect alone, such as isolated tachycardia without problematic behavior, is **not** "substance intoxication."
- Intoxication may **outlast detectability** in body fluids because CNS effects persist. Distinguish this from withdrawal, which is triggered by a **falling** level.

### Generic withdrawal
- **A** Class-specific problematic behavioral change, with physiological and cognitive features, caused by **stopping or reducing heavy, prolonged use**. **B** Class-specific syndrome. **C** Clinically significant distress or impairment. **D** Not due to a medical condition or another mental disorder.
- Usually, but not always, accompanies an SUD. There is usually an urge to re-dose.
- Withdrawal from prescribed medication taken appropriately does **not** count toward an SUD.

### Pharmacology principles
- **Route:** IV, smoked or snorted routes give faster, more intense intoxication and more escalation toward withdrawal. Rapid-acting drugs intoxicate more immediately.
- **Duration of action:** short-acting drugs carry **higher withdrawal potential**. Long-acting drugs produce a **later-onset, longer, and generally milder** withdrawal. Half-life parallels time to onset and duration.
- **Multiple substances:** record each intoxication or withdrawal **separately**.
- **Labs:** blood or urine confirm recent use and identify the agent. A positive test ≠ disorder, and a negative test does not rule a disorder out. Normal function at high blood levels → tolerance. Labs also help with unknown-agent withdrawal.
- **Development:** ages 18–24 have the highest prevalence for almost every substance. Intoxication is usually the first substance-related disorder and often starts in the teens. Withdrawal can occur at any age given enough dose and duration.

### Recording intoxication/withdrawal
- Use the class code and name the specific substance (e.g., F13.230 secobarbital withdrawal; F15.120 methamphetamine intoxication).
- The code **depends on whether there is a comorbid SUD**. F15.120 = intoxication **with mild** use disorder; without SUD (and without perceptual disturbances) it would be F15.920.
- Unclassed or unknown substances use F19 (e.g., F19.920 anabolic steroid intoxication; F19.920 unknown substance intoxication).
- If symptoms fall short of any specific disorder → **unspecified [class]-related disorder** (e.g., F12.99).
- **One combined code covers both:** e.g., heroin withdrawal plus moderate heroin use disorder = single code **F11.23**.

## 4. Substance/medication-induced mental disorders: shared framework (pp. 762–765)

Potentially severe, **usually temporary**, sometimes persisting CNS syndromes caused by drugs of abuse, medications or toxins. Full criteria live in the phenomenology chapters. The shared structure is:

- **A.** Clinically significant symptoms of the relevant class predominate.
- **B.** History, exam or labs show **both**: (1) onset **during or soon after** intoxication, withdrawal, or exposure to or withdrawal from a medication; **and** (2) the agent **can produce** these symptoms.
- **C.** Not better explained by an **independent** mental disorder. Evidence for an independent disorder:
  1. symptoms **preceded** severe intoxication, withdrawal or medication exposure; **or**
  2. symptoms **persisted a substantial time (e.g., ≥1 month)** after acute withdrawal, severe intoxication or the medication stopped. The 1-month rule **does not apply** to substance-induced **NCD** or **HPPD**, which persist by definition.
- **D.** Not exclusively during **delirium**.
- **E.** Clinically significant distress or impairment.

**Pattern generalizations (p. 763)**
- **Sedating drugs** (alcohol, sedatives/hypnotics/anxiolytics) → **depression during intoxication** and **anxiety during withdrawal**.
- **Stimulating drugs** (amphetamines, cocaine) → **psychosis and anxiety during intoxication** and **major depressive episodes during withdrawal**.
- Both sedating and stimulating drugs → temporary **sleep** and **sexual** disturbances.
- **Medications:** NCD from anesthetics, antihistamines and antihypertensives. Toxins: organophosphates, insecticides, carbon monoxide. Psychosis from anticholinergic, cardiovascular and steroid drugs and from OTC stimulant- or depressant-like drugs. Severe mood disturbance from steroids, antihypertensives, disulfiram, and any depressant or stimulant.
- If symptoms occur **only during a substance-induced delirium** (e.g., alcohol withdrawal delirium), diagnose **only the delirium**.
- People with induced disorders also show the associated features of the substance class.

**Course**
- Most resolve within **~1 month** (often days to weeks) of stopping, even without psychotropics.
- **Long-duration exceptions:** alcohol-, inhalant- and sedative-induced **NCD**, and **HPPD**.
- Taking substances on top of a **pre-existing** mental disorder tends to **intensify** that disorder.
- Risk rises with **quantity and frequency** of use.
- Consequences can be just as severe as in the independent disorders, **including suicide**, but they generally fade within a month of abstinence.
- **Why it matters:** the cross-sectional picture can be identical to an independent disorder, but treatment and prognosis differ. Rule out an induced disorder **before** diagnosing an independent one, just as you would rule out a medical cause.

**Recording**
- When **comorbid with an SUD**, give a **single** diagnosis that names the substance, the induced disorder and the SUD severity (e.g., "cocaine-induced psychotic disorder, with severe cocaine use disorder").
- **Without an SUD** (e.g., one-time use, or a medication taken as prescribed), record only the induced disorder (e.g., "corticosteroid-induced depressive disorder").
- Induced disorders are diagnosed **instead of** intoxication or withdrawal **only when** symptoms exceed what is usual for intoxication or withdrawal, **predominate**, and **warrant independent clinical attention**.

## 5. ICD-10-CM coding logic (the most testable part)

### Structure of an F1x code
```
F1  x  .  y  zz
    |     |  └─ 5th–6th chars: the induced condition (e.g., 20 intoxication, 30 withdrawal, 59 psychotic, 80 anxiety…)
    |     └──── 4th char: comorbid use-disorder status → 1 = mild · 2 = moderate or severe · 9 = no use disorder
    └────────── class: 0 alcohol · 1 opioid · 2 cannabis · 3 sedative · 4 cocaine · 5 other stimulant (incl. caffeine) · 6 hallucinogen (PCP + other) · 7 nicotine · 8 inhalant · 9 other/unknown
```
- **Rule 1:** if intoxication, withdrawal or an induced disorder is present, **do not** also code the SUD. Its severity is carried in the 4th character.
- **Rule 2:** moderate and severe share the same code (2). The severity word is still **written** in the diagnosis name.
- **Rule 3:** the 4th character "9" means no SUD (e.g., one-time use, use without a disorder, or medication taken as prescribed).
- **Rule 4:** remission is coded **only** on the stand-alone SUD code (F1x.11 / F1x.21).
- **Caffeine** has no use disorder, so caffeine codes are **always the "without use disorder" F15.9xx forms**.
- **Text quirk:** the alcohol use disorder coding note (p. 767) gives "F10.129 / F10.229" as the example intoxication codes. The alcohol intoxication criteria set and the TR code listing give **F10.120 / F10.220 / F10.920**. Use F10.120/220/920.

## 7. Notable content in this edition
- **Updated ICD-10-CM combination codes** throughout. Intoxication, withdrawal and induced codes carry the SUD severity, and moderate and severe share one code.
- **Withdrawal codes split by perceptual disturbances** for alcohol (x.130/x.132 pattern). Cannabis intoxication is also split (x.120/x.122).
- **Cannabis withdrawal** has an explicit **F12.93** code for withdrawal without an SUD, e.g., under appropriate medical supervision. The text also stresses medical-cannabis tolerance and withdrawal as **not** the primary basis for diagnosing an SUD.
- **Delirium from medications taken as prescribed** (pharmaceutical cannabinoid agonists; ketamine) is cross-referenced to the NCD chapter.
- **Alcohol-induced NCD amnestic-confabulatory type** (Wernicke-Korsakoff / "persisting amnestic disorder") is the named exception to the 1-month resolution rule for alcohol.
- Expanded **suicide** sections. Acute alcohol use carries ~7× the risk of a suicide attempt, with ~30% added risk per drink. Chronic, rather than acute cannabis use, has evidence of association with suicidality. The hallucinogen–suicide link is uncertain.
- Updated content on **vaping and dabbing**, high-potency cannabis, synthetic cannabinoids (K2/Spice), **ketamine's antidepressant use**, MDMA withdrawal evidence, and cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome.
- Structural-inequity language has been added to the risk factors (discrimination, differential incarceration, unequal access to addiction medications).

## 8. Chapter-level pitfalls and differential logic
1. **Use disorder vs. use:** occasional intoxication, or even daily low-dose drinking, is not an SUD. Fewer than 20% of drinkers and 20–30% of cannabis users develop a disorder. Look for the **pattern** across the 11 items.
2. **Prescribed medication:** physiological tolerance or withdrawal alone ≠ SUD.
3. **Intoxication vs. induced disorder:** call it an induced disorder only when the symptom (e.g., anxiety, depressed mood, psychosis) is **in excess of** the usual intoxication or withdrawal picture, **predominates**, and **warrants independent attention**.
4. **"With perceptual disturbances" vs. induced psychotic disorder:** perceptual disturbances mean hallucinations with **intact reality testing**, or illusions without delirium. If reality testing is lost, consider **substance/medication-induced psychotic disorder** instead.
5. **Induced vs. independent disorder:** symptoms that **preceded** use, or **persisted ≥~1 month** after stopping, point to an independent disorder. A pre-existing disorder can be **intensified** by use.
6. **Delirium trumps:** symptoms confined to a delirium are coded as the delirium only.
7. **Withdrawal does not exist (as a diagnosis) for PCP, other hallucinogens or inhalants**. Do not count criterion 11 for them.
8. **Caffeine has no use disorder diagnosis** in the main text, so caffeine codes are always "without use disorder."
9. **Nitrous oxide and amyl/butyl/isobutyl nitrites** → **other (or unknown) substance** (F19), not inhalant.
10. **MDMA** is classed as an "other hallucinogen" (F16). **Cannabis and THC** are **not** hallucinogens.
11. **Ketamine** belongs with **phencyclidine** (arylcyclohexylamine), not other hallucinogens.
12. **Polysubstance:** record each substance's diagnoses separately. Contamination is common (PCP added to cannabis or cocaine; contaminated hallucinogens).
13. **Lab tests** support but never establish or exclude a diagnosis. Standard drug screens **miss inhalants**. THC metabolite stays positive for weeks in heavy users. LSD is potent at microgram doses and is often missed.
14. **Conduct disorder / ASPD** co-occur with nearly every SUD and predict earlier onset and worse prognosis. Establish both.

## Documentation cues (chapter-wide)
- For an SUD, document **which of the 11 items** are met, with dated examples, a **12-month window**, the **count → severity**, and **remission status with timeframe** (3–12 mo vs ≥12 mo). Note whether craving persists, since it is allowed in remission. Note whether the setting is a **controlled environment**.
- Name the **specific substance** (e.g., "alprazolam," "toluene," "LSD"), not only the class.
- Document **sources**: self-report, collateral, observation, labs (BAC, GGT/CDT, urine tox).
- For intoxication or withdrawal, document **time since last use**, objective signs (vitals, tremor, nystagmus, pupils), **reality-testing status** where perceptual symptoms are present, and medical rule-outs.
- For induced disorders, document **temporal onset** (during intoxication vs. withdrawal), whether symptoms **pre-dated** use, and **reassessment after ~1 month of abstinence**.
- If an induced condition is present, record **one combined code** and do not list a separate SUD code.
