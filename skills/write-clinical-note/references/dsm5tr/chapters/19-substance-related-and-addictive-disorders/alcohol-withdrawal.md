# Alcohol Withdrawal

> **Spark note:** After stopping or cutting down **heavy, prolonged** drinking, **≥2 of 8** signs appear within hours to days: autonomic hyperactivity, tremor, insomnia, nausea or vomiting, transient hallucinations or illusions, agitation, anxiety, **grand mal seizures**. Onset is about **4–12 h**, the peak is **day 2**, and it is much better by **day 4–5**. Fewer than 10% develop dramatic features such as DTs, and fewer than 3% seize. Common mistakes: confusing "with perceptual disturbances" (reality testing intact) with an alcohol-induced psychotic disorder or withdrawal **delirium**, and missing withdrawal in hospitalized patients.

## Criteria at a glance (pp. 779)
- **A.** Stopping or reducing alcohol use that has been **heavy and prolonged**.
- **B.** **≥2** of the following, within **several hours to a few days** of A:
  1. Autonomic hyperactivity (e.g., sweating, **pulse >100 bpm**)
  2. Increased hand tremor
  3. Insomnia
  4. Nausea or vomiting
  5. **Transient** visual, tactile or auditory hallucinations or illusions
  6. Psychomotor agitation
  7. Anxiety
  8. Generalized tonic-clonic seizures
- **C.** Clinically significant distress or impairment.
- **D.** Not due to another medical condition, and not better explained by another mental disorder **including intoxication with or withdrawal from another substance**.

## Specifiers & severity (pp. 779–780)
- **With perceptual disturbances:** rare. Hallucinations (usually visual or tactile) **with intact reality testing**, or auditory, visual or tactile **illusions**, in the **absence of delirium**.
- If hallucinations occur **with clear sensorium but without intact reality testing**, consider **substance/medication-induced psychotic disorder**.
- If consciousness or cognition is disturbed → **alcohol withdrawal delirium** (NCD chapter).

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls (p. 780)
- The picture is autonomic hyperactivity plus anxiety plus GI symptoms.
- **Relieved by alcohol or benzodiazepines** (e.g., diazepam).
- **Time course:** onset as BAC falls sharply (**4–12 h**), **peak on day 2**, **marked improvement by day 4–5**. **Protracted** low-level anxiety, insomnia and autonomic dysfunction can last **3–6 months** and feed relapse.
- **<10%** ever develop dramatic symptoms (severe autonomic hyperactivity, tremors, withdrawal delirium). **Tonic-clonic seizures in <3%.**

## Associated features (pp. 780)
- **Alcohol withdrawal delirium (delirium tremens):** confusion and changes in consciousness are not core withdrawal criteria, but delirium may develop. It includes disturbed consciousness and cognition plus visual, tactile or (rarely) auditory hallucinations.
- **When DTs develop, suspect a concurrent medical condition:** liver failure, pneumonia, GI bleeding, sequelae of head trauma, hypoglycemia, electrolyte imbalance, postoperative status.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- About **50%** of middle-class, high-functioning people with AUD have ever had full withdrawal. In **hospitalized or homeless** people with AUD the rate is **>80%**. **<10%** of those in withdrawal have DTs or seizures. No difference across US ethnoracial groups (p. 780–781).
- An episode lasts **4–5 days** and occurs only after **extended heavy drinking**. **Rare under 30.** Risk and severity **rise with age** (p. 781).
- Higher risk with heavier intake (often in CD/ASPD), **concurrent dependence on other depressants**, and **more prior withdrawals** (kindling).
- **Predictors of severe withdrawal:** prior **withdrawal delirium**, prior severe withdrawal, **low potassium**, **low platelets**, **systolic hypertension**.
- *Environmental:* risk rises with quantity and frequency. Most are drinking **daily, >~8 drinks/day, for multiple days**.
- Individual risk is enhanced by medical comorbidity, a **family history of withdrawal** (genetic), prior withdrawals and co-use of sedatives.

## Diagnostic markers (p. 781)
- **Autonomic hyperactivity with moderately high but falling BAC**, plus a history of prolonged heavy drinking, suggests withdrawal.

## Functional consequences (p. 781)
- Withdrawal perpetuates drinking and relapse, keeping social and occupational function impaired.
- Medically supervised detox → hospital use and lost productivity.
- Withdrawal is associated with **greater impairment and poorer prognosis** in AUD.

## Differential diagnosis (pp. 781–782)
- **Other medical conditions:** **hypoglycemia** and **DKA** can mimic withdrawal. **Essential tremor** (often familial) can mimic withdrawal tremulousness.
- **Alcohol-induced mental disorders (onset during withdrawal)** such as alcohol-induced anxiety disorder: symptoms are **in excess** of usual withdrawal, predominate, and warrant attention.
- **Sedative, hypnotic or anxiolytic withdrawal:** a **very similar** syndrome. Rely on history and toxicology.
- **Generalized anxiety disorder** and other mental disorders (Criterion D).

## Comorbidity
- See Alcohol Use Disorder.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **A:** drinking history (drinks/day, duration), **time of last drink**, abrupt stop vs taper.
- **B:** list the signs with objective data, e.g., "HR 112, diaphoretic" (B1); "coarse bilateral hand tremor on extension" (B2); "slept 2 h" (B3); "vomited ×2" (B4); "sees insects on wall, knows they are not real" (B5, supports *with perceptual disturbances*); "pacing, unable to sit" (B6); "reports feeling 'jittery, on edge'" (B7); "witnessed GTC seizure ~24 h after last drink" (B8).
- **Severity tracking:** a standardized withdrawal scale score if used (e.g., CIWA-Ar), vitals trend, BAC trend (falling).
- **Reality testing and orientation:** document insight into perceptual symptoms and **orientation and attention** to separate perceptual disturbances from psychosis or **delirium**.
- **Risk factors for complicated withdrawal:** prior DTs or seizures, number of prior withdrawals, K+, platelets, BP, co-use of benzodiazepines, age, medical comorbidity.
- **Rule-outs:** glucose, electrolytes, liver function, infection, head trauma, essential tremor history, sedative withdrawal (tox screen).
- **Code** with AUD severity and perceptual status, e.g., "F10.232 alcohol withdrawal, with perceptual disturbances, with severe alcohol use disorder."
