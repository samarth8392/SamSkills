# Sedative, Hypnotic, or Anxiolytic Withdrawal

> **Spark note:** An alcohol-withdrawal-like syndrome (≥2 of 8) after stopping prolonged sedative use. It is **potentially lethal**: untreated, up to **20–30% have grand mal seizures**, and withdrawal delirium can occur. Timing follows half-life: hours for short-acting agents, but **>1 week** for diazepam, so late-onset symptoms can be mistaken for relapse of the original anxiety disorder.

## Criteria at a glance
- **A.** Cessation or reduction of **prolonged** sedative, hypnotic or anxiolytic use.
- **B.** **≥2 of 8**, within several hours to a few days after A:
  1. Autonomic hyperactivity (e.g., sweating, pulse **>100 bpm**)
  2. Hand tremor
  3. Insomnia
  4. Nausea or vomiting
  5. Transient visual, tactile or auditory hallucinations or illusions
  6. Psychomotor agitation
  7. Anxiety
  8. Grand mal seizures
- **C.** Clinically significant distress/impairment.
- **D.** Not due to another medical condition or better explained by another mental disorder, including intoxication/withdrawal from another substance.

## Specifiers & severity
- **With perceptual disturbances:** hallucinations with intact reality testing, or auditory/visual/tactile illusions, in the absence of delirium.
- Hallucinations **without** intact reality testing → consider substance/medication-induced psychotic disorder.
- If withdrawal **delirium** is present (disturbed consciousness and cognition with hallucinations), diagnose delirium **instead of** withdrawal.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Follows a marked decrease or cessation after **several weeks or more** of regular use.
- Autonomic signs include raised HR, RR, BP and temperature, with sweating.
- Severe withdrawal hallucinations usually occur within a withdrawal delirium.
- **Relief by any sedative-hypnotic supports the diagnosis.**
- Can occur without a use disorder after abrupt discontinuation of long-term therapeutic-dose benzodiazepines.

## Associated features: timing and dose
| Agent type | Onset | Peak | Marked improvement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short-acting, rapidly absorbed, no active metabolites (e.g., triazolam) | Within hours | — | — |
| Action ≤~10 h (lorazepam, oxazepam, temazepam) | 6–8 h after levels fall | Day 2 | Day 4–5 |
| Long half-life / active metabolites (diazepam) | 1–2 days or longer; may be **>1 week** | Week 2 | Weeks 3–4 |

- Low-intensity symptoms can persist for **several months**.
- Longer duration and higher dose → more severe withdrawal.
- Dose anchors (diazepam-equivalent daily): withdrawal reported with as little as **15 mg for several months**; **~40 mg** more likely to cause clinically relevant withdrawal; **~100 mg** more likely to be followed by **seizures or delirium**.
- Life-threatening delirium possible.

## Prevalence · Development & course
- Prevalence unknown.
- For course, risk, culture and functional consequences, the text refers to the use disorder.

## Diagnostic markers
- **Seizures + autonomic instability + history of prolonged sedative exposure** = high likelihood of SHA withdrawal.

## Differential diagnosis
- **Other medical conditions:** hypoglycemia, DKA. If seizures are present, consider infection, head injury and poisoning.
- **Essential tremor:** familial; can be mistaken for withdrawal tremor.
- **Alcohol withdrawal:** near-identical syndrome; distinguished by history, plus liver damage or cardiomyopathy pointing to alcohol.
- **SHA-induced mental disorders** (e.g., SHA-induced anxiety disorder with onset during withdrawal): symptoms exceed usual withdrawal, predominate, and warrant attention.
- **Anxiety disorders:** relapse of an underlying anxiety disorder mimics withdrawal, but **delirium and true seizures are never anxiety-disorder symptoms**. An abrupt dose cut suggests withdrawal. During a taper the two are hard to separate, and lingering withdrawal (anxiety, moodiness, poor sleep) can be misread as GAD or depression.

## Comorbidity
See the use disorder.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Agent(s), daily dose (diazepam-equivalent), duration, half-life class, date/time of last dose or dose cut; abrupt stop versus taper.
- Each Criterion B sign with objective data: HR (>100?), BP, temperature, sweating, tremor, vomiting, agitation, any seizure (witnessed, type).
- Perceptual disturbances: content, reality testing intact or not, orientation/attention to exclude delirium.
- Prescription status. If taken as prescribed with no use disorder, use F13.93x and say so.
- Prior withdrawal seizures or delirium (predict severity); concurrent alcohol use.
- When anxiety rises during a taper, document the timing relative to the dose change and the pre-benzodiazepine baseline anxiety, to separate withdrawal or rebound from an independent anxiety disorder.
- Safety: seizure precautions or medical-level withdrawal management; never advise abrupt cessation of high doses.
