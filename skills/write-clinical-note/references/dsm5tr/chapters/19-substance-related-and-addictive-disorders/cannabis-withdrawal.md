# Cannabis Withdrawal

> **Spark note:** Cannabis withdrawal is often behavioral/emotional rather than visibly dramatic. A physical symptom is one optional symptom item; it is not mandatory when three other items are met. (pp. 803–805)

## Criteria at a glance
- **A:** Heavy, prolonged cannabis use ends; usually this means daily/nearly daily exposure for at least a few months.
- **B:** Within about a week, ≥3 of: irritability/anger/aggression; nervousness/anxiety; disrupted sleep or disturbing dreams; appetite/weight reduction; restlessness; depressed mood; **or** a physical-symptom cluster with ≥1 significantly uncomfortable abdominal pain, tremor, sweating, fever, chills or headache. Count that cluster once.
- **C:** Clinically meaningful distress or impaired functioning.
- **D:** No better medical, psychiatric, or other substance explanation. (p. 803)

## Specifiers & severity
No formal severity scale or perceptual-disturbance specifier is provided. Greater intensity/frequency of prior use generally predicts greater symptoms, but no exact exposure threshold is established. (pp. 803–804)

## Coding
| Comorbid cannabis use disorder | Source code |
|---|---|
| Mild | F12.13 |
| Moderate/severe | F12.23 |

Use the combination code without a second stand-alone use-disorder code. (p. 803)

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
Symptoms typically start in 24–48 hours, peak at 2–5 days and resolve in 1–2 weeks; sleep problems can last longer. Fatigue, yawning, concentration difficulty or later rebound appetite/sleep can accompany the syndrome without adding separate criterion items. (pp. 803–804)

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
The source reports the full DSM-5 syndrome in about 12% of adult regular users in a general-population study; much higher rates in heavy-use/treatment samples should not be generalized. Withdrawal can occur in adolescents and adults. Heavier use and psychiatric symptoms increase severity; women may experience greater intensity. (p. 804)

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed in this section.

## Functional consequences
Symptoms, particularly sleep difficulty, can motivate renewed cannabis use or other-drug use to obtain relief. (p. 804)

## Differential diagnosis
Assess tobacco/alcohol and other withdrawals, primary depression/anxiety/bipolar illness and medical causes. Establish onset after reduction rather than assuming cannabis is treating an independent condition. (p. 805)

## Comorbidity
Depression, anxiety, antisocial personality disorder and cannabis use disorder commonly coexist. (p. 805)

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Record prior use pattern, last use/reduction and dated onset of each counted symptom.
- Document distress/function and the physical cluster's significant discomfort if counted.
- Assess other withdrawals/psychiatric symptoms and determine use-disorder status independently.
