# Alcohol Use Disorder

> **Spark note:** AUD is the generic 11-item SUD template applied to alcohol: ≥2 items in 12 months, with severity set by count. Heavy drinking alone or occasional drunkenness is **not** AUD. Fewer than 20% of drinkers ever develop it. The commonest errors are **coding** errors. Do not use F10.1x/F10.2x when alcohol intoxication, withdrawal or an alcohol-induced disorder is also present, because the combined code carries the AUD. Another common error is treating AUD as hopeless: the typical case has a **far better prognosis** than the treatment-seeking minority suggests.

## Criteria at a glance (p. 765–766)
**A.** Problematic alcohol use causing clinically significant impairment or distress, with **≥2 of 11 items within 12 months**:

*Impaired control*
1. Drinks larger amounts or over a longer period than intended.
2. Persistent desire to cut down or control drinking, or unsuccessful efforts to do so.
3. A great deal of time spent obtaining alcohol, drinking, or recovering.
4. **Craving** (strong desire or urge to drink).

*Social impairment*
5. Recurrent drinking leads to failure in major role obligations (work, school, home).
6. Keeps drinking despite persistent or recurrent social or interpersonal problems caused or worsened by alcohol.
7. Important social, occupational or recreational activities given up or reduced.

*Risky use*
8. Recurrent drinking in physically hazardous situations.
9. Keeps drinking despite knowing of a persistent or recurrent physical or psychological problem likely caused or worsened by alcohol.

*Pharmacological*
10. **Tolerance**: (a) needs markedly more to reach intoxication or the desired effect, **or** (b) markedly less effect from the same amount.
11. **Withdrawal**: (a) the alcohol withdrawal syndrome (Alcohol Withdrawal Criteria A–B), **or** (b) alcohol **or a closely related drug (e.g., a benzodiazepine)** taken to relieve or avoid withdrawal.

## Specifiers & severity (p. 766–767)
| Severity | Criteria met |
|---|---|
| Mild | 2–3 |
| Moderate | 4–5 |
| Severe | ≥6 |

- **In early remission:** full criteria were met before, then **no criteria for ≥3 to <12 months**. Craving (A4) may still be present.
- **In sustained remission:** no criteria (except craving) at any time for **≥12 months**.
- **In a controlled environment:** access to alcohol is restricted. Used as a further specifier of remission, e.g., in a closely supervised substance-free jail, therapeutic community or locked unit.
- There is **no "on maintenance therapy" specifier** for alcohol.
- Track change over time through **frequency** (drinking days per month) and **dose** (standard drinks per day), using self-report, collateral, observation and labs.

## Coding
| Code | Use when |
|---|---|
| F10.10 | Mild AUD, no comorbid alcohol intoxication, withdrawal or induced disorder |
| F10.11 | Mild AUD, in early **or** sustained remission |
| F10.20 | Moderate **or** severe AUD (write the severity in words) |
| F10.21 | Moderate or severe AUD, in early or sustained remission |
| *(none: use combined code)* | If alcohol intoxication, withdrawal or an alcohol-induced mental disorder is present, code **only** that disorder. The 4th character carries AUD severity: **1** = mild, **2** = moderate/severe |

Examples: AUD (moderate) plus alcohol intoxication → **F10.220** alone. Mild AUD plus alcohol-induced depressive disorder → **F10.14** alone. See the overview tables for every combined code.
*Source quirk:* the coding note on p. 767 uses "F10.129/F10.229" as its intoxication example. The intoxication criteria set gives **F10.120/F10.220**, which are the correct TR codes.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls (pp. 767–768)
- **Withdrawal** starts about **4–12 h** after reducing heavy, prolonged intake. Drinking to avoid or relieve withdrawal drives continued use despite harm. Low-grade symptoms such as **sleep problems can persist for months** and contribute to relapse.
- **Craving** is an urge so strong it is hard to think of anything else, and it often leads to drinking.
- Role failure can come from hangover or from intoxication at school or work, from neglect of child care or household duties, and from absences.
- Hazardous use: **driving**, swimming or operating machinery while intoxicated.
- Use despite harm: **blackouts**, liver disease, depression, violent arguments with a spouse, child abuse.

## Associated features (pp. 768–769)
- Often co-occurs with problems from **other substances**. Alcohol may be used to ease their side effects or to substitute when they are unavailable.
- **Conduct problems, depression, anxiety and insomnia** often accompany heavy drinking and sometimes come first.
- **Organ effects:** GI (gastritis, ulcers; **cirrhosis and/or pancreatitis in ~15%** of heavy drinkers; esophageal and gastric cancers), **low-grade hypertension** (very common), cardiomyopathy and myopathies, raised triglycerides and LDL → heart disease, **peripheral neuropathy**.
- **CNS:** cognitive deficits, cerebellar degeneration, and thiamine and other B-vitamin deficiency. **Wernicke-Korsakoff** (persisting amnestic disorder) is rare but devastating and severely impairs new learning. It is now coded as **alcohol-induced major NCD, amnestic-confabulatory type**.
- Suicide risk is raised during severe intoxication and during temporary alcohol-induced depressive or bipolar episodes.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
**Prevalence (pp. 769)**
- US adult **lifetime** DSM-5 AUD **29.1%**: mild 8.6%, moderate 6.6%, **severe 13.9%**. Australia: 31.0%.
- Lifetime: men **36.0%**, women 22.7%.
- 12-month DSM-IV AUD: **4.6%** at ages 12–17, **16.2%** at 18–29, **1.5%** at ≥65.
- Adolescents (12-month): Native American 2.8%, non-Latinx White 2.2%, Asian American 1.6%, multiracial 1.6%, Hispanic 1.5%, African American 0.8%.
- Adults (12-month DSM-5): African American 14.4%, non-Hispanic White 14.0%, Hispanic 13.6%, Asian American/Pacific Islander 10.6%. Native American tribal samples ranged 4.1–9.8%. Communities vary widely and some have high abstinence rates, so interpret with caution. Historical dispossession and ongoing discrimination raise risk.

**Development & course (pp. 769–770)**
- First intoxication in the **mid-teens**. Isolated problems may appear before 20. Onset of a ≥2-criterion cluster **peaks in the late teens to early/mid 20s**. Most cases develop by the **late 30s**.
- **Withdrawal appears late**, after many other features are already established.
- Earlier onset is seen with pre-existing **conduct problems** and early intoxication.
- Course is **remitting and relapsing**. A crisis prompts weeks of abstinence, then a period of controlled drinking, and once drinking resumes it tends to **escalate rapidly**.
- It is **not intractable**. Treatment-seekers are a severe minority, and the typical person has a much better prognosis.
- About **10%** have **later onset** (after 40). In older adults, increased brain sensitivity, slower liver metabolism and less body water mean more severe intoxication at lower intake, and more medical complications.

**Risk & prognostic factors (pp. 770–771)**
- *Environmental:* poverty, **discrimination and structural inequities** (differential incarceration, unequal access to addiction medications), unemployment, low education, cultural attitudes, availability and price, stress. Mediators include heavy-drinking peers, **exaggerated positive expectancies** and poor coping.
- *Genetic and physiological:* **40–60% of variance genetic**. Risk is **3–4×** in close relatives and rises with the number and closeness of affected relatives and their severity. MZ concordance exceeds DZ. Adoption studies show a 3–4× risk in adopted-away children.
  - **Low-risk phenotype:** the alcohol **flushing** reaction (more common in Asian descent).
  - **High-risk phenotypes:** pre-existing **schizophrenia or bipolar disorder**, **impulsivity**, and a **low level of response (low sensitivity) to alcohol**.
  - Any single gene variant explains only **1–2%** of risk. Gene × environment: genetic effects are stronger when social constraints are low (low parental monitoring, deviant peers).
- *Course modifiers:* high impulsivity → earlier onset and more severe AUD.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues (pp. 771–772)
- Alcohol is the most-used intoxicant in most cultures. **2.8 million deaths/yr** globally (2.2% of age-standardized female deaths, **6.8% of male deaths**). There are about 237 million men and 46 million women with AUD worldwide. Prevalence is highest in the **European Region** (men 14.8%, women 3.5%) and the **Americas** (men 11.5%, women 5.1%), and higher in high-income countries.
- **Acculturation** to the US raises AUD risk in immigrants, especially women. **Ethnic density** is protective through social support. **Segregation** is a risk through concentrated alcohol advertising and outlets.
- **ALDH/ADH polymorphisms** (up to **40%** of Japanese, Chinese and Korean individuals) cause flushing and palpitations and protect against AUD. The protection is weakening with westernization and changing norms about women drinking.
- The criteria perform **equally well across ethnoracial groups**.
- Men have higher rates, but the **gap is narrowing** as women start younger. Women reach **higher BAC per drink** (less body water, more fat, less gastric metabolism) and are more vulnerable to **blackouts and liver disease**. Environmental risk factors differ by sex, especially in adolescence. **Drinking during pregnancy** may itself signal AUD.

## Diagnostic markers (pp. 772–773)
Markers **flag** people who need more assessment. They do not make the diagnosis.
| Marker | Key point |
|---|---|
| **BAC** | The most direct cross-sectional measure. **150 mg/dL with no signs of intoxication → tolerance.** At 200 mg/dL most non-tolerant people are severely intoxicated |
| **GGT** | Sensitive. Modestly raised or high-normal (**>35 units**) and may be the only abnormal lab. **≥70%** of people with high GGT are persistent heavy drinkers (**≥8 drinks/day**) |
| **CDT** | Sensitivity and specificity comparable or better. **≥20 units** identifies regular use of ≥8 drinks/day |
| GGT + CDT | Better together. Both normalize within **days to weeks**, so they are good for **monitoring**. A **rise** over time suggests return to heavy drinking |
| **MCV** | High-normal from direct toxicity to red-cell production. **Poor for monitoring** because RBCs are long-lived |
| LFTs (ALT, alk phos) | Show liver injury |
| Nonspecific | Raised triglycerides and HDL, high-normal **uric acid** |

**Physical signs:** dyspepsia, nausea, bloating (gastritis); hepatomegaly, esophageal varices, hemorrhoids; tremor, unsteady gait, insomnia, erectile dysfunction; **testicular atrophy and feminization** in men; menstrual irregularity in women, plus **spontaneous abortion and fetal alcohol syndrome** in pregnancy. Prior epilepsy or head trauma → alcohol-related seizures. In withdrawal: nausea, vomiting, hematemesis, dry mouth, puffy blotchy complexion, mild peripheral edema.

## Suicide risk (pp. 773–774)
- Risk factors in AUD (Australian psychological autopsy): **aggression, psychiatric comorbidity, recent interpersonal conflict**.
- Both **acute intoxication and chronic heavy use** are linked to suicide. Restrictive alcohol policies may prevent suicide at the population level.
- **Acute use → about a sevenfold increase in suicide-attempt risk.** Heavier use within 24 h is much more potent than lighter use.
- **Alcohol plus sedatives** used together is associated with an even higher attempt risk.
- Alcohol use is linked to **firearm** possession. Drinkers are **4–6×** more likely to die by suicide with a gun, and heavy drinkers prefer firearms over other methods.
- Disinhibition, sadness and irritability in severe use all contribute.

## Functional consequences (pp. 773–774)
- Areas affected: driving and machinery, school and work, relationships, health. Absenteeism, job accidents, low productivity. Rates are elevated in homeless people, yet **most people with AUD live with family and stay employed**.
- About **1 in 5 urban ICU admissions** are alcohol-related. **40%** of Americans have an alcohol-related adverse event in their lifetime. Alcohol accounts for **up to 55% of fatal driving events**.
- Severe AUD, especially **with ASPD**, is linked to criminal acts including homicide.
- **Missed AUD in hospitalized patients → unanticipated withdrawal**, which adds risk, cost and length of stay.

## Differential diagnosis (p. 774)
- **Nonpathological use:** fewer than 20% of drinkers develop AUD. Daily low-dose drinking or occasional intoxication alone does not qualify. The key is heavy use **with repeated significant distress or impairment**.
- **Alcohol intoxication, withdrawal and induced disorders:** these are syndromes arising from heavy use. AUD is the problematic **pattern**. They often co-occur, and the AUD is then captured in the combined code.
- **Sedative, hypnotic or anxiolytic use disorder:** signs and symptoms are very similar, but the course differs, especially the medical problems. Distinguish the two.
- **Conduct disorder / ASPD:** AUD and other SUDs occur in most people with ASPD or prior CD. They predict earlier onset and worse prognosis, so diagnose both.

## Comorbidity (pp. 774–775)
- Bipolar disorders, schizophrenia and ASPD. Most anxiety and depressive disorders.
- Part of the depression–AUD association reflects **temporary alcohol-induced depressive symptoms** during intoxication or withdrawal. This is long debated.
- Severe repeated intoxication suppresses immunity, which raises the risk of infection and cancer.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **Map each endorsed criterion** with a concrete example and date. For example: "drinks 'just two' but finishes the bottle 4×/wk" (A1); "three failed attempts to stop since January" (A2); "missed 5 shifts after drinking nights" (A5); "DUI arrest in March" (A8); "continues despite physician's warning re elevated LFTs" (A9); "needs ~10 drinks for the effect previously from 5" (A10); "morning tremor relieved by a drink" (A11b).
- State the **12-month window**, the **criterion count** and the resulting **severity**.
- **Remission:** document the date criteria were last met, months symptom-free (3–12 vs ≥12), whether craving persists, and whether in a **controlled environment**.
- **Quantity and frequency:** standard drinks per day, drinking days per month, binge pattern, last drink (time).
- **Objective data:** BAC or breathalyzer, GGT, CDT, MCV, LFTs. Physical signs (tremor, diaphoresis, hepatomegaly, stigmata).
- **Withdrawal history:** prior seizures or DTs, which are predictors of severe withdrawal. Prior medically supervised detox.
- **Rule-outs to document:** independent mood or anxiety disorder (onset before drinking, or persistence >1 month into abstinence); sedative use disorder; medical causes of the presenting symptoms.
- **Suicide screen:** acute intoxication, recent interpersonal conflict, firearm access, co-use of sedatives.
- **Coding check:** if intoxication, withdrawal or an induced disorder is present, record the single combined code (e.g., "F10.230 alcohol withdrawal, without perceptual disturbances, with moderate alcohol use disorder") and **no separate F10.20**.
- MSE and phrasing examples: "odor of alcohol on breath," "fine bilateral hand tremor," "minimizes consequences of drinking," "reports strong urges to drink when passing the bar after work," "collateral (spouse) confirms nightly drinking and two blackouts this month."
