# Cannabis Use Disorder

> **Spark note:** Cannabis use becomes a disorder through impaired control and meaningful consequences, not a positive test or daily use alone. Include synthetic cannabinoids; assess the medical-use context before interpreting physiological dependence. (pp. 791–800)
## Criteria at a glance
A clinically impairing/distressing pattern with ≥2 of the following in the same 12-month period:
1. More substance or longer use than intended.
2. Persistent wish to reduce use or unsuccessful attempts.
3. Much time spent obtaining/using/recovering.
4. Craving.
5. Repeated failures in major work/school/home roles.
6. Continued use despite recurrent interpersonal/social problems caused or worsened by use.
7. Important activities curtailed or abandoned.
8. Recurrent use in physically hazardous situations.
9. Continued use despite knowing a persistent physical/psychological problem is probably caused or worsened by it.
10. Tolerance: markedly greater dose needed or markedly weaker effect at the same dose.
11. Characteristic withdrawal or use of the same/related agent to avoid or relieve it.
(pp. 791–800)
## Specifiers & severity
Mild = 2–3 criteria; moderate = 4–5; severe = ≥6. Early remission follows previously full criteria with no criteria other than possible craving for ≥3 but <12 months; sustained remission requires ≥12 months. Record restricted access with **in a controlled environment**, including the remission context. Frequency/dose trends supplement, rather than replace, symptom counting. (pp. 757–759)

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
Assess smoked, vaped, dabbed, edible and pharmaceutical products separately by route, potency and frequency. Inhalation acts more quickly than oral products. Synthetic cannabinoids have unpredictable potency and can produce more severe effects. Tolerance/withdrawal with medically indicated use should not be the primary basis for diagnosing a use disorder. Time spent intoxicated/recovering and abandoning valued activities can be underrecognized by the patient. (pp. 793–795)

## Associated features
Coping with mood, insomnia, anger or pain may coexist with worsening of those problems. Low motivation may resemble persistent depression. Conjunctival redness, odor, chronic cough and changes in eating may support recent/chronic use but do not establish disorder. (p. 795)

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
Source U.S. adult 12-month DSM-5 estimate is about 2.5%; prevalence is highest at 18–29. Earlier onset, especially before 15, conduct problems/disinhibition, family/peer exposure, trauma and availability increase risk. A gradual escalation is common; course varies. (pp. 795–797)

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
Social acceptance and medical/recreational contexts vary. Assess actual impairment rather than legal status or cultural disapproval alone. Women may experience more severe withdrawal and faster transition to disorder in some studies. Prevalence estimates and regulations in this edition describe its publication context. (pp. 797–798)

## Diagnostic markers
Urinary THC metabolite can remain positive for weeks in frequent users; it cannot reliably date intoxication or prove ongoing failure of remission without appropriate interpretation. (p. 798)

## Suicide risk
The text describes associations of cannabis use disorder/chronic use with self-injury and suicidality; it does not establish acute cannabis use as a causal suicide risk. Record the person's actual risk assessment. (p. 798)

## Functional consequences
Attention/executive deficits, impaired driving, school/work problems, loss of goal-directed activity and relationship problems may occur. Separate acute intoxication from persistent functional concerns. (pp. 798–799)

## Differential diagnosis
- **Nonproblematic use:** establish ≥2 criteria and attributable impairment; neither exposure nor a positive test suffices.
- **Intoxication/withdrawal:** time-linked syndromes differ from the longitudinal pattern; document both and use the combination code.
- **Primary mood/anxiety/psychotic disorders:** chronology and persistence outside use help attribution; comorbidity remains possible. (pp. 799–800)

## Comorbidity
Other SUDs, depression, bipolar disorders, anxiety/PTSD, personality disorders and externalizing disorders are common. Cannabis may precipitate/exacerbate psychotic illness; association is not sufficient proof of individual causation. Respiratory disease and cannabinoid hyperemesis are relevant medical comorbidities. (pp. 799–800)

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Record products, route, potency if known, amount/frequency, source, and medical-use context.
- Map dated examples to the 11 items, specify the 12-month window and count, and distinguish expected medical tolerance/withdrawal.
- Document independent psychiatric symptoms, other substances, functional consequences, driving hazards and any assessed suicide risk.
- Record remission dates, residual craving and access restrictions; interpret urine results cautiously.
