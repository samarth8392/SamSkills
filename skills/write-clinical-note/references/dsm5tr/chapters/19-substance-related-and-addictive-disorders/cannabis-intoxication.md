# Cannabis Intoxication

> **Spark note:** Diagnose the time-linked behavioral syndrome plus physical signs, not a positive cannabinoid result. Reality testing determines whether perceptual symptoms can be specified within intoxication or suggest a psychotic disorder. (pp. 800–802)
## Criteria at a glance
**A:** Recent cannabis use. **B:** Problematic behavioral/psychological change during/soon after exposure (e.g., altered judgment, coordination, anxiety or time perception). **C:** ≥2 within two hours: red conjunctivae, greater appetite, dry mouth, tachycardia. **D:** Another medical/mental disorder or intoxicant does not better explain it. (pp. 800–802)

## Specifiers & severity
No separate symptom-count severity scale is specified for this acute syndrome. **With perceptual disturbances** requires preserved reality testing during hallucinations, or illusions, without delirium. Loss of insight calls for a psychotic-disorder assessment; impaired attention/awareness calls for delirium assessment. (pp. 800–802)

## Coding
Source-edition codes (pp. 800–802):

| Comorbid same-class use disorder | Code |
|---|---|
| Mild | F12.120 |
| Moderate/severe | F12.220 |
| None | F12.920 |

With perceptual disturbances, use F12.122 / F12.222 / F12.922 for mild / moderate-severe / no use disorder.

Use the combined code rather than a separate stand-alone use-disorder code; name the specific substance if known.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
Smoking usually produces rapid onset; oral products can be delayed and last longer. Typical effects last about 3–4 hours, occasionally recurring/persisting 12–24 hours. Dose, route, sensitivity and tolerance affect presentation; synthetic cannabinoids may cause more severe psychotic effects at higher doses. (pp. 801–802)

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
Episode prevalence is unknown. Exposure prevalence is only an approximation; intoxication still requires problematic change and the stated signs. (p. 802)

## Suicide risk
Not specifically addressed in this section.

## Functional consequences
Driving, school/work, relationships and sexual decisions may be impaired; occasional psychotic complications occur. (p. 802)

## Differential diagnosis
Alcohol/sedative intoxication more often involves reduced appetite, nystagmus/ataxia or aggression. PCP more often produces marked ataxia/aggression. Low-dose other hallucinogens can resemble cannabis. A predominant, clinically significant anxiety or psychotic syndrome warrants assessment for the corresponding induced disorder. (p. 802)

## Comorbidity
Often occurs with cannabis use disorder; see that file for psychiatric/substance comorbidities. (p. 802)

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Record agent/route/dose, time of use and change in behavior; identify ≥2 physical signs within two hours.
- Record attention, awareness and reality testing separately; exclude other substances and medical causes.
- State use-disorder status from its own criteria; document functional impact and objective/collateral evidence.
