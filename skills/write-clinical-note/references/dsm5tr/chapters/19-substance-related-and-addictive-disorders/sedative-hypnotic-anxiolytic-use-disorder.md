# Sedative, Hypnotic, or Anxiolytic Use Disorder

> **Spark note:** The same 11-criterion SUD set applied to benzodiazepines, Z-drugs, barbiturates, carbamates and similar drugs. The key trap: **long-term patients taking benzodiazepines as prescribed often develop tolerance and withdrawal, and those two alone never make the diagnosis.** Look for drug-seeking, using more than prescribed, falsified symptoms, or multiple prescribers. Unlike opioids, there is **no "on maintenance therapy" specifier.**

## Criteria at a glance
**A.** Problematic use → clinically significant impairment/distress, **≥2 of 11 within 12 months** (pp. 847–848):
1. Larger amounts / longer than intended
2. Persistent desire or failed efforts to cut down/control
3. Lots of time obtaining, using, recovering
4. Craving
5. Recurrent use → failed role obligations (e.g., absences, poor work, school suspensions/expulsions, neglect of children/household)
6. Continued use despite social/interpersonal problems (e.g., arguments with spouse about intoxication, physical fights)
7. Activities given up/reduced
8. Recurrent use when physically hazardous (e.g., driving or operating machinery while impaired)
9. Continued despite known physical/psychological problem caused/worsened by it
10. Tolerance (markedly more needed, or markedly less effect). **Not met if taken under medical supervision.**
11. Withdrawal: characteristic sedative withdrawal (Criteria A+B), or the drug **or a closely related substance such as alcohol** taken to relieve/avoid withdrawal. **Not met if under medical supervision.**

## Specifiers & severity
- Severity: **Mild 2–3 · Moderate 4–5 · Severe ≥6**. Severity rests on criteria count because the symptoms are unidimensional.
- **In early remission:** no criteria for ≥3 to <12 mo (craving allowed).
- **In sustained remission:** no criteria for ≥12 mo (craving allowed).
- **In a controlled environment:** access restricted. A further specifier of remission (supervised substance-free jails, therapeutic communities, locked units).
- No maintenance-therapy specifier for this class.

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
- Class: benzodiazepines; benzodiazepine-like drugs (zolpidem, zaleplon); carbamates (glutethimide, meprobamate); barbiturates (secobarbital); barbiturate-like hypnotics (glutethimide, methaqualone, propofol). Includes most prescription sleep and anti-anxiety drugs.
- **Excluded:** non-benzodiazepine anxiolytics such as **buspirone and gepirone** (no significant misuse).
- Brain depressants like alcohol, with similar induced and use disorders.
- Obtained by prescription and illegally. Not everyone who misuses them or uses for intoxication develops a use disorder.
- Rapid-onset, short-to-intermediate-acting agents are favoured for intoxication, though long-acting ones are used too.
- **Craving** during use or abstinence is typical.
- Polysubstance patterns: sedatives to "come down" from cocaine or amphetamines; high-dose benzodiazepines to "boost" methadone.
- **Prescribed-use rule (p. 850):** tolerance/withdrawal after long-term therapeutic-dose benzodiazepines stopped abruptly does not count. Diagnose only if *other* criteria are met. Check for inappropriate prescribing or use: falsifying symptoms, using more than prescribed, undisclosed multiple doctors.

## Associated features
- Often co-occurs with other SUDs (alcohol, cannabis, opioid, stimulant), with sedatives used to ease the unwanted effects of those drugs.
- Tolerance to sedation develops faster than tolerance to **brain-stem depression**, so dose escalation for euphoria can cause **sudden respiratory depression, hypotension and death**.
- Intense or repeated intoxication can cause severe temporary depression → suicide attempts or suicide.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
**Prevalence**
- 12-mo DSM-IV SHA use disorder, US: **0.3%** in ages 12–17 and adults. Stable despite rising prescribing.
- No consistent US gender difference; other countries find higher rates in females.
- Falls with age: 18–29 = 0.5%; ≥65 = 0.04%.
- Misuse by ethnoracial group: 0.6–2.5% (adolescents), 0.7–10.1% (adults).

**Course (two pathways)**
1. *Common*: teens/20s; occasional social use (parties) → daily use, high tolerance → interpersonal problems, cognitive dysfunction, withdrawal. Especially with other SUDs.
2. *Less common*: originally prescribed for anxiety, insomnia or somatic complaints → tolerance/dose creep → justifies use by the original symptoms while seeking becomes prominent; multiple prescribers. High tolerance, and withdrawal can include **seizures and delirium**.
- Onset usually adolescence/early adulthood; risk declines after about 30.
- **Older adults:** more cognitive side effects, slower metabolism, worse memory/motor toxicity. Those with major NCD are intoxicated at lower doses. Intoxication can **resemble progressive major NCD**. Problems also seen at 40+ from escalating prescribed doses.
- Deliberate "high" seeking mostly in teens/20s.

**Risk & prognostic**
- Temperamental: impulsivity, novelty seeking; personality disorders.
- Environmental: **availability** (own prescriptions and those of family/friends). Misuse follows prescribing trends (barbiturate decline → benzodiazepine rise). People with AUD who keep receiving prescriptions for alcohol-related anxiety/insomnia are at risk.
- Genetic: twin studies show an important genetic role, growing from puberty into adulthood.
- Course modifier: early onset of use → higher likelihood of the disorder.

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
- Prescribing and availability vary by country. In the US, benzodiazepine use is reported more by non-Latinx Whites, but **among users**, benzodiazepine use disorder was higher in African Americans (3.0%) and non-Latinx "others" (2.6%) than non-Latinx Whites (1.3%).
- No apparent gender difference in prevalence.

## Diagnostic markers
- Almost all agents detectable in urine/blood (blood quantifies).
- Urine positive up to **~1 week** after long-acting agents (diazepam, flurazepam).

## Suicide risk
- US data link **hypnotics** with suicide, but it is unclear whether this is confounded by depression and insomnia, which are themselves suicide risk factors.
- Intense or repeated intoxication → severe temporary depression → suicide attempts and suicide (associated features).
- Deliberate overdoses occur (see below).

## Functional consequences
- Disinhibition like alcohol: accidents, interpersonal and work/school problems, aggression and fights, legal problems.
- Exam: mild autonomic decrease (slower pulse, slightly lower RR, slight BP drop, especially postural).
- Injuries/MVAs while intoxicated; trauma sequelae (internal bleeding, subdural hematoma).
- Elderly: even short-term prescribed use increases cognitive problems and **falls**. Link to major NCD unclear.
- Lethal at high doses, especially **combined with opioids or alcohol**. Benzodiazepines alone have a wide safety margin, but combined with opioids/alcohol they are especially dangerous, and accidental overdoses are common in US data. Barbiturate overdose → respiratory arrest. Barbiturates and methaqualone are now less available, so overdoses from them are infrequent.
- IV use → hepatitis, HIV.

## Differential diagnosis
- **SHA intoxication / withdrawal / induced disorders:** syndromes of heavy use; code them with the use disorder carried in the code.
- **Other medical conditions:** slurred speech and incoordination may reflect MS or prior head trauma (subdural hematoma).
- **Alcohol use disorder:** distinguished mainly by history; liver damage or cardiomyopathy point toward alcohol.
- **Clinically appropriate use:** long-term prescribed benzodiazepines with tolerance/withdrawal but no preoccupation with obtaining and no role interference = **no disorder**.

## Comorbidity
- Nonmedical use is associated with AUD, tobacco use disorder and illicit drug use.
- Overlap with antisocial PD (especially when obtained illegally), depressive, bipolar and anxiety disorders, and other SUDs.
- Comorbid SUDs or psychiatric disorders raise the chance of moving from use to disorder and **lower the chance of remission**.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Agent, prescriber(s), prescribed dose versus actual dose, source (own Rx, family/friends, street), PDMP findings (early refills, multiple prescribers).
- Explicitly separate **physiological dependence from prescribed use** from criteria-level behaviours (A1–A9). Note "tolerance/withdrawal not counted: taken as prescribed" where it applies.
- Hazardous use: driving or machinery while sedated, falls, MVAs, blackouts/anterograde amnesia.
- Co-use with opioids, methadone or alcohol, a major overdose risk. Document counselling and naloxone access when opioids are involved.
- Older adults: cognitive screen, fall history, and whether "dementia-like" symptoms track with dosing.
- Remission start date; controlled-environment status.
- MSE: sedation, slurred speech, nystagmus, ataxia, memory gaps, disinhibited or labile affect.
- Rule-outs: AUD (history, liver/cardiac signs), neurological causes of ataxia, underlying anxiety/insomnia disorder that prompted the prescription (document separately).
