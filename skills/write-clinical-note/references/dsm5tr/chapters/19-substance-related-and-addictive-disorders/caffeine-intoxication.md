# Caffeine Intoxication

> **Spark note:** Recent high caffeine intake (typically **well over 250 mg**) plus **≥5 of 12** signs, with distress or impairment. Low-dose symptoms (restlessness, nervousness, insomnia, flushing, diuresis, GI upset) can appear at **~200 mg** in caffeine-naive people. The "big-dose" signs (twitching, rambling speech, tachyarrhythmia, inexhaustibility, agitation) usually need **>1 g/day**. **5–10 g can be lethal.** Caffeine has **no use disorder** diagnosis in the main text, so the code is always **F15.920**. It can mimic panic and mania, so always ask about energy drinks, OTC analgesics and supplements.

## Criteria at a glance (pp. 784–785)
- **A.** Recent caffeine consumption, typically a high dose **well in excess of 250 mg**.
- **B.** **≥5** of the following, during or shortly after use:
  1. Restlessness
  2. Nervousness
  3. Excitement
  4. Insomnia
  5. Flushed face
  6. Diuresis
  7. GI disturbance
  8. Muscle twitching
  9. Rambling flow of thought and speech
  10. Tachycardia or cardiac arrhythmia
  11. Periods of inexhaustibility
  12. Psychomotor agitation
- **C.** Clinically significant distress or impairment.
- **D.** Not due to another medical condition, and not better explained by another mental disorder (e.g., an anxiety disorder) **including intoxication with another substance**.

| Code | Use when |

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls (p. 785)
- **Sources:** coffee, tea, soda, **energy drinks**, OTC analgesics and cold remedies, **weight-loss aids**, chocolate, and increasingly vitamins and food additives. **>85%** of US children and adults consume caffeine.
- Some people show tolerance, withdrawal and problematic use, but **the clinical significance of a caffeine use disorder is not established** (see Conditions for Further Study). Intoxication and withdrawal **are** established.
- **Dose tiers:**
  - **Low dose (~200 mg) in vulnerable people** (children, elderly, caffeine-naive): restlessness, nervousness, excitement, insomnia, flushing, diuresis, GI complaints.
  - **>1 g/day:** muscle twitching, rambling thought and speech, tachycardia or arrhythmia, inexhaustibility, psychomotor agitation.
- **Tolerance** can prevent intoxication despite high intake.

## Associated features (p. 785)
- High doses: mild sensory disturbances (**ringing in the ears, flashes of light**).
- Large doses raise heart rate. Typical dietary doses may **slow** it.
- Whether excess caffeine causes headache is unclear.
- **Exam:** agitation, restlessness, sweating, tachycardia, flushing, increased bowel motility.
- **Caffeine blood levels** help when history is poor but are **not diagnostic alone** because individual response varies.

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
- General-population prevalence is unclear. About **7%** of the US population may have ≥5 symptoms with impairment.
- **Energy drinks**, often mixed with alcohol, are a growing problem in adolescents and young adults. Energy-drink-related ED visits **doubled** in the US from 2007 to 2011.
- **Half-life ~4–6 h**. Symptoms usually remit **within about the first day** with no known lasting effects. **5–10 g** needs urgent medical care and **can be lethal**.
- Reactions **intensify with age** (more sleep interference and hyperarousal).
- **Children and adolescents** are at higher risk: low body weight, no tolerance, little knowledge of caffeine's effects.
- *Environmental:* **infrequent users**, or users with a **recent large increase** in intake. **Oral contraceptives** markedly slow caffeine elimination and raise risk.
- *Genetic:* genetic factors may affect risk.

## Functional consequences (p. 786)
- Dysfunction at work or school, social indiscretions, role failure. **Extremely high doses can be fatal.** May precipitate a **caffeine-induced disorder**.

## Differential diagnosis (pp. 786–787)
- **Independent mental disorders:** symptoms such as **panic attacks** may resemble independent disorders. Consider **manic episodes, panic disorder, GAD**.
- **Other substances:** **amphetamine intoxication**; **sedative, hypnotic or anxiolytic withdrawal**; **tobacco withdrawal**.
- **Sleep disorders** and **medication side effects** (e.g., **akathisia**).
- The **temporal link** to increased caffeine use, or to abstinence, establishes the diagnosis.
- **Caffeine-induced anxiety disorder or sleep disorder, with onset during intoxication:** anxiety or insomnia is **in excess** of usual intoxication, predominates, and warrants independent attention.

## Comorbidity (p. 787)
- Typical dietary doses are not consistently linked to medical problems. **Heavy use (>400 mg)** can cause or worsen anxiety, somatic symptoms and GI distress. **Acute extreme doses → grand mal seizures, respiratory failure, death.**
- Excessive use is associated with depressive, bipolar, eating, psychotic, sleep and substance-related disorders. People **with anxiety disorders tend to avoid caffeine**.

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- **A:** quantify the dose by listing all sources over the past 24 h (cups of coffee, energy drinks and their mg, pre-workout or diet pills, OTC analgesics). Note the timing of the last dose.
- **B:** list ≥5 signs with observations, e.g., "pressured, rambling speech," "fine muscle fasciculations," "HR 118, regular," "facial flushing," "frequent urination," "unable to sleep, reports feeling 'wired,' 'could go all night.'"
- **C:** impairment (left work, unable to focus, ED visit).
- **Rule-outs:** history of panic or mania, thyroid disease or arrhythmia, amphetamine, cocaine or other stimulant use (tox screen), recent benzodiazepine or alcohol cessation, nicotine cessation, new antipsychotic (akathisia).
- Note contributing factors: **oral contraceptive** use, caffeine-naive status, a recent sharp increase, adolescent age.
- Very high ingestion (grams) → document medical referral and vital-sign monitoring.
- Code **F15.920**.
