# Other (or Unknown) Substance Use Disorder

> **Spark note:** Use this class for a known substance outside the named classes or for a truly unidentified agent. It is not a shortcut for polysubstance use: diagnose identifiable class-specific disorders separately. (pp. 885–890)
## Criteria at a glance
A clinically impairing/distressing pattern with ≥2 of the following in the same 12-month period:
1. More substance or longer use than intended.
2. Persistent wish to reduce use or unsuccessful attempts.
3. Much time spent obtaining/using/recovering.
4. Craving.
5. Repeated failures in major work/school/home roles.
6. Continued use despite recurrent interpersonal/social problems caused or worsened by use.
7. Important activities curtailed or abandoned.
8. Recurrent use in physically hazardous situations.
9. Continued use despite knowing a persistent physical/psychological problem is probably caused or worsened by it.
10. Tolerance: markedly greater dose needed or markedly weaker effect at the same dose.
11. Characteristic withdrawal or use of the same/related agent to avoid or relieve it.
(pp. 885–890)
## Specifiers & severity
Mild = 2–3 criteria; moderate = 4–5; severe = ≥6. Early remission follows previously full criteria with no criteria other than possible craving for ≥3 but <12 months; sustained remission requires ≥12 months. Record restricted access with **in a controlled environment**, including the remission context. Frequency/dose trends supplement, rather than replace, symptom counting. (pp. 757–759)

## Diagnostic features & clinical pearls
Examples include nitrous oxide, nitrites, anabolic steroids, some medications, betel nut and kava. Inhaled gas is not necessarily an inhalant-class agent: the latter is limited to hydrocarbons. Synthetic cannabinoids remain cannabis-class; cathinones remain stimulant-class; propofol remains sedative-class. If the agent is known, name it; revise unknown attribution if subsequently identified. (pp. 887–889)

## Associated features
Atypical/repeated intoxication with negative routine screens or a local outbreak of unfamiliar drug effects can be clues. Nitrous oxide misuse can be associated with myeloneuropathy, spinal-cord degeneration and psychosis; these medical effects do not independently establish the use-disorder criteria. (p. 888)

## Prevalence · Development & course · Risk & prognostic factors
Disorder prevalence is poorly known and cannot be inferred from lifetime exposure rates. No single course applies across these diverse agents. Earlier problems, disinhibition, other SUDs, trauma and availability are general risk factors. (pp. 888–889)

## Culture- & sex/gender-related issues
Locally customary substances and occupational access affect exposure; assess impairment rather than equating cultural use with disorder. (pp. 888–889)

## Suicide risk
General SUD suicide associations apply; the source reports no unique suicide risk factors for this residual class. (p. 888)

## Functional consequences
Consequences depend on agent and are reflected in role, relationship, health and hazardous-use criteria; do not assume identical pharmacology across the class. (pp. 885–890)

## Differential diagnosis
- **Exposure below use-disorder threshold:** ≥2 criteria in 12 months are still required.
- **Known class-specific agent:** reclassify appropriately rather than using F19 as a generic multiple-drug label.
- **Intoxication/withdrawal or induced disorder:** distinguish temporal syndromes from the repeated pattern and use a combination code when applicable. (pp. 888–890)

## Comorbidity
Other SUDs, conduct disorder and antisocial personality disorder commonly coexist. (p. 890)

## Documentation cues (for client notes)
- Identify the actual agent or why it remains unknown, with sources, product/street name and toxicology limitations.
- Record the 12-month criterion pattern/count and actual impairment, medical-use context and other identifiable substances.
- State remission dates/access restrictions and update the diagnosis when substance identity becomes clearer.
